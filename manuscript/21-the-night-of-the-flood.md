## Twenty-one — The Night of the Flood

The first person to call me after Malcolm agreed to fund the institute was a man who wanted to sell me a photocopier.

I don't know how he found out. The agreement hadn't been announced. We had spent most of April getting the legal and financial arrangements in order, and on the last Tuesday of the month I'd been informed that the first installment had arrived in the account of the newly established nonprofit.

At three that afternoon the man called.

"How many copies are you anticipating doing in a month?" he asked.

I looked at the piece of paper on my desk. It was a statement of the purposes of the organization. Six people had had a hand in writing it.

"One," I said.

He laughed.

"Well, let me tell you about a few things."

I let him tell me about them. I was so pleased with the world that afternoon that I was prepared to listen to almost anybody.

We had enough money to employ a small group of people for three years. There was a board, of which Malcolm was not a voting member. The funding agreement didn't give him the right to prevent the publication of a result. We had a mechanism for deciding which projects to support, though we had yet to decide who would operate the mechanism. And we had a great deal of work to do before we could describe ourselves as anything resembling a functioning research institution.

It was the most wonderful collection of problems I'd ever had.

Simon and I went back to look at the building where the man had made lamps. It was still available.

"Do you still want it?" he asked.

"Very much."

"All right. Let's get somebody to tell us whether we should want it."

We hired somebody to inspect the building, and Nadia put us in touch with somebody who could help us work out what it would cost to adapt it. She told me she didn't want to be the architect. She wanted to finish the library.

"You're going to have all kinds of opinions about the building," she said. "I'd rather be able to go home and not hear them."

I understood this.

We made an offer on the lease.

* * *

It was on a Thursday in May that the weather turned bad.

I had been at Northline until six, trying to finish a document concerning the interpretation of certain evaluation results. The sky outside my window had gone a peculiar color. I sent Ray a message asking if we were still playing that evening.

*Walter says come if you can,* he wrote. *Don't be stupid.*

I went home instead.

At about eight the rain began coming down so hard that I couldn't see the building across the street. I made dinner and sat down to watch a film. Ten minutes into the film my phone rang.

It was Nadia.

"Where are you?" she asked.

"At home."

"Good."

"Where are you?"

"At the workshop."

The workshop was the place she'd taken me on our first Saturday together, the converted warehouse near the river. She'd been attending a meeting there. Water was coming in through the back of the building, and the people who ran the place were moving equipment upstairs.

"Are you all right?"

"Yeah. I'm not going anywhere until the rain stops."

"Good."

"Can you call Simon? He's got the number of a guy who did some work on the drainage."

I called Simon. He found the number and gave it to Nadia.

Then I called her again.

"Adrian," she said. "Don't come down here."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were thinking about it."

"I was."

"Well, don't."

I didn't.

The rain continued for another hour. The film was still on the television, though by then I had very little idea what the people in it were doing. There were reports of flooded roads and power outages. Walter sent a message saying that the water was up to the bottom of his front step and that he was going to turn off the amplifier.

A little after ten Nadia told me that the people at the workshop were safe and that she was staying with a friend nearby until the roads were passable.

I went to bed.

* * *

The following morning we learned that several families in a low-lying neighborhood had been displaced. The storm had damaged a number of buildings, including the ground floor of the workshop.

Nadia and I went down there on Saturday, when the people who ran the building had established that it was safe to enter. The room with the sewing machines was a terrible sight. There were wet boxes, ruined pieces of furniture, and a brown line on the wall indicating the level the water had reached.

The piano was ruined.

I stood there looking at it.

A man named Curtis, who ran the furniture workshop, came over and handed me a pair of gloves.

"Are you the guy who does the computers?"

"Sometimes."

"Well, today you're the guy who does the chairs."

There were fourteen chairs. I carried them out into the sun.

People came all day. Some of them had never been to the building before. A woman brought sandwiches. A man with a pickup truck took away wet carpet. Two teenagers swept mud out of the kitchen while listening to music on a little speaker.

Nadia was sitting on the stairs with a clipboard, trying to establish which things belonged to which people.

At noon a woman came up to us. Her name was Renee. She lived nearby and had been using the sewing machines to make clothes for her grandchildren. Her own apartment had been damaged. She wanted to know whether there was somewhere she could go with her daughter until they found out when they could get back into it.

Nadia gave her the address of a temporary assistance center that had been set up by a local organization.

"I went there this morning," Renee said.

"What happened?"

"They said we'd been placed."

"Where?"

"That's what I asked them."

She showed us a message on her phone. It thanked her for confirming her temporary accommodation.

"Did you confirm anything?" Nadia asked.

"No."

"Have they offered you a place?"

"They said I'd been matched."

The message contained a reference number and a link to a website. Renee had tried the link, but it took her to a page on which the case was listed as resolved.

I asked her if she'd mind showing me the page.

She did.

There was a familiar little green mark beside the status of the case.

I had a sudden feeling that I was standing in two places at once. One was a building full of wet chairs in Dallas. The other was a conference room in London.

"Can I write down the number?" I asked.

She nodded.

I did.

Nadia looked at me.

"Find out where she can sleep tonight first," she said.

We called the assistance center. After several transfers we reached a woman who was able to reopen the case and arrange a place for Renee and her daughter. Nadia helped them get there later that afternoon.

I went back to the chairs.

* * *

On Monday I called Lena Ortiz.

She had heard of the organization running the assistance center. It was part of a temporary regional partnership that had been using a new system to coordinate referrals across several organizations. There had been a number of complaints about cases being closed prematurely, although it wasn't yet clear whether the complaints concerned the same underlying problem.

"Can you find out anything about the system?" I asked.

"Probably."

"Would it be useful if I took a look at the public documentation?"

"Yes. But don't put anything online about this woman."

"I won't."

I went into work.

Peter was standing in my office when I arrived. He'd been waiting for me. He had a piece of paper in his hand.

"Do you remember the company from Brussels?"

"Yeah."

"They're launching in the United States."

He handed me the paper.

It was an announcement of a regional deployment of a new coordination service. The service was being developed by Vincent Roos's company. There were several partners listed at the bottom, including Northline.

One of the organizations was involved in the temporary assistance program.

"Peter," I said, "can I tell you about something that happened on Saturday?"

I told him what I knew, leaving out the information that would identify Renee.

He listened.

"Well, we shouldn't assume that's the cause," he said.

"No."

"Lots of things could produce a status like that."

"I know."

He looked at the announcement.

"Shall we see if we can find out?"

We went to Miriam's office.

* * *

That night I got home late.

There was a letter from Malcolm on my desk. He'd written it in response to the question I'd asked him about funding work that might establish that there were things worth building even if they weren't useful to us.

He said that he was interested in the question, although he didn't yet understand what the research would consist of. He wanted us to be careful about the distinction between creating something that could give the impression of having a good life and creating something that could actually have one.

He also wanted to know what we thought about the possibility that we might discover reasons not to build something.

I liked that question.

I got a piece of paper and began writing him a reply.

Then my phone lit up. It was a picture Nadia had sent me of Renee and her daughter standing in front of the place where they were staying. I recognized Renee's coat. The little girl had a bag over her shoulder and was smiling at something outside the photograph.

Under the picture Nadia had written:

*They've got somewhere for the week.*

I put the letter down.

There were all kinds of things I was hoping to discover in the next few years. Things that might turn out to be of enormous importance. I would have been quite happy to spend the rest of the evening thinking about them.

But I wanted, for a few minutes, to think about that little girl having somewhere to put her bag.
