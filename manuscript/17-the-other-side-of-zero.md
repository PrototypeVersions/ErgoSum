## Seventeen — The Other Side of Zero

The first piece of original research produced by my institute was an argument to the effect that my institute did not exist.

Simon and I were filling out a form. In the box marked ORGANIZATION I had written The Open Question. Simon had crossed it out.

"Is that an organization?"

"It's going to be."

"Okay. What is it now?"

I looked around the kitchen. There were two laptops, a bowl of walnuts and an electrical cord running across the table. Simon had come over to help me set up a separate account for the project.

"It's this," I said.

"Great. Then write down your name."

I did.

We had agreed on the name the previous week. It wasn't particularly original, and there were probably a number of organizations that had already arrived at it. But it was the first name I'd suggested that didn't cause Simon to take his glasses off.

We were going to call the little project Open Question, and if it ever acquired an institute, the institute could have a name of its own.

"Can we get shirts?" I asked.

"No."

"One shirt?"

"No."

He moved the bowl of walnuts.

"Have you heard from Miriam?"

"Yeah."

"And?"

"She's willing to discuss the conditions."

"That's good."

"She wants me to suspend the independent project until we've had the discussion."

"Why?"

"She thinks there might be a conflict of interest."

"Is there?"

"Not as far as I can tell. None of this is Northline's stuff."

Simon closed his laptop.

"Adrian, don't tell me what you can tell. Get somebody who knows what the contract says to tell you what the contract says."

I hired a lawyer who spent two hours reading my contract and twenty minutes explaining it to me. The independent mathematical project was sufficiently removed from my assigned work that, in her view, I had a good argument for proceeding with it, provided I used no Northline resources or confidential material. She suggested that I seek written confirmation before making any commitments.

I took her advice. Miriam sent me a letter permitting the project to go ahead, with certain reasonable restrictions.

It had cost me a considerable portion of my first month's experimental budget to arrive at the point where I was allowed to conduct an experiment.

"Welcome to business," Simon said.

* * *

We had recruited a mathematician.

Her name was Beatrice Shaw. She lived in Albuquerque and had been a friend of a friend of mine in graduate school. She worked on combinatorics, taught part-time, and had an extraordinary ability to identify the precise point in a mathematical explanation at which the person giving the explanation had ceased to know what he was talking about.

I asked her whether she would be interested in helping us construct some problems.

"How much are you paying?" she asked.

I told her.

"That's fine. How much do I have to talk to the machines?"

"Not at all if you don't want to."

"Excellent."

She sent me a collection of twenty-four problems. Some had short solutions. Others had solutions that took up several pages. In some cases the problem was to determine whether a proposed generalization was true at all. For each one she provided an answer and, separately, an account of how the answer could be checked.

We put these aside. The artificial systems wouldn't have access to the solutions.

There was a small amount of programming to do. In the evenings I wrote code and made tea and occasionally discovered, at midnight, that I'd been running a program that had been waiting for me to press something since ten fifteen.

I had a wonderful time.

The first results were disappointing. One system spent its entire allowance of computing time attempting to prove a false generalization. Another produced a very good solution to a problem we hadn't set it. A third supplied three pages of entirely correct mathematics connected by two sentences of entirely incorrect English.

Beatrice was delighted.

"Oh, this is terrific," she said.

"Really?"

"Yes. Look at the third one. That's what half my students do."

"Well, it's not what I was hoping for."

"What were you hoping for?"

"I don't know. Something that would surprise us."

"That's surprised me. I thought it'd be worse."

We agreed to run the next set of experiments.

* * *

One of Beatrice's problems involved a little game.

You had a row of counters, each of which was either black or white. You could select any two adjacent counters of the same color and replace them with a single counter of the other color. You could do this until there were no adjacent counters of the same color left.

The question was whether the final number of counters depended on the order in which you made the replacements.

It did. You could find examples of that fairly quickly. We knew this, and so did Beatrice. The more interesting question was whether there were any properties of the final arrangement that did not depend on the order of the moves.

The systems came up with a number of possibilities. Most were false.

On Sunday evening one of them produced an observation about the difference between the number of black counters and the number of white counters. Whenever you made a move, that difference went up or down by three.

I called Beatrice.

"That's right," she said. "It's the invariant I had in mind."

"Okay."

"That's not what you're calling about, is it?"

"No."

The system had gone on to consider a game in which the counters could be arranged on a circle rather than in a row. It had produced a small result about the possible lengths and colors of terminal arrangements, then given an example which suggested a much stronger conjecture about how those arrangements could be reached.

I sent it to her.

"Give me a minute," she said.

I made some coffee.

Forty minutes later she called me back.

"Where'd you get this problem?"

"You gave it to me."

"No, the other problem. The circular one."

"It made it up."

She was quiet for a little while.

"Well, don't announce anything," she said.

"I wasn't going to announce anything."

"You're exactly the kind of person who would announce something."

"I know."

"I think the conjecture's false. But I can't find a counterexample."

We spent the rest of the evening looking.

Just after midnight Beatrice found one. It involved a circle of nineteen counters. I had never been so disappointed by a circle of nineteen counters.

Then she said something I hadn't expected.

"Hang on. There's another way to state what it's trying to say."

She sent me a line of notation. The restriction she had added excluded the troublesome arrangement, but it was not an arbitrary restriction. It followed naturally from a particular way of looking at the moves.

"Can you give it that?" she asked.

"Sure."

We did.

By Tuesday afternoon we had an argument that looked promising. By Wednesday morning Beatrice had found a hole in it. On Friday we found a way around the hole. By the following Monday she had written out a proof of a modest result concerning a class of circular arrangements.

We sent it to two mathematicians we knew. We explained exactly how we had arrived at the problem, which parts had been proposed by the system, and which parts had been supplied by Beatrice.

One of them pointed out that a closely related problem had been investigated in the seventies. The other suggested that the particular result we'd obtained might be new.

I read that email in a parking lot.

It was a little result. There were no implications for the Riemann hypothesis. No branch of mathematics would have to be rewritten. There was a good chance somebody had proved it already.

But at the start of the experiment, none of the three of us had been trying to prove that result.

We'd had a different question.

I sat there for a very long time, trying to work out what I thought was so wonderful about this.

Then I called Simon.

"Have we made any money?" he asked.

"No."

"All right. What's happened?"

I told him.

When I'd finished he said, "That's pretty cool, Adrian."

"Yeah."

There was a pause.

"Have we spent all the money?"

"No."

"That's even cooler."

* * *

Beatrice and I were preparing a short public account of the experiment when I received a phone call from a journalist.

Her name was Elise Warren. She had been writing about artificial intelligence for several years and was working on an article about the reliability of claims concerning mathematical discovery. Somebody had forwarded her an early description of Open Question.

"Would you say the system discovered a theorem?" she asked me.

"I'd say we did an experiment in which a system suggested a question, and we arrived at a theorem by working on the question with it."

"Okay. Is it possible to distinguish which person discovered the theorem?"

"Well, if by person you mean Beatrice or me, it was overwhelmingly Beatrice."

She laughed.

"And the system?"

"It suggested the direction. It produced a false conjecture. Then there was a process in which we arrived at a true one."

"Do you think that's significant?"

"Yes."

"Even though the original conjecture was false?"

"Well, a large proportion of the interesting things that have happened to me have happened as a consequence of my being wrong about something."

She asked if she could quote me on that.

"Sure."

"Good. One other question. Is this work connected with the Observatory?"

"No. How do you know about the Observatory?"

She said she'd attended a presentation given by Vincent Roos's company. It had been a demonstration of a new service for coordinating decision-making among institutions.

There had been some discussion of the research on which the service was based. Northline was identified as one of the organizations that had contributed to the evaluation work.

"Okay," I said.

"Did you contribute to it?"

"Not that I know of."

"Right. Well, I ask because your name came up."

"In what context?"

She hesitated.

"Give me your email address. I'll send you the relevant slide."

A few minutes later I received a photograph of a screen in a large auditorium.

There were four names on it, each accompanied by a photograph.

I recognized Elliot Finch. I recognized a woman who had been at the meeting in Brussels.

And I recognized myself.

The heading above the photographs was *Independent Expert Endorsements*.
