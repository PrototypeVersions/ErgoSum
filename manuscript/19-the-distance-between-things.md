## Nineteen — The Distance Between Things

Simon had been to Santa Fe once before, in 1998, and retained a grievance against a man who had charged him twelve dollars to park his car.

"He wasn't even wearing anything," he told me on the airplane.

"What do you mean?"

"Anything to indicate that he was the person you were supposed to give twelve dollars to."

"Did you give it to him?"

"Of course."

"Did you park?"

"Yeah."

"Well, it sounds like you had a reasonably successful encounter."

Simon looked out the window. We were somewhere above New Mexico. The land below us was the color of bread that had been in the oven slightly too long.

"I hope your millionaire is going to pick us up," he said.

"He's not my millionaire."

"Don't tell him that."

Malcolm had arranged to meet us the following morning. We rented a car, checked into a hotel, and went out to have dinner. It was cold, and there was a little snow on the roofs. I found a store selling blankets and went in to look at them. Simon followed me.

"Did you tell Nadia about Claire?"

"Yeah."

"What'd she say?"

"She said to tell her hello."

"That's nice."

"Yeah."

Simon lifted a corner of a blanket and looked at the price.

"Jesus Christ."

He put it down.

"Did Claire say she'd see you?"

"Tomorrow afternoon."

"Good."

He went back outside. I looked at the blankets for a while longer, then bought one for Nadia. It had a deep red stripe running through it and was the sort of object that made me wish I owned things with which it would go.

I joined Simon on the pavement.

"How much was that?"

"Less than an institute."

"Everything's less than an institute."

* * *

Malcolm Reed lived in a house outside town that was very hard to see from the road. When we reached it, a man was standing on a ladder beside the front door. He had a screwdriver in his hand.

"Are you Malcolm?" I asked.

"Yeah."

He came down from the ladder.

"What's happened is I've installed a light that turns itself on during the day and turns itself off at night."

"That's pretty good," Simon said.

"Do you know anything about it?"

"Probably."

They spent ten minutes discussing the light. Then Malcolm took us inside.

The house was full of books and had practically no furniture. In the room where we sat there were two chairs, a couch, a table, and an enormous window through which we could see the mountains.

"Is it always like that?" I asked.

"Like what?"

"The view."

"No. Sometimes it's dark."

I liked him.

He brought us coffee. Then he asked me to tell him what I wanted to do.

I described Open Question. I told him about Beatrice, about the experiment, and about the difficulties of distinguishing an actual intellectual achievement from something that merely resembled one.

He listened without interrupting. When I'd finished, he got up and went to a cabinet in the corner of the room.

He took out a little tape recorder.

"My father," he said.

He put the recorder on the table.

"Did he record things?"

"All the time. He got the idea that he might be able to leave instructions for himself."

Malcolm's father had developed a progressive illness. There had been occasions on which he became very confused about where he was, or why he was there. Sometimes he refused things that, on other occasions, he had asked for.

"He didn't like people telling him what he'd said the day before," Malcolm told us. "I think the idea was that it might be different if he could hear himself saying it."

"Was it?"

"Sometimes."

He picked up the recorder.

"The trouble was that once he started making these recordings, everybody wanted him to make more of them. We had him making recordings about all kinds of things. The doctors, his finances, who was supposed to visit him. It turned into a sort of part-time job."

He smiled.

"He used to record weather reports."

"Why?"

"Because he thought they were interesting. He'd get up in the morning and tell you it was seventy-two degrees and the wind was coming out of the west."

"Did you keep those?"

"Those are the ones I listen to."

We sat there looking at the little recorder.

Malcolm told us that after his father's death he had been approached by a company developing personalized AI companions. It had offered to build a representation of his father using recordings and written material. He had declined.

"Do you think it could have been helpful?" I asked.

"Probably."

"Then why didn't you?"

"I'd spent quite a lot of time saying goodbye."

He put the recorder back in the cabinet.

"I don't object to other people doing it. I can imagine circumstances in which I'd have wanted to do it myself. But I started thinking about all the things you'd have to find out before you could tell anybody that it was a good idea."

"That's a pretty large research program," I said.

"Yeah."

He sat down.

"I was going to give a university some money to work on it. I talked to three universities. They all sent me descriptions of projects they'd already been planning to do."

Simon laughed.

"So," Malcolm said, "what would it cost to run your place for three years?"

I looked at Simon.

Simon looked at me.

Then Simon opened his bag and took out a folder.

I had assumed that the folder contained something about the rental car.

* * *

There was no agreement that morning. There were several figures discussed, all of which seemed sufficiently large to belong to other people's lives. There were questions about who would own the work, how funding decisions would be made, what would happen if the research produced results Malcolm didn't like, and whether he was interested in making an investment or a gift.

"A gift," he said.

Simon looked at him.

"With what conditions?"

"I'd like somebody to tell me what you're doing once in a while."

"That's fine."

"And I'd like to be able to come and ask questions."

"That's fine, too."

"Other than that, I'd like to figure out what the conditions ought to be with you."

We agreed that we'd get professional advice and prepare a proper proposal. Malcolm wanted to see how we would allocate the money, what protections there would be for the independence of the researchers, and what we would do if we ran out of useful things to investigate.

"I don't think that'll be the problem," Simon said.

"No," Malcolm said, looking at me. "I don't suppose it will."

When we left, Simon went back to the light by the front door. He showed Malcolm how to adjust something on it. They stood there for a few minutes, and then the light went off.

"Well, that's encouraging," Malcolm said.

On the way back to town, Simon was very quiet.

"What do you think?" I asked.

"I think we need an accountant."

"Yeah."

"And I think we need to think pretty carefully about whether we want to take that much money from one guy."

"Yeah."

He looked at me.

"Also, I think you might be getting your institute."

I looked out the window.

There was snow on the ground in the places where the sun hadn't reached it. Every so often we passed a house, or a mailbox, or a little road that disappeared over a hill.

I had no idea what to say.

Simon turned on the radio.

"Don't cry in the rental car," he said. "I didn't get the insurance."

* * *

Claire's studio was in a little building with a wooden gate. There were photographs on the walls and a large table covered with paper. She was standing at the table when I came in.

"Adrian!"

She came over and hugged me.

She had cut her hair. She had also acquired a pair of glasses which I had never seen before and which seemed to me to have been designed specifically for her.

"Look at you," she said.

"Look at you."

"Well, it's been a while."

"Yeah."

She showed me around the studio. I recognized several of the places in the photographs. Others I had never seen. There was a photograph of a swimming pool in winter. There was one of a woman standing at a bus stop, looking away from the camera. There was a photograph of two empty chairs, side by side, on a concrete porch.

"That one's beautiful," I said.

She looked at it.

"I took that in Kansas."

"What were you doing in Kansas?"

"Driving through Kansas."

We laughed.

Then she made us some coffee.

Her husband, Miles, taught art at a school outside town. He was coming by later. I asked about the dog that ate rocks.

"He's moved on to sticks."

"That's good."

"Relatively."

We sat down. I told her about London, about Northline, about Nadia. I told her a little about Malcolm, although I didn't tell her any of the figures.

"You sound happy," she said.

"I am."

"Good."

She drank some coffee.

"Are you still doing the thing where you decide what you're going to do for the rest of your life about once a month?"

"Probably."

"Okay."

"But I think I'm doing a little less of the thing where I wait until I've decided before I do anything."

"That's good."

I looked at the photographs.

"Can I ask you something?"

"Sure."

"Do you think you would've been happy if we'd moved to Europe?"

She thought about it.

"Probably. For a while."

"Yeah."

"Would you?"

"I think so."

She nodded.

"Well, there you go."

I laughed.

"That's it?"

"What else do you want me to say?"

"I don't know."

She leaned back.

"Adrian, there are a lot of lives we could've had together. Some of them would've been lovely. I don't think we have to pretend they wouldn't have been."

I looked at her.

"But I like the one I've got," she said.

"Yeah."

"And it sounds like you're beginning to like yours."

I nodded.

For a while we didn't talk. I looked again at the photograph of the two chairs.

I thought of how often I had imagined seeing Claire again. In those imaginings she was always somebody I had known very well, and I was always somebody who was going to show her how much I had changed.

I had not anticipated how interesting it would be to discover all the ways in which she had changed without my being there.

Miles arrived at five. He was a nice man with a beard and a small piece of blue paint on his wrist. The three of us went out to dinner. He told a story about a student of his who had spent an entire semester painting different kinds of fruit blue.

"What'd you give him?" I asked.

"An A. They were really good blue apples."

On the way back to the hotel I called Nadia.

"How'd it go?" she asked.

"Really well."

"With both of them?"

"Yeah."

"Good."

I told her about the photographs.

"Did you buy one?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"I don't know."

"Well, go back and buy one."

So the following morning, before we left for the airport, Simon and I went back to the studio and I bought the photograph of the two chairs.

It is on the wall of my office as I write this.

For a long time I thought of it as a photograph of a place where nobody was sitting.

I think of it differently now.
