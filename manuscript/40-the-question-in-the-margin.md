## Forty — The Question in the Margin

The question was on the second page, between a discussion of some experiments I'd conducted in graduate school and a discussion of whether a system could make a prediction about its own behavior.

It was a rather ordinary question.

**DIRECTOR:** What would you be doing if you hadn't come here today?

There was no answer attributed to me. The next entry concerned something else.

I took the laptop into the kitchen.

Nadia was making toast.

"Can I read something to you?"

"Can I eat this first?"

"Sure."

She buttered the toast and ate a piece of it. Then she looked at me.

"Okay."

I read her the question.

"Well, what were you doing?" she asked.

"On that Tuesday?"

"Yeah."

"I don't remember."

"Can you find out?"

I could, probably. I had a calendar on the computer and a great many records of things I'd done at particular times.

But I was interested in a different question.

The imaginary version of me had been asked what he'd be doing if he hadn't attended a meeting. At the time the question was supposedly asked, that was the life I was actually living.

I knew something about the man in the transcript. The man in the transcript didn't know anything about me that I didn't already know.

And he had no answer to give.

"Adrian," Nadia said.

"Yeah?"

"You're going to be late."

She pointed at the clock.

I shut the laptop.

* * *

I called Miriam from the institute.

I asked whether she remembered the way the original recruitment experiments had been conducted.

"Vaguely," she said.

"Do you think I could have a look at the instructions that were given to the system?"

"We might be able to arrange that."

I explained what I wanted to know.

She laughed.

"You've been reading the transcript again."

"Yeah."

"Don't you have anything better to read?"

"Occasionally."

She said that the review of the appointments program had been completed, and that a public report would be available shortly. She would find out whether there were parts of the material concerning my own case that could be released to me.

I asked her how she was doing.

"Well," she said, "we're a rather smaller organization than we were when you were here."

"Oh."

"We've lost some work. We've also acquired some work. It's been an interesting year."

"Are you still doing the research you want to do?"

"Some of it."

She was quiet for a moment.

"Would you have lunch with me next week?"

"Of course."

We arranged to meet on Thursday.

* * *

The invitation to Paris had come at a bad time.

Two of our applications for research funding had been unsuccessful. We had been waiting to hear about a third. There was still a reasonable amount of time before we would be obliged to make decisions about reducing the size of the institute, but Fatima wanted us to begin discussing the possibility now.

She put the figures on the big table.

I looked at them.

There were six salaries in the document, including Celia's fellowship. There were figures for the rent, the computing costs and various other things. At the end was an estimate of the amount of money we'd need to raise in order to continue doing all the things we were currently doing.

I asked whether we could reduce my salary.

"Yes," Fatima said.

"That'd help."

"A little."

"How much?"

She told me.

It would help considerably less than I'd imagined.

We spent the morning discussing the options. Nobody wanted to stop doing anything. Several people had suggestions about other things we might be able to do.

At about eleven, June got up and went to her desk. She came back with her laptop.

She had received an inquiry from a group of researchers in Canada. They'd been using the provenance software she'd written and wanted to know whether she could help them adapt it to a particular kind of investigation.

"What would they pay us?" Simon asked.

June told him.

"That's good," he said.

"Yeah. But I'd have to spend about two months doing it."

"Okay."

She looked at me.

"Would you be all right with that?"

"Of course."

"Even if I didn't have time to work on P2?"

I hesitated.

She looked down at the laptop.

"Well," she said, "that's the part I wanted to talk about."

I had been assuming that the people at the institute were generally going to want to continue working on the same things I'd become interested in.

It had been an agreeable assumption. There were occasions on which it had even been correct.

"What would you like to do?" I asked her.

"I'd like to finish the thing I started."

"Okay."

She looked up.

"And I'd like somebody else to be able to use it."

"Then let's see if we can make that happen."

There was a discussion about how much time she would need, and whether the work for the Canadian group would be compatible with keeping the original software openly available. We agreed to find out more about the proposed arrangement.

When the meeting was over, Simon came over to me.

"That wasn't so bad," he said.

"No."

He sat down.

"Did you think we were going to spend the rest of our lives doing the things you were interested in last November?"

"No."

He looked at me.

"Well, I was hoping we would do quite a few of them."

"I know."

He pointed at the laptop June had left on the table.

"She's built something people want."

"Yeah."

"That's pretty cool."

It was pretty cool.

By the end of the day I was looking forward to finding out more about the people in Canada.

* * *

I drove to El Paso on Friday.

Nadia had been working there for almost three weeks. I'd arranged to spend a few days with her, and then we were going to visit her sister.

When I arrived, she was in a room with a group of people discussing the building she'd shown me photographs of in Dallas.

She introduced me to a man named Luis, who was going to teach dancing if they could find him somewhere to do it. Luis wanted a floor that people could dance on without being particularly good at dancing. A woman sitting beside him wanted to know what would happen to the floor when somebody came in with a chair.

They began discussing the floor.

I sat down at the end of the table.

There was a woman named Marta who worked at a grocery store. She wanted to be able to bring her mother somewhere in the afternoons. Her mother didn't speak much English and didn't particularly like the places Marta had been taking her.

"What does she like doing?" Nadia asked.

Marta said that her mother liked making things and talking to people who were making things.

"Okay," Nadia said.

She made a note.

There were about a dozen people in the room. They had been discussing the building for a number of months. Some of the things they'd originally wanted had become less important to them. Some of the things they now regarded as essential hadn't been mentioned at the first meeting.

At one point somebody asked Nadia whether there would be a charge for using one of the rooms.

She said they hadn't decided.

"Well, I'd like to talk about that," the woman said.

So they talked about it.

I had been expecting that at some point during the afternoon Nadia would have to explain to the people that there were certain things that simply couldn't be done.

She did.

They seemed to be perfectly capable of discussing the things that couldn't be done without coming to the conclusion that nothing could be done.

At six, the meeting ended.

I helped carry some chairs into a cupboard.

Then Nadia and I went out to dinner.

* * *

We had been married for several months, and there were still occasions on which I would look at her in a restaurant and have the extraordinary realization that I was married to her.

This was one of them.

She had been telling me about the problem with the floor. I asked whether she thought there was a solution to it.

"Probably."

"Good."

She looked at me.

"How are things in Dallas?"

I told her.

I told her about the applications for funding and the discussion we'd had at the institute. I told her I was beginning to think that Open Question might have to become a rather different organization from the one I'd imagined.

"Would that be bad?" she asked.

"I don't know."

"Okay."

She drank some water.

"Are you worried about money?"

"Somewhat."

"All right. Let's look at it."

"What do you mean?"

"Us."

She meant our finances. What we were spending, what we needed, and what would happen if I took a reduction in salary.

We went through some of it at dinner. The following morning we went through the rest of it at the hotel.

It was not, as it turned out, necessary for me to continue earning precisely the amount of money I'd been earning in order for us to have precisely the life we'd been having.

There were some things we'd have to be careful about. There were others we would have to discuss. There were even a few things we decided we could do without.

I was enormously relieved.

Nadia was slightly irritated.

"Why didn't we talk about this before?" she asked.

"I thought I'd have a better idea of the situation in a few weeks."

"Well, you probably will."

"Yeah."

"And then we'll talk about it again."

I looked at her.

"Okay."

She shut the laptop.

"Now, do you want to go see my sister?"

"Yeah."

"Good."

She picked up her bag.

* * *

On Sunday Anthony took me out into the garage.

He had bought an old motorcycle and was in the process of getting it to work. It was the motorcycle he'd been telling me about a year and a half earlier, although it had acquired a considerable number of additional parts.

He was twenty now.

He'd been working at a repair shop and taking classes a few evenings a week. He was thinking about doing some additional training.

"What kind?" I asked.

He told me.

I was pleased to discover that I didn't know anything about it.

He showed me the motorcycle.

"Do you want to hear it?"

"Of course."

He wheeled it outside and started it.

It made an extraordinary noise.

Teresa appeared at the back door.

"Anthony!"

He turned it off.

"Did you hear that?" he asked me.

"Yeah."

"Pretty good, right?"

"Wonderful."

Teresa went back inside.

Anthony looked at the motorcycle.

"She's actually pretty happy about it," he said.

"Yeah?"

"Yeah. She's just trying to watch something on TV."

We went inside.

* * *

On Monday morning, Miriam sent me a document.

It contained the instructions that had been given to the system in the original experiment, and an account of some of the sources from which the material about me had been obtained. There was nothing particularly surprising about the sources. My papers, some conference abstracts, a few descriptions of projects I'd worked on.

There were also a number of descriptions of the kind of person Northline was hoping to recruit.

I hadn't anticipated how much of the material would be about Northline.

The system had been asked to produce a possible exchange with a candidate under a particular set of circumstances. It had been asked to identify possible difficulties with the proposed appointment. There were several examples of the kinds of exchanges the researchers were interested in.

I read the examples.

In one, a candidate wanted to know what would happen if she accepted an offer and subsequently decided to leave.

In another, a candidate wanted to know whether the other people working at the organization would be able to refuse a request from the director.

I looked at the instructions again.

There was a fair amount of material about the sorts of questions the people at Northline thought it would be interesting for a candidate to ask.

I had regarded the transcript, from the beginning, as an attempt to produce an account of me.

Now it seemed to me that there had been some fairly interesting information about somebody else in it.

I called Miriam.

"Did you write these examples?"

"Some of them."

"Why?"

"Well, we were interested in those questions."

"Which questions?"

"The ones about the institution."

I looked out of the hotel window.

"Had it occurred to you that the transcript would tell you things about the institution as well as things about the candidate?"

"Not in those terms."

There was a silence.

"Adrian?"

"Yeah?"

"Have you got a question you want to ask me?"

I did.

I wanted to know what she'd been hoping would happen to Northline when she first started it.

It turned out to be the first time I'd asked her.
