## Thirty-six — The Last Page

The last scheduled session with Peregrine was on a Friday.

Beatrice had come to Dallas. She was staying at a hotel near the institute, although she was spending so little time at the hotel that I began to wonder whether we ought to pay for a room there and get some other people to use it in shifts.

The negotiations with Continuance had concluded. The company wasn't willing to provide us with access to the research software on a sufficiently independent basis. It was willing to pay for the collaboration if we undertook the wider program. We had declined.

There was one concession Anika had secured. With the permissions of the relevant contributors, we could retain the parts of the research state that concerned our joint work. Léonie was helping June put the material into a format that could be read without the original software.

June had been working on it for three days.

"Do you think we can make it work somewhere else?" I asked her.

"Make what work?"

"Peregrine."

She turned around.

"We can make a thing that knows a lot of what Peregrine knew. We can give it some of the same tools. We can give it the history of the work."

"Okay."

"But I can't tell you it's the same thing."

"Right."

She looked back at the screen.

"And I don't think I could tell you it was the same thing if I had all the software."

I sat down beside her.

She showed me the collection of material she'd managed to preserve. There were the proofs and the attempts at proofs. There were questions Peregrine had chosen to investigate. There were some notes concerning the differences between the various mathematical systems with which it had interacted. There was a record of the little game Walter's granddaughter had changed.

There was even the old proof Beatrice had sent it, the one it had retained after removing a number of other things from its collection.

"Did it choose these?" I asked.

"Some of them. Some were saved by the framework. Some we're saving because the people who worked with it think they're important."

I nodded.

I remembered the boxes in Elliot's room at Cambridge. The photograph he'd put aside. The recipe he'd remembered because the recipe had been so bad.

There was a little folder on my computer containing recordings of some of the music we'd played at Walter's house. I had thrown away quite a few of the recordings when I'd gone through them. I wondered whether Walter would have thrown away the same ones.

"Do you need anything?" I asked June.

"A bigger monitor."

"Okay."

"Not today."

She went back to work.

* * *

At noon Beatrice asked if I'd like to go out.

We got sandwiches and drove to the lake. It was hot enough that nobody wanted to sit in the sun. We found a bench under a tree.

Beatrice took a little piece of paper out of her bag.

"Look at this."

There was a mathematical expression on it. Underneath, she'd written three questions.

"I think the answer to that one is yes," she said, pointing at the second question.

"Have you tried it?"

"Yeah."

"Does it work?"

"Not all the time."

I laughed.

She folded the paper and put it back in the bag.

"I want to ask it about that one this afternoon."

"Okay."

We ate for a while.

"Adrian?"

"Yeah?"

"Are you going to make a thing about the session being the last session?"

"What do you mean?"

"Are you going to ask it a lot of questions about whether it's sad?"

"No."

"Good."

She took another bite of her sandwich.

"I'm sad," she said.

I looked at her.

"Yeah."

"I had a really good time working on that problem. And I've been having a really good time working on the next one."

"Yeah."

She looked out at the water.

"It's annoying, actually. I'd just about got over being annoyed with the people in Switzerland."

I laughed.

"Well, you can go on being annoyed with them."

"I know. I'm very good at that."

We finished our sandwiches.

On the way back she told me about a mathematical question she'd been thinking about for a while. It had nothing to do with the reconstruction problem. By the time we got to the institute, she was sufficiently excited about it that she forgot her bag in the car.

I brought it in for her.

* * *

At two we began the session.

Anika and Léonie were there, along with Tomas and the people at our end. Fatima had arranged for everyone who wanted to participate to have access to the room.

We explained the arrangements to Peregrine. We described the materials we would be able to keep and the conditions under which we might be able to conduct further research.

Then Beatrice gave it the mathematical question.

For most of the next hour nothing occurred that would have distinguished the session from any of the other sessions we'd conducted. Beatrice argued with a proposed solution. June checked an example. Tomas pointed out that one of the assumptions was unnecessary. Somebody went to get coffee.

We got quite a long way with the problem.

Toward the end of the session Léonie told us how much of the allocation remained.

Beatrice asked Peregrine to prepare an account of the things that would be useful to somebody continuing the work.

It did.

There were two pages of notes. There was a list of references. There were some suggestions concerning the difficulty that had occupied most of our afternoon.

There was a question at the bottom.

*If the research is resumed under a different arrangement, will the new researchers have access to the unsuccessful attempts?*

June said they would.

Beatrice wrote that we thought the unsuccessful attempts were among the most useful things in the record.

The system supplied a little additional information about two of them.

Then it made a suggestion.

It thought that a researcher who had been unable to solve the first problem might be given the opportunity to investigate the second one, even if we did not regard that as the most efficient way of obtaining a solution.

Beatrice read it.

"Well, that's interesting," she said.

I wrote the suggestion down.

Léonie told us we had another minute.

I looked at the screen.

I had been thinking, for several weeks, about things I might want to say in these circumstances. It had occurred to me that I would like to thank the system. It had also occurred to me that I would like to tell it something about the significance of the work it had done.

I didn't know what effect either of those things would have.

Beatrice typed:

*Thank you for the mathematics.*

A response appeared.

*Thank you, Beatrice.*

Then Léonie ended the session.

We sat there.

Somebody's telephone rang. June got up to answer it.

Beatrice shut her notebook and went outside.

I went after her.

We stood in the little paved area behind the building. There was a truck parked across the street. A man was standing beside the truck eating an apple.

"Well," Beatrice said.

"Yeah."

She took the little piece of paper out of her bag.

"Do you know what I think the problem is with that second question?"

"No."

She showed it to me.

For the next twenty minutes we talked about mathematics.

* * *

That evening I went over to Walter's house.

His granddaughter was there. Her name was Sophie. She'd begun learning the piano, and she was extremely interested in the question of how to play a particular bit of music without moving her thumb.

After we'd played for a while she asked me whether I'd brought the game.

"No."

"Why not?"

"I forgot."

"Oh."

"Did you like it?"

"Yeah. But I thought of another thing we could do with it."

She drew a picture.

It was quite a good idea.

I got a piece of paper and made a note of it.

Then Walter asked whether we wanted to play Westbound, and we played Westbound.

It sounded different from the way it had sounded when we first recorded it. Judy had begun playing something at the end of the third bar that she'd never played before. Ray had a new guitar. Walter was using a different amplifier. And there was a little bit in the middle during which Sophie played something that was not especially well suited to the piece but that made us all laugh.

When we finished, she wanted to do it again.

So we did it again.

* * *

On Monday morning Fatima sent me a copy of the revised proposal for the study Vincent had invited us to participate in.

Celia and Asha had been working on it. They wanted participants to know when a recommendation was being made in pursuit of a particular outcome. They wanted the participants to be able to decline the recommendation without losing access to the underlying opportunities. They proposed measuring not only satisfaction with the choices made but also access to alternatives, the discovery of new interests, and the ability to reconsider earlier decisions. There would be no experiment in which participants were deliberately deprived of an educational or professional opportunity for the sake of obtaining a more interesting result.

They wanted the study to be governed independently of the company.

The proposal had been sent to Vincent.

His reply came while I was reading it.

He was willing to discuss the design. Some of the proposed measures, he thought, would be quite useful. He had also included a question for me.

If the new system proved to be less effective at achieving outcomes the participants had originally wanted, would I be prepared to regard that as a disadvantage of the system?

I wrote back that of course I would.

Then I added something.

Would he be prepared to regard the discovery that his system had made it more difficult for people to pursue things they had subsequently come to want as a disadvantage of his system?

I waited for the answer.

It didn't come that afternoon.

At five I shut the computer down and went home.

Nadia was in the yard pulling a few small weeds out of the ground. She had planted the seeds I'd bought several months earlier. Practically nothing had come up where she'd planted them. In a little patch of ground about two feet away there was something growing that neither of us had planted.

"What's that?" I asked.

"I don't know."

"Do you want me to pull it out?"

"No."

She got up.

"I want to see what it is."

We went inside to make dinner.

* * *

I received Vincent's answer the next morning.

It was a long message. He had some concerns about the way the second kind of outcome would be established. He thought it was important that the comparison be made fairly. He wanted us to meet with the other people who would be responsible for the work before agreeing to anything.

At the end he said that he was willing to accept the possibility that a finding of the kind I described would count against his system.

I printed out the message.

I took it over to Celia.

"Well," she said, "that's a start."

"Yeah."

"Are we going to be able to publish it if our system does worse?"

"I hope so."

"No. I'm asking you."

I looked at her.

"Yes."

She nodded.

"Good."

Then she went back to her work.

I went into my office and opened the document containing the research questions we'd inherited from the work with Peregrine.

I added another question.

It was a question about the conditions under which a person could discover that a system they liked very much had not been particularly good for them.

I thought it might be useful to begin investigating that question before we had built a system anybody liked very much.
