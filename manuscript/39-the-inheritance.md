## Thirty-nine — The Inheritance

It was Peter's son who first gave me a reasonably good explanation of how the refrigerator at the institute worked.

He had been at the institute for about half an hour. Peter had brought him in because Beth had an appointment and there was a period of the afternoon for which neither of them had made arrangements.

James explained that the refrigerator made things cold because it had been shut for a long time.

"Ah," I said.

He opened the door.

"Now it's going to get warm."

He shut the door.

"Okay."

He appeared to be satisfied with the explanation. I asked him whether he could think of any other things that happened when you shut the door.

"Things get dark," he said.

"Yeah."

He considered this.

"I don't think it's dark in there."

"Why not?"

"There's a light."

"Yes. But the light goes off."

"How do you know?"

I opened the refrigerator and showed him the little switch. We spent several minutes investigating the switch. Then he asked whether we had anything to eat.

We had a considerable number of things that neither Peter nor I particularly wanted him to eat.

I gave him an apple.

* * *

June and Beatrice had been examining the first results from the experiment involving the new research systems.

They had each been given the same mathematical tools, the same amount of time and access to the same collection of public research materials. One of them had also been given a selection of material from the work with Peregrine.

The selection was the thing June had spent much of the summer putting together.

It was immediately apparent that the system that had received the additional material could do some things that the other one couldn't do nearly as well. It knew, for example, what Beatrice was referring to when she asked a question about a particular unsuccessful proof.

But the other system was quite good at a number of things that the first system was doing badly.

There was one problem on which it performed considerably better.

"Why?" I asked.

Beatrice showed me the solution.

The system with access to the old research history had spent much of its time investigating the problem using an approach that had proved fruitful on several occasions during the previous year. The other system had tried something simpler.

"I didn't think of that," Beatrice said.

"Is it right?"

"Yes."

We were pleased.

June wanted to establish whether the difference was attributable to the additional material or to something that had occurred in the course of this particular run. We arranged a number of repetitions of the experiment. We also wanted to find out what would happen if the systems were able to choose which parts of the research histories they consulted.

This was going to take a while.

In the meantime, Beatrice asked if she could talk to P2.

"About mathematics?"

"Among other things."

We went over the arrangements with her.

Then she sat down at June's desk and began writing.

* * *

I went to lunch with Simon.

He'd been looking at the figures for the coming year, and he wanted to tell me about some things that weren't going particularly well.

We had enough money to continue our existing work for a while. We didn't have a particularly convincing account of how we were going to continue the work after the end of Malcolm's original commitment. There were some promising opportunities to obtain additional funding, but the people who were interested in funding the mathematics weren't particularly interested in funding the work on welfare. The people interested in funding the work on welfare weren't particularly interested in funding the study of public institutions.

"Why do you think that is?" I asked.

"Because you've got about four institutes."

"Well, there's a relationship between the things we're doing."

"Yeah."

"I think it's a fairly important relationship."

"I know."

He drank some coffee.

"Have you ever tried explaining it to somebody who has to pay for it?"

I laughed.

He took out a piece of paper.

There were three different estimates of what it would cost to operate the institute over the following eighteen months. The difference between them involved some fairly substantial decisions about the kinds of things we would be able to do.

"What do you think we should do?" I asked.

"I think you should get the people who work there to help you figure out which one of these they want to work at."

"Okay."

"And I think you should stop telling people that everything's fine."

I looked at him.

"Is that what I've been doing?"

"Occasionally."

I nodded.

There were a number of people I'd been telling that everything was fine. I had been telling them this because, at the time I was telling them, I thought everything was probably fine.

I hadn't been telling them very much about the word *probably*.

* * *

When I got back to the institute, Beatrice was in my office.

"How'd it go?" I asked.

"Fine."

She handed me a piece of paper.

She had asked P2 about a particular discussion with Martin. The discussion was recorded in some of the research materials Eleanor had permitted us to use. She wanted to see how the system would describe its relationship to the discussion.

I read the response.

*I have access to a record of the exchange. I do not have a basis for claiming that I was one of the parties to it.*

"That's good," I said.

"Yeah."

She'd asked a different question about the work we'd done in Dallas. The answer was rather similar.

Then she'd asked it whether there were things in the research history it would like to continue investigating.

It had identified several things.

One of them was a question Peregrine hadn't been particularly interested in.

"What do you think that means?" I asked.

"Well, it means I have another interesting question to investigate with it."

I laughed.

She sat down.

"Do you want it to be Peregrine?" she asked.

I thought about it.

"Sometimes."

"Yeah."

She looked at the paper.

"I think I wanted Martin to have been there when we finished the proof. I didn't particularly want Peregrine to be Martin."

"No."

"And now I'd quite like Peregrine to be there for some of the things we're going to do."

"Yeah."

She folded the paper.

"But I don't think I want to spend the next few months trying to get this thing to tell me it's Peregrine."

I looked at her.

"Okay."

She got up.

"Anyway, it's quite good at mathematics."

* * *

We had decided to make some of the material from the project publicly available. There were things we couldn't include, either because we didn't have permission or because they concerned the people with whom the system had worked in ways that we didn't think were necessary for the investigation.

The public collection would include the mathematical results, the relevant unsuccessful attempts, the formal proofs and a description of how the research had been conducted.

It would also include a number of problems that hadn't been solved.

June put the material on the website.

Two days later we received a message from a woman in Argentina.

She had been working on one of the problems with a colleague. There was a mistake in one of the examples. She thought that a slightly different version of the example might enable us to establish something interesting.

Beatrice wrote to her.

They began corresponding.

Then a man in Poland wrote to us about something in another part of the collection. Then there was a graduate student in Singapore who wanted to know whether she could contribute a formalization.

I went into the main room.

"What's happening?" I asked June.

She looked at her screen.

"People are reading it."

"Yeah."

"That's what we put it there for."

"I know."

I went back to my office.

I opened the page containing the history of the reconstruction problem. There was a record of Martin's work. There were some things Beatrice had done twenty years earlier. There was the contribution from Peregrine, and there were the subsequent changes made by the researchers who examined the proof.

There was now a little link to the message from Argentina.

I clicked on it.

The message was about a problem that hadn't been formulated when Martin was alive.

I read it.

Then I called Beatrice.

* * *

That evening I went to see the other Adrian.

Simon was there. They were sitting in the little shed at the end of the yard. On the workbench was a radio that I recognized from my first visit.

The radio was working.

Simon turned a knob and we listened to a man describing something that was happening at a basketball game.

"That's pretty good," I said.

"Yeah," the other Adrian said. "Listen to this."

He turned another knob.

The man went away, and there was music.

It wasn't very good music. It sounded as though it was being played in a small room on the other side of a very large room.

"Well," Simon said, "we've got a few things to do."

I asked the other Adrian whether he'd owned the radio when it was new.

"No. My dad had one like it."

"Oh."

He turned the knob again.

"Does it sound like the one your dad had?"

"I don't know."

He laughed.

"I don't remember what that one sounded like."

We listened to the radio for a while.

Then Simon got up.

"Do you want to go get something to eat?" he asked me.

"Sure."

We walked out to the street.

The other Adrian had asked him to come back on Saturday.

"Are you going to?" I asked.

"Yeah."

"Good."

We walked down the street.

"Simon?"

"Yeah?"

"Have you been enjoying doing that?"

"Very much."

I looked back at the house.

For a long time I'd been telling Simon about the things I wanted to do. It was nice, for a change, to have been responsible for something he'd wanted to do without having been particularly aware that I was responsible for it.

* * *

The following morning, I received an invitation to speak at a conference in Paris.

The organizers had been reading *Learning Within a Life*. They were arranging a discussion involving researchers, companies, educational institutions and a number of people who had been involved in the development of international standards for the use of AI.

Vincent was one of the invited speakers.

So was Miriam.

The letter included a question that the organizers hoped I would address.

Were there any conditions under which a system that had developed a sufficiently good understanding of an individual's preferences should be permitted to make decisions on the individual's behalf without consulting the individual?

It was almost exactly the question I'd been asked, in the interview transcript, before I'd ever gone to Northline.

I opened the old transcript.

I found the part I remembered.

Then I read the rest of it.

And for the first time I noticed that, near the beginning, there was a question to which the person in the transcript had never given an answer.
