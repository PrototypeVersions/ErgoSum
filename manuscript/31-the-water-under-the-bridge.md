## Thirty-one — The Water Under the Bridge

Nadia arrived in Edinburgh on a Wednesday morning carrying a suitcase, a cardboard tube and a bag containing a quantity of food she had been unable to finish on the airplane.

I had bought her flowers. At the airport it occurred to me that the flowers represented an additional thing one of us would have to carry through Edinburgh.

She gave me the bag of food.

"Trade you," she said.

We took the tram into town.

It was the first time I'd been able to show somebody around a city in which I was still in the process of getting lost. I enjoyed this enormously. I knew a good place to get coffee, and a little bookstore, and a way of getting from the bookstore to the university that involved going down a rather beautiful street. I showed her all three.

She showed me an exceptionally beautiful building I'd been walking past for a month.

"Did you know that was there?" she asked.

"Yeah."

"What is it?"

"I have no idea."

We went to find out.

* * *

The cardboard tube turned out to contain drawings for a project in Texas.

Nadia had been asked to consider joining a group of architects who were working on a series of small public buildings in towns along the border. It was a rather unusual arrangement. The towns would have a good deal of say in what got built. Some of the work would be done in El Paso, and some elsewhere. She had been trying to decide whether she wanted to be involved.

She unrolled the drawings on the table in the flat.

"How long would it be?" I asked.

"Three years. Maybe more."

"Would you have to move?"

"No. I'd be away a lot, though."

"Okay."

She looked at me.

"I've been thinking about doing it."

I looked at the drawings.

There was a building in one of them that she had been working on for several months. I recognized it. She had shown me an earlier version of it in Dallas, on an evening when I'd been quite interested in the possibility that an artificial system had developed something resembling an enduring preference.

I'd forgotten about the building.

"It's beautiful," I said.

"Thanks."

"Would you like to do it?"

"Yeah."

"Then I think you should."

She looked down at the table.

"I wasn't asking for permission."

"I know."

"Okay."

She began rolling the drawings up.

"Do you want to talk about how we'd do it?" I asked.

She stopped.

"Yeah. I do, actually."

We spent some time doing that.

It was not an especially romantic conversation. At one point I got out the calendar on my computer. At another point Nadia drew a map on the back of an envelope. There were going to be some periods in which we might not see very much of each other.

There were also going to be some places we could go together.

By the end of the conversation I was beginning to look forward to spending a month in a town I'd never heard of.

* * *

We went out to the island on Saturday.

Ian was delighted to meet Nadia. He was also delighted that she'd brought a jacket that was suitable for being on a boat. I had been unable to explain to him why I regarded this as such an extraordinary personal achievement on her part.

It was a lovely day. The water was quite different from the water I'd encountered on my first visit.

There were a number of things we could see on the other side of the channel that I hadn't known were there.

Ian took us out to look at the instruments. Nadia asked him how they were secured, how much they weighed and what happened to them in bad weather. I had spent a substantial portion of my previous visit discussing the information they produced without once asking any of those questions.

After lunch, we walked across the island with Morag. She showed us a little building that had been used as a school. There had been twelve children in the school when she was a child. There were currently no children of school age living permanently on that part of the island.

"What'll happen to the building?" Nadia asked.

"I don't know," Morag said. "Somebody wants to turn it into a holiday cottage."

Nadia went to look through a window.

Morag and I stood on the path.

"She's got a good eye," Morag said.

"Yeah."

"Does she play anything?"

"Not really."

"Well, we'll do something about that."

We played music again that evening.

There was a man named Alasdair who had been listening to the others play for some time. He had a beautiful singing voice. At about ten somebody asked him to sing something, and he sang a song that I hadn't heard before.

I didn't understand all the words. Nonetheless, by the end of it I had begun to feel a considerable affection for a woman in the song who had been dead for perhaps two hundred years, assuming she'd ever existed.

Then he sang something funny.

Then Morag got out the fiddle.

Nadia sat beside me. She had a little wooden instrument that somebody had given her, and occasionally she struck it with a stick. She seemed to be having a very good time.

When we went outside there were no lights on in most of the buildings. We walked a little way down toward the water.

"Can you believe this?" I asked her.

"Yeah."

"Really?"

"Well, I'm here."

I laughed.

We could hear the water moving against the rocks.

For a while she held on to my arm. Then she let go of it and took a few steps down toward the water.

"Adrian," she said.

"Yeah?"

"I think I'm going to take the job."

"Good."

She looked back at me.

"Come here."

I went over to her.

* * *

On Monday I gave a lecture in Edinburgh.

The subject was the relationship between a person's capacity to form new intentions and the capacity of an institution to anticipate the person's behavior. There were about sixty people in the room. I had worked on the lecture for several weeks, and I was quite pleased with the beginning of it.

Halfway through, a student raised his hand.

"Do you think we have an interest in being unpredictable?" he asked.

"Sometimes."

"Would you have that interest even if nobody could do anything to you on the basis of a prediction?"

I thought about the question.

"I think it would depend on what you mean by being unpredictable."

"Suppose somebody could accurately predict everything you were going to decide to do. But the predictions were never communicated to you or acted on by anybody. Would that bother you?"

There were a number of answers to the question that I'd given at various times in my life. I didn't find any of them particularly satisfactory at that moment.

"Not necessarily," I said.

"Then what is it you're trying to preserve?"

I looked at the board.

I had put up a diagram involving a collection of possible choices and a prediction about which one of them a person would make.

I erased part of it.

Then I drew a second diagram.

"Suppose there are two people who have made the same decisions throughout their lives," I said. "In the case of the first person, there were other things they could have done. They knew about them, and they might have done them under slightly different circumstances. In the case of the second person, the other things were unavailable."

"Okay."

"Would you be able to establish from the record of the decisions which person was which?"

"No."

"Well, I think there are things we're interested in preserving that aren't necessarily evident in a record of what somebody has decided."

He looked at the diagram.

"Isn't that just the problem of freedom?"

"To some extent."

A woman in the front row raised her hand.

"Does the second person know the other things are unavailable?"

"That's a good question."

There was a discussion.

It wasn't until the lecture was over that I realized I'd been asked two questions that I hadn't been able to answer, and that neither of the people who asked them seemed particularly disappointed about it.

I went out to lunch with the student who'd asked the first one.

His name was Robert. He was twenty-two. He'd been thinking about leaving philosophy to do something else, and he wanted to know whether I thought this would be a mistake.

"I don't know," I said.

"Oh."

"What do you want to do?"

He told me.

We talked for an hour.

At the end of the hour he said that he still didn't know whether he wanted to leave philosophy.

"Well," I said, "I'm not sure I want you to leave it."

He smiled.

"Thanks."

"But I'm fairly sure I don't want you to stay in it because I told you not to leave."

* * *

Nadia and I had two days left before I was due in Switzerland.

We got a train north and stayed in a little hotel where our room overlooked a river. There was a bridge that we walked across several times for no particularly good reason.

We bought sandwiches and ate them on a bench.

I told her about the questions the students had asked. I told her that I had begun to think there might be a connection between the old argument about complete representations of a person and the work we'd been doing on incomplete representations of mathematical objects.

"Have you worked out what the connection is?" she asked.

"Not yet."

"Okay."

She finished her sandwich.

"Do you want to go down there?"

She pointed to a path that ran beside the river.

We went down there.

At the end of the path there was a little gate. Beyond the gate was a sign explaining that the path was closed.

Nadia looked at me.

"Go on," she said.

"What?"

"Say something philosophical about the gate."

I laughed.

"No."

"Good."

We walked back to the bridge.

That evening I asked her whether she'd ever considered getting married again.

She was sitting on the bed taking off her boots.

"Yeah," she said.

"Do you think you'd want to?"

"Possibly."

She got one of the boots off.

"Are you asking me something?"

"I think I am."

She put the boot on the floor.

"Well, I think you ought to ask it properly, then."

I went over and sat beside her.

I was about to begin telling her about some things I'd been thinking about for quite a long time. They were reasonably interesting things, and I was fairly confident that at least one of them would have been relevant.

Instead I asked her.

She put her arms around me and laughed.

"Yes," she said.

I couldn't think of anything to say.

"Yes," she said again.

We sat there for quite a while. Then she took off the other boot.

"Now," she said, "let's go get dinner."

So we went and got dinner.
