## Thirty-seven — The People Who Came

The morning of our wedding, I went out to buy a screwdriver.

I already owned a screwdriver. I owned several screwdrivers. Unfortunately, at some point during the previous evening, somebody had moved the box in which I kept them, and there was a small problem with the handle of the door to the room in which Nadia was going to get dressed.

Simon had pointed out the problem.

"What's wrong with it?" I asked.

"It comes off."

"Does it come off every time?"

"Adrian, it's a door handle."

I went to buy the screwdriver.

The man at the hardware store asked whether I was doing anything interesting that weekend.

"I'm getting married."

"Oh."

He looked at the screwdriver.

"Well, you're off to a good start."

When I got home, the handle had been repaired. Simon had found the box.

I put the new screwdriver on the table.

"Congratulations," he said.

"Thanks."

He handed me a cup of coffee.

We went outside and sat on the little steps at the back of the house. It was early, and the day hadn't yet acquired the heat it was going to acquire.

"How are you feeling?" he asked.

"Good."

"Really?"

"Yeah."

He nodded.

"Me too."

We sat there drinking coffee. From somewhere down the street came a noise that we'd both become quite familiar with.

"Morning, Raymond," Simon said.

* * *

We were getting married at the workshop.

Nadia had suggested it several months earlier. The ground floor had been repaired after the flood. There were new sewing machines, some new furniture, and a piano that had been donated by a family in Richardson. Curtis had spent a considerable amount of time making tables for the reception, and several people from the neighborhood had offered to help with the arrangements.

Renee was going to be there. She had made a dress for her daughter, who was now sufficiently tall that I didn't recognize her when she came in wearing it.

The wedding itself was to take place outside, in an area beside the building where Nadia had helped put in some plants. There was a large tree, a number of borrowed chairs, and a little path that led to the door of the workshop.

I liked the place because of all the other things that had happened there. I liked the idea that on Monday there would be somebody sitting where we got married, trying to work out how to repair a chair.

When I arrived, Anthony was on a ladder.

"What are you doing?" I asked him.

"Putting up lights."

"Do you need anything?"

"No."

He pointed toward the building.

"They're looking for you."

"Who is?"

"I don't know. A lot of people."

I went inside.

Morag had come from Scotland. Ian hadn't been able to get away, but he had sent a package containing a small wooden model of the boat. Morag had carried it across the Atlantic in her luggage.

"Don't put it in water," she told me.

"Why not?"

"It'll go to the bottom."

She handed me the boat.

Elliot had come from Cambridge, and he had brought his clarinet.

"You're not going to make me play it, are you?" he asked.

"No."

"Good."

"Morag might."

He looked over at her.

"Oh, dear."

Beatrice was standing at one of the tables with June. She had a glass of something in one hand and was drawing a diagram with the other.

"Beatrice," I said.

"Yeah?"

"Are you doing mathematics?"

"No."

June looked at me.

"Yes."

I went to find Fatima, who informed me that I had fifteen minutes to put on my jacket.

"Do I have to wear the jacket the whole time?"

"No."

"Good."

"Adrian?"

"Yeah?"

"Go and put it on."

* * *

I had written something to read during the ceremony.

I had tried writing it on several different occasions. The first version was about four pages long and included a passage concerning the distinction between a life that was good and a life that one was glad to have had. I read it to Simon.

He said, "Are you marrying Nadia or applying for something?"

I wrote another version.

It wasn't until the night before the wedding that I arrived at the version I wanted to read.

There were a number of things I wanted to tell Nadia that I thought other people might find interesting. There were also things I wanted to tell her that I didn't particularly want other people to find interesting. I decided to keep the second sort of thing in the piece and to take out most of the first sort.

I had it on a piece of paper in my pocket.

When Nadia came out of the building, I forgot nearly everything that was on the paper.

She had a dress on that her sister had helped her choose. Her hair was up, though a little bit of it had come down near one of her ears. She was walking beside her mother. At one point her mother said something to her, and Nadia laughed.

I can remember the exact sound of the laugh.

There were about sixty people sitting in the chairs. I had seen most of them earlier that afternoon. I knew roughly where some of them were sitting.

For the next few minutes, they could all have been sitting somewhere else.

* * *

Nadia spoke first.

She told a story about the day I'd come to the workshop with her and had wanted to know whether it was possible for a person to spend time in the building without being expected to do anything. She said she'd thought this was a fairly odd question at the time.

People laughed.

She said that one of the things she had discovered about me since then was that I occasionally needed somebody to tell me that I wasn't expected to do anything.

People laughed again.

Then she looked at me.

She said she'd had a very good life before she met me. She'd done a number of things she was pleased to have done, and she'd had a number of experiences she didn't wish had been different. She had friends she loved, and a family she loved, and work she loved.

She hadn't been looking for somebody to give her a different life.

She had met me.

And now there were things about the life she had that she couldn't imagine wanting to be without.

I stood there looking at her.

When she'd finished, I took the piece of paper out of my pocket.

I told her about the first evening she came to my apartment. About the pasta and the guitar and the suitcase on the floor. I had been looking forward to getting on an airplane the following morning. She had helped me pack, and then she'd sat beside me on the couch.

It had occurred to me, while sitting there, that for the first time in a very long time I was looking forward to coming home from a place I hadn't gone to yet.

I told her that I'd been thinking about that evening a great deal.

Then I told her that I loved her.

I managed to get through the rest of it.

When it was over, we kissed.

Sophie clapped before anybody else did.

* * *

At dinner Malcolm told a story about Simon that I hadn't heard.

During their first meeting in New Mexico, when I'd gone to use the bathroom, Simon had asked him what would happen to me if, after a couple of years, Malcolm decided he didn't like the work I was doing.

Malcolm had told him that he'd have to decide whether to continue funding it.

"And Simon said, 'Yeah, but I'm asking what happens to Adrian.'"

Simon looked down at his plate.

"I was asking about the agreement," he said.

"I know."

Malcolm looked at me.

"Well, anyway, I'm glad he asked."

"So am I," I said.

There was another speech from Teresa. She told everybody about the shed that her husband had been building for several years. It was finished. She had photographs. There was considerable applause.

Then there was music.

I had intended to play one piece of music during the reception. It was something I'd been working on in the room at the back of the house. It didn't have any words, although I'd made several unsuccessful attempts to give it some.

Walter had agreed to play bass on it, and Judy had agreed to play the keyboard. We had rehearsed it.

Then Morag asked if she could join us.

Then Elliot took his clarinet out of its case.

We didn't rehearse that version.

I began playing the piece. Walter came in, and Judy played something very simple. Morag played a little bit of the melody I'd been playing, then went off in another direction.

Elliot waited.

He was holding the clarinet in a manner that indicated to me that he was considering putting it back in its case.

I nodded at him.

He played three notes.

They were good notes.

He played some more.

We got to a part of the piece where I'd always played the same thing, and I found that there was something else I wanted to play.

I played it.

Somebody came onto the little area in front of us and began dancing. It was Nadia's mother. Then Nadia came over. Then Anthony and one of his cousins. There was a child running between them who didn't appear to be dancing but was doing something considerably more energetic.

By the time we got to the end of the piece, I didn't know how to end the piece.

So we kept playing.

* * *

Much later, when most of the people had gone home, Nadia and I sat at one of Curtis's tables.

There were some flowers on it and a number of glasses. Across the room Simon was trying to fit a large piece of cake into a container of insufficient size.

"Did you have a good day?" I asked.

"Yeah."

"Me too."

She took my hand.

"Do you know what I liked best?"

"What?"

"Watching you play."

I looked at her.

"You looked so happy," she said.

"Yeah."

She put her head on my shoulder.

Outside, somebody was taking the lights down.

I could hear a little bit of music still playing on the speakers, and somebody laughing in the kitchen.

I wanted to remember everything.

Then Nadia got up.

"Come on," she said. "Let's go home."

I got up, too.
