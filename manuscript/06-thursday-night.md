## Six — Thursday Night

Walter's place turned out to be a house in Richardson with a blue door, three cars in the driveway, and a ceramic frog that said WELCOME in letters that had almost completely worn off.

I drove past it twice.

The first time I drove past it because I wasn't sure it was the house. The second time I was sure it was the house but hadn't worked out what I was going to say when somebody opened the door. I went around the block and had the good sense to recognize that if I went around it a fourth time I would probably just go home.

Walter opened the door. He was a big, cheerful man in a T-shirt advertising a bowling alley in Oklahoma.

"You a friend of Ray's?"

"Yeah."

"Well, don't stand there."

He stepped aside.

"You can put your stuff anywhere that's not on fire."

There were six people in the living room. Ray was there, which I had not counted on and was very glad of. He introduced me to everybody. Walter played bass. A woman named Judy played keyboards. There was a retired firefighter, two brothers who owned a roofing business, and a slender man with an extraordinarily expensive-looking guitar who was explaining something about the history of the vacuum tube.

There was also a little girl on the sofa watching a cartoon without sound.

"That's my granddaughter," Walter said. "She's nine. She tells us what to play."

"What do you like?" I asked her.

She regarded me with some suspicion.

"Songs I know."

It was going to be a difficult evening.

Judy asked me what kind of music I played.

"A lot of things that don't have names," I said.

"Oh, good. We know all those."

Ray helped me plug in.

They played a song I knew from the radio but had never learned on the guitar. Walter told me the chords and I played them very quietly. There was a remarkable difference between being bad at the guitar in my living room and being bad at the guitar in Walter's living room. In my living room, every mistake was the end of something. In Walter's living room the music went on, and a few seconds later there was an opportunity to do something else.

This was not a discovery about music. It was a discovery about the existence of other people.

We played for about an hour. I contributed relatively little. Then Judy got up to answer the door, and in the interval Ray asked me to play those chords I'd been playing in the shop.

I played them.

Walter came in on the bass, very tentatively at first. The drummer found something to do with the rim of his snare. I got to the fifth chord and played the version Ray had shown me.

"Go around again," Walter said.

I did.

By the fourth or fifth time through I had almost ceased to recognize the thing I had been playing in my apartment. The bass was doing something rather beautiful underneath it. Judy had returned and was playing these little bright notes at intervals, the way light occasionally appears between trees when you're driving.

I stopped playing.

"Keep going," Ray said.

"No, I want to hear you guys."

Walter looked over at me.

"Well, then, give us something to play with."

I started again.

I don't know how long we played. Long enough for the little girl to get up from the sofa and go and look for something in the kitchen, and for me to forget that I was nervous, and then to remember it again and find that it didn't matter very much.

When we finished, Walter asked what it was called.

"Nothing."

"Well, we've already got a tune called that."

"What's the other one like?"

"About the same."

Judy brought me a glass of water.

"Come back next week," she said.

I told her I would.

* * *

At ten thirty, when I got back to Dallas, there was a message from Nadia.

It said:

*Simon gave me your number. I'm assuming you're responsible for the disappearance of a menu from a reputable establishment in Oak Cliff.*

I wrote back:

*I have the menu. I have been consulting it in my capacity as a philosopher.*

*Well, I need it. They had a very good sandwich on the back.*

We arranged to meet on Saturday. She wanted to show me a building near the Trinity that she had worked on several years earlier.

I set the phone down and went into the kitchen. I was hungry, although I had eaten a considerable number of pretzels at Walter's house. I made a sandwich and stood at the window.

The city looked especially nice that night. I had lived in it for long enough to have seen a number of things I loved disappear. Restaurants, trees, a perfectly good bookstore, a house that had been painted a magnificent and unpopular shade of green. I had also lived in it long enough to have seen things appear that I couldn't imagine having wanted before they appeared.

I ate my sandwich.

Then I went and got the guitar and put it on the stand. I didn't play it. I was afraid I would play those five chords and be disappointed by the absence of Walter.

* * *

The next morning Helen was waiting outside my office.

"Have you got a minute?"

"Sure."

She followed me in.

"I got the report."

She handed me a single sheet of paper. It contained a list of dates, identification numbers, and two columns of letters.

"What am I looking at?"

"Candidates whose records were closed in the past twelve months without a documented communication of the decision."

"How many?"

"Sixty-three. Some will be ordinary administrative errors. Some people won't have responded to messages."

"How many of these are associated with the Observatory?"

"That's what I asked for. They said they couldn't establish that from the report."

I looked down the list.

"Well, can we?"

"Maybe."

She pointed to a line about halfway down the page. I recognized the date. It was the Wednesday before I had received the call at the guitar shop.

"That's you," she said.

There was an R in the first column. In the second column there was an A.

"Refused and accepted?"

"Refused and active. The system reopened the record after the correction."

"Okay."

"Do you see anything else?"

I looked at the dates again. There were several identification numbers that appeared more than once. Two of them had an R in both columns.

"What does that mean?"

"Still closed."

"Can we get in touch with them?"

"Possibly. But I'm not sure that'll be my job much longer."

I looked up.

"Why?"

"Because I was offered a different one this morning."

"Here?"

"Yes. They're reorganizing appointments. They're going to make me a liaison for the European program."

"Is that something you want to do?"

She took the piece of paper back from me.

"I'd like to have been asked that."

"Have you accepted?"

"No. I got the email about forty minutes ago. It was from Human Resources."

"What did it say?"

"It said congratulations."

I sat down.

Helen laughed. It was a strange little laugh, almost entirely without amusement.

"To be fair," she said, "I have always wanted to live in Denmark."

"Is that where it is?"

"Apparently."

"Well, have you told anybody that?"

"A number of people over the years. I think I may have mentioned it on a questionnaire when I came to work here."

"Do you still want to?"

She thought about it.

"I have a daughter here," she said. "She's sixteen. She's got a horse."

"A horse?"

"Well, half a horse. It's a rather complicated financial arrangement. She does the front half of the work and we pay for the back half."

We both laughed.

She looked down at the paper.

"Peter's coming over at eleven. I'd like you to show him this."

"Okay."

"And could you not mention Denmark just yet?"

"Of course."

She walked toward the door, then turned around.

"Adrian, do you think they actually know what they're doing?"

I considered this.

"I think probably some of them do."

"That's what I'm afraid of."

* * *

At eleven Peter came over. We spent the rest of the morning constructing a list of things we would need to know in order to establish whether anything improper had happened to the candidates.

I was rather proud of us. We didn't start with an accusation. We started with a set of questions and worked out which answers would support which conclusions. Peter suggested a number of alternative explanations I hadn't considered, and I suggested a few he didn't like. It felt like research.

At lunch I went down to the cafeteria and bought an apple. I sat by the window and sent Ray a message thanking him for the evening.

He wrote back:

*You did good. Turn up next time.*

I didn't know whether he meant the amplifier or my person. I decided to do both.

When I got upstairs there was an email inviting me to attend a research meeting in London in December. There would be eleven of us, including three people from other institutions. The topic was the evaluation of simulated human behavior. The email asked me to prepare a brief presentation of my current work.

I read it with a pleasure that I was unable to make any more complicated than it was.

Then I looked at the attachment.

It was a provisional program.

I was scheduled for the second afternoon. Immediately above my name was the name of a man whose work I had read for years and with whom I had once had a brief, rather humiliating exchange about a paper I had written. Professor Elliot Finch, University of Cambridge.

There was an asterisk next to his name.

At the bottom of the page it said:

*Remote participation. Professor Finch has indicated a preference not to travel.*

I didn't know why I noticed that. In another life I might have been irritated that a distinguished philosopher wouldn't get on a train to go to a conference in London. This time I found myself wondering whether he had been invited.

I went to Peter's office.

"Do you know a guy named Elliot Finch?"

"Sure."

"Is he involved with this?"

"With what?"

"The London meeting."

"Oh, yeah. He's been involved since the beginning."

"How?"

Peter looked at the door.

"Has Miriam not told you about Finch?"

"No."

"He's the reason we're doing the individual simulations in the first place."

I sat down.

Peter said, "I thought that's why she hired you."

"Why would that be why she hired me?"

He opened his browser and found a paper. I recognized the title. I had published it some years ago in a journal with a readership approximately equal to the seating capacity of Walter's living room.

"You wrote this, didn't you?"

"Yeah."

"Finch talks about it all the time."

I looked at him.

"Does he like it?"

"No," Peter said. "Not at all."
