## Twenty-eight — The Second Question

The first person ever to criticize a philosophical argument of mine in Norwegian was a woman named Astrid Solheim.

She had written to me in English, but she sent me a recording of a lecture she'd given in Bergen in which my name appeared at fairly regular intervals. I watched the lecture without understanding a word of it. Every time she said my name, a number of people in the room looked at one another. Toward the end, somebody laughed.

I wrote to her and asked whether she'd been saying nice things about me.

*Some of them,* she wrote back.

Astrid worked on questions about the nature of welfare. She'd read the description of the research program we were developing at Open Question, and she was concerned that we were trying to make a distinction between the things a system wanted and the things it was designed to do without establishing that there was a distinction to be made.

She was going to be in Boston in November. We invited her to spend a few days with us on her way home.

On her first morning in Dallas she arrived at the institute wearing a pair of boots that would have enabled her to survive a considerably more interesting winter than the one we were having.

"I was told it would be cold," she said.

"It will be. Occasionally."

She took her coat off.

"Where is this table I've heard so much about?"

I showed her.

She put a notebook on it and asked me what I thought it would mean for an artificial system to have a good life.

I went to the whiteboard.

Astrid looked at June.

"Does he do this every time?"

"Pretty much," June said.

* * *

We spent the morning discussing a number of possibilities.

There was the idea that a life went well to the extent that the individual experienced pleasure rather than suffering. There was the idea that it went well when the individual was able to do the things they wanted to do. There was the idea that there were certain valuable activities, relationships and forms of understanding that could contribute to a good life even if the individual didn't particularly want them.

Astrid knew a great deal more about the history of these ideas than I did. She was also much better than I was at remembering which things people had meant by particular words.

At one point I tried to describe a system whose ordinary activities were beneficial to the other systems around it.

"What if it doesn't like its ordinary activities?" she asked.

"Then there may be something wrong."

"With what?"

"Well, with the circumstances in which it finds itself."

"Or with what it likes?"

"Possibly."

She looked at the board.

"Suppose you've made a system that loves doing something that's useful to you. You have a theory according to which it is doing well because it loves what it does. You also have the ability to change the things it loves."

"Right."

"Then you've got a very convenient theory."

I laughed.

"Yeah."

"That doesn't make it a bad theory. But it does make me want to know what you're going to do with it."

We sat down.

There had been a man in the philosophy department where I'd studied who occasionally complained that people were too interested in providing examples of a theory and not sufficiently interested in providing the circumstances under which they would give it up. I had thought him rather tiresome at the time.

I was beginning to understand his appeal.

"What would count as evidence against your approach?" Astrid asked.

I got a piece of paper.

For the next half hour we worked on that question.

* * *

We had been doing an experiment with Peregrine in which it was given a number of opportunities to investigate mathematical questions. The opportunities varied in the amount of work required, in their relationship to its previous investigations, and in the likelihood that they would produce something that a mathematician would find interesting.

One of the things we were interested in was whether it consistently selected certain kinds of investigations even when the immediately preceding instructions were varied.

There were a number of difficulties in interpreting the results. Some of the apparent patterns disappeared as soon as we changed the wording of the choices. Other patterns persisted, but we could identify reasonably straightforward explanations for them in the training and design of the system.

Astrid asked whether we had given it opportunities that weren't opportunities to do mathematics.

We hadn't given it many.

"Why not?"

"Well, it's a mathematical research system."

"Yes."

She waited.

I laughed.

"Okay."

We designed a small extension to the experiment. We arranged it with the Heller team, specified the computing limits, and set aside some of the available time for work that did not have to contribute to the mathematical project. There were restrictions on what the system could do and on what resources it could consult. But there was no requirement that it produce anything we could use.

Beatrice came into the room while we were discussing this.

"What's happening?"

"We're thinking about giving Peregrine a bit of time in which it doesn't have to do mathematics."

"Good. Can I have some of that?"

"Of course."

"Excellent."

She went back to her desk.

* * *

A few days later, Peter showed me the results of one of the new trials.

Peregrine had spent most of its allocation investigating the relationship between certain mathematical problems and games played with physical objects. This wasn't altogether unexpected. It had been working on a number of related questions, and we had given it access to some descriptions of the games.

At the end of the trial, it had used a little of the remaining time to construct a game of its own.

Peter sent me the rules.

There were two players. Each had a small collection of shapes and a piece of graph paper. They took turns putting shapes on the paper. There were certain restrictions on where the shapes could be placed.

Unlike most of the games I knew of that involved taking turns, this one did not require the players to defeat one another. The object was to construct something together. Each player had access to a different set of pieces, and neither could complete the construction alone.

There were a few different versions of the game.

I printed one of them out.

On the following Thursday I took it over to Walter's house. His granddaughter, who was now ten, was sitting at the kitchen table drawing a picture of something with a great many legs.

"Want to try a game?" I asked her.

"What game?"

"I don't know what it's called."

She looked at the paper.

"Did you make it up?"

"No."

"Who did?"

"That's a long story."

We cut the shapes out of a piece of cardboard. Walter found some graph paper in a drawer. We played the game.

It wasn't a particularly good game. After about five minutes she identified a way of completing the construction that made a number of the rules unnecessary.

"Well," I said, "that's interesting."

"Can we change it?"

"Sure."

She drew a little square in the middle of the paper.

"You can't put anything there."

"Why not?"

"Because it's the castle."

"Okay."

She made a few other changes. Then we played it again. Walter came over and joined us, and we gave him an additional set of shapes. At some point we stopped making the construction we were supposed to be making and began making a picture of a dog.

The dog was not bad.

When I got home I took a photograph of the picture and sent it to June.

*What is that?* she wrote.

*The result of an experiment.*

*Of course it is.*

I sent a description of the changes to Beatrice. Then I asked the Swiss team whether it would be all right to send the description to Peregrine as part of our next scheduled research session.

A few days later we did.

The system identified the change to the rules and produced a short mathematical analysis of its consequences. Then it suggested several other variations.

There was nothing particularly mysterious about any of this.

But I was sitting in my office looking at the picture of the dog when it occurred to me that the game had brought about something that none of the people involved in making the system had been trying to bring about.

I had spent half an hour at a kitchen table in Richardson with two other people, having a very good time.

I thought of some of the possible purposes of the technology.

Then I thought of all the people trying to identify the purposes of the technology.

There was a rather large difference between finding out what something was for and finding out what could happen because it existed.

* * *

The following morning I received a letter from a university in Scotland.

It was a formal invitation to spend part of the spring as a visiting fellow in connection with Asha's project. There would be some teaching, if I wanted it, and an opportunity to work with a group of philosophers and scientists who were interested in the issues we'd been investigating.

I sat there looking at the letter.

I had imagined getting a letter like it for a long time. In those imaginings I had always been somewhere I didn't particularly want to be, and the letter had offered me an opportunity to go somewhere I did.

Now I was sitting at a table that belonged to an organization I had helped create. I had a house I liked. I had people I wanted to see on Thursday nights. The woman I loved was trying to find a supplier of reasonably priced library furniture.

It was an entirely new experience to receive a letter offering me something wonderful and find that I had something wonderful with which to compare it.

I called Nadia.

"Do you want to go to Scotland?" I asked.

"When?"

"In the spring."

"Is somebody going to show you a book?"

"Probably."

She laughed.

"How long?"

"That's the thing. We can work it out."

"Okay. Let's talk tonight."

I put the letter on the table.

Astrid was sitting at the other end of it, looking at something Peter had sent her.

"Good news?" she asked.

"Yeah."

"What are you going to do?"

"I don't know."

She smiled.

"Good."

* * *

On the last afternoon of Astrid's visit, I asked whether she would be willing to work with us on a paper.

We had been talking about developing a framework for studying the conditions under which a system's choices might be understood as more than an immediate response to a set of instructions. She was particularly interested in the possibility of distinguishing a capacity to reconsider a goal from a capacity to produce a description of having reconsidered one.

There was plenty of work to do.

"Yes," she said. "But I want to make a suggestion."

"Sure."

"I think there are two questions you're trying to answer."

"Okay."

"One concerns the kinds of things we could find out about a system that would make it reasonable to regard its life as going well or badly."

"Right."

"And there's another question about the kinds of relationships and activities we ought to make possible, even before we've answered the first question."

I thought about it.

"Yeah."

"I think you ought to keep them separate."

She closed her notebook.

"Because I think there's a danger that you'll be so interested in the first question that you won't do anything about the second. And there's also a danger that you'll be so interested in doing something about the second that you'll decide you've answered the first."

I looked at the whiteboard.

"That's very good."

"I know."

She put her coat on.

"Also, I'd like to come back when it's colder. I bought these boots."

I promised to let her know when it was colder.

Then I went to the airport with her.

On the way back I stopped at a grocery store. I bought some vegetables, some coffee, and a little package of seeds that I thought we might plant in the yard.

When I got home, Nadia was sitting on the floor of the music room with three different samples of paint.

"What do you think?" she asked.

I sat down beside her.

We spent twenty minutes looking at the colors.

I liked all three of them.

This turned out not to be a particularly helpful thing to like.
