## Forty-six — The First Person

On the first day of my first course at the university, a student came into the classroom carrying a large cardboard box.

"Is this philosophy?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Okay. Can I put this somewhere?"

"Sure."

I helped her put it beside the wall.

"What's in it?"

"Fish."

I looked at the box.

"Not actual fish," she said. "They're ceramic."

"Oh."

She sat down.

Her name was Hannah. She was taking a course in ceramics and had a number of pieces she'd been hoping to take home that afternoon. She asked whether she could leave the box where it was for the duration of the class.

I told her she could.

Then I went to the front of the room.

There were twenty-one students.

For a number of years I'd been imagining the experience of standing in a room in front of a group of students and being able to tell them about some of the things I'd been thinking about. The students in these imaginings had all been sitting in approximately the same manner, and the room had always been quite quiet.

The actual students were sitting in a number of different manners. There was a man trying to get something out of a bag. There were two women discussing something that had happened in another class. Somebody had a bottle of water that kept making a noise.

I looked at the clock.

Then I introduced myself.

* * *

I had called the course *What We Know About Ourselves*.

There was going to be some Descartes, some work on the nature of knowledge, and some discussion of the kinds of things a person could find out about themselves by finding out what other people knew about them.

I had been looking forward to the discussion of Descartes. There was a little passage I had liked since I was nineteen. It concerned his discovery that the proposition that he existed was true whenever he thought it.

I put the proposition on the board.

Then I told them about a system that could produce a written account of somebody thinking.

"Would you say that the system's production of the account establishes that there's somebody thinking?" I asked.

A young man named Jacob raised his hand.

"Somebody wrote the program."

"Sure."

"So somebody was thinking."

"Okay."

He smiled.

"Is that not the answer?"

"It's an answer."

"What was the question?"

I laughed.

I went back through the question. We ended up distinguishing a number of different claims. There was a claim about the person represented in the account. There was a claim about the thing producing the account. There was a claim about the people responsible for building the thing that produced it.

The claims weren't all established by the same evidence.

Hannah raised her hand.

"What if the person in the account is you?"

"Okay."

"And it's a really good account of something you're thinking."

"Right."

"But you're reading it and you're thinking, 'I wasn't thinking that until I read it.'"

I looked at her.

"Then what?"

"That's a good question."

She looked at the board.

"Because now it's true."

"Possibly."

There was a discussion.

One of the students thought the account could be right about the person for the wrong reason. Another wanted to know whether it was ever possible for something to be an account of what a person was thinking at the same time as being one of the causes of their thinking it.

I took a piece of chalk and began writing.

At some point the young man who'd asked about the program looked at the clock.

"Are we supposed to be somewhere?" I asked.

"Class ended about a minute ago."

"Oh."

I told them I'd see them on Wednesday.

Then I helped Hannah carry the fish downstairs.

* * *

Judith and I ate lunch together on Thursdays.

She was very interested in the relationship between the things we'd been discussing in my course and some questions she had about the nature of explanation.

"There's one thing you do that I find a little odd," she said.

"Only one?"

"Well, one thing I'd like to talk about."

"Okay."

"You spend a lot of time trying to establish that a person can be mistaken about the things that would be good for them."

"Among other things."

"And you spend a lot of time trying to establish that other people shouldn't necessarily decide those things for them."

"Yeah."

"Why?"

I told her that I didn't think there was a contradiction between the two claims.

"I know," she said. "I'm asking you why you care about the second one."

I thought about it.

I told her that I thought part of the importance of a person's being able to decide what to do with their life consisted in the person's being able to participate in determining what kind of life it would become.

She nodded.

"Well, what does that consist in?"

I gave her a few examples.

There was somebody deciding to go somewhere. There was somebody deciding to undertake a piece of work. There was somebody discovering something they liked doing that they hadn't known about before.

"Those are fairly agreeable examples," she said.

"Yeah."

"Suppose the person isn't particularly good at it."

"At what?"

"Determining what kind of life their life is going to become."

I looked at her.

"Okay."

"And suppose there are people who would be rather good at helping them do it."

"Good."

"Then isn't that one of the things you want?"

"Yes."

She put down her fork.

"Well, I'd like to know what makes something an instance of helping somebody participate in the determination of their life, rather than an instance of determining their life for them."

I thought of Louis and his sister. I thought of Malcolm's father and the little tape recorder.

Then I thought of the radio in the shed two houses down.

I got a piece of paper.

Judith laughed.

"I was hoping we'd finish lunch first."

We finished lunch.

* * *

I had received an invitation to give a series of lectures in England the following summer. The university that was organizing the lectures wanted me to prepare a book based on them.

I showed the letter to Nadia.

"That's exciting," she said.

"Yeah."

"What's the book going to be about?"

"I've got some ideas."

She put the letter down.

"How many?"

"Quite a few."

She laughed.

I told her that there was a question I'd been thinking about for much of the previous year. It concerned the circumstances under which a person could regard the decisions they made as their own, even if they'd acquired the capacities, the information and the desires involved in making those decisions from somebody else.

"Like the bicycle?" she asked.

"Possibly."

"Do you wish I'd let you decide which bicycle to buy?"

"No."

"Okay."

She looked at the letter.

"Would you like to go over there for the summer?"

"Yeah."

"Okay."

She was going to be working in Texas for part of the summer. She thought she could arrange to spend a few weeks in England with me. There were also some places in Spain she wanted us to go to afterward.

We looked at a calendar.

It occurred to me that there was a very good chance I was going to write an entire book about questions of this sort while spending a considerable amount of my time engaging in activities of this sort.

I liked the idea.

* * *

On a Friday in October, I found Simon sitting at our kitchen table.

He had brought a package containing a thing that would enable him to connect an old radio to a more recent piece of equipment. He was waiting for the other Adrian to get home.

I told him about the book.

"Good," he said.

"Yeah."

"Is this going to be the book you were talking about when I met you?"

"No."

"Okay."

"Why?"

"Well, I thought that was going to be quite a good book."

"Do you remember what it was about?"

"No."

He turned the package over.

"Do you?"

"Some of it."

He smiled.

"Well, write the other one, then."

He went to get some coffee.

I looked at the package.

There were now a number of things I'd been able to tell Simon about that had actually happened. I'd once spent a considerable proportion of our friendship telling him about things that I thought I would do if various things happened.

I asked him whether he'd been pleased by the way things had gone.

"With what?"

"The institute. The research. All of it."

"Yeah."

"Really?"

He came back to the table.

"Adrian, I've known you for fourteen years."

"Yeah."

"I was pretty happy when you were living in that awful apartment."

"It wasn't awful."

"Well, it was a little awful."

I laughed.

He opened the package.

"I didn't think you'd be a better friend if you had an institute."

I looked at him.

"Okay."

He took the thing out of the package.

"Although it was fun having an institute."

"Yeah."

He held the thing up.

"Do you want to know what this does?"

"Sure."

He told me.

* * *

In the evenings I began writing the lectures.

I had a tendency to begin writing a philosophical paper with an account of something somebody had said that I thought was incorrect. This time I tried something different.

I began with an account of a decision I'd made.

It was a fairly ordinary decision. I had decided to go to an interview. I had a number of reasons for going to the interview, some of which I'd had before I received the telephone call from Helen and some of which I hadn't.

I began listing the people and things that had contributed to my having those reasons.

There was the woman at the university in Oregon. There was Ray. There was Simon. There were people who had written things I'd read many years earlier. There was an artificial system that had produced a document.

I got quite a long way with the list.

Then I stopped.

The decision I was describing had been mine. I hadn't made any of those things happen in order to be able to make it. And I wasn't particularly interested in the possibility of having been a person who had made the decision without them.

I tried to say something about that.

I wasn't satisfied with what I'd written.

I went into the music room and played for a while.

When I came back, there was a note from Hannah in my email. She'd been reading a paper I'd recommended to her. She had a question about a claim I'd made in class.

I read the question.

I deleted a paragraph I'd written.

Then I wrote back to her.

The following week she brought me one of the fish.

It was blue. It had a crooked little tail and an expression that seemed to indicate considerable surprise at the circumstances in which it found itself.

I put it on the bookshelf in my office.

It is still there.
