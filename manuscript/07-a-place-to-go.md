## Seven — A Place to Go

Nadia had told me to meet her under a bridge, which struck me as a wonderfully economical way of finding out whether a person was willing to go on a date with you. I was not sure it was a date. I was, however, under the bridge.

She arrived on a bicycle.

"Where's yours?"

"I don't have one."

"You live in Dallas and you don't have a bicycle?"

"I live in Dallas, which is why I don't have a bicycle."

She took off her helmet. Her hair had acquired several architectural features.

"I guess we're walking."

We were near the river, though you wouldn't have known it from where we stood. There was a great deal of grass, a very big sky, and a series of concrete columns supporting an impressive quantity of traffic. Nadia locked her bicycle to a rack and began walking toward a low brick building on the other side of the road.

It had once been a warehouse. There were still old painted letters on one wall, and somebody had done a fairly good job of leaving them alone. The windows were new. Through them I could see tables and chairs, several enormous plants, and a woman with a needle and thread.

"What is it?" I asked.

"A few things."

We went inside. There was a workshop where people were making furniture and a room full of sewing machines. At the far end, beyond a partition, somebody was giving a piano lesson.

A man in an apron called out to Nadia. She waved.

"You did all this?"

"Not the piano lesson."

She showed me a wall that could be opened to join two rooms, a little kitchen, and a staircase that went up to a second floor.

"Originally they wanted a coffee shop here," she said. "Which would have been fine. I like coffee shops. But you have to keep buying coffee."

"I've noticed."

"Here you can just be here."

We went upstairs. There were bookshelves and a long window with a view of the bridge. A boy was asleep on a couch with his mouth open and one foot on the floor. I envied him.

"Is it free?"

"Some things are. Some things aren't. They've got grants, and the workshop helps pay for the rest."

She pointed out a piece of the old ceiling that she'd wanted to keep. It contained a pulley that apparently had once been used to move heavy equipment around. It was a beautiful object, although I probably would not have thought so if she hadn't drawn my attention to it.

"How'd you get involved in architecture?" I asked.

"My father built houses. Badly."

"Really?"

"No, pretty well. But he had terrible taste."

She told me about growing up in El Paso and going with her father to houses he'd built, sometimes years after the owners had moved in. He liked to see what people had done to the places. A couple in one of the houses had put in a window where there'd been no window. Another family had painted every interior wall purple.

"He loved it," she said. "I think he felt like he'd started something."

"That's a nice way of thinking about what you do."

"It is. Except when people put in a terrible window."

A little later we got coffee from a machine downstairs and took it outside. There was a large wooden table with two benches. The sun was out, and the table was warm.

I gave her the menu with the library on it.

"You didn't have to bring it."

"I've been carrying it around all week."

"Oh, God. Don't tell me you laminated it."

"Not yet."

She studied the drawing.

"We might get another shot at this."

"Really?"

"Different site. Smaller budget. There's a woman on the council who hasn't given up on it."

She folded the menu and put it in her bag.

"How's your mysterious job?"

"Interesting."

"Are they mysterious people?"

"Some of them. One's got a refrigerator in his office and nothing in it but mustard."

"That's a pretty mysterious guy."

I told her about the general problem I was working on, without telling her about the individual cases. She listened, asked several questions, and eventually interrupted me.

"Can you give me an example of what a good prediction would be?"

"Well, suppose you're trying to work out whether there's a need for a bus route."

"Okay."

"And you make a reasonably accurate prediction of how many people would use it."

"Great."

"But suppose it turns out nobody can afford the bus fare. They don't use it. Does that make your prediction about the need for a bus route wrong?"

"Depends on the prediction."

"Exactly."

She waited.

"That's it?"

"That's a big part of it."

"That's what you're getting paid to do?"

"Yeah."

She looked delighted.

"I've been doing that for free."

We finished our coffee.

There was a man in a wheelchair trying to get through the door of the building. A woman inside noticed him, pushed the door open, and held it until he got through. Nadia watched them.

"That's a problem," she said.

"What is?"

"The door. We had a push button there. It must be broken."

She got out her phone, took a photograph, and sent somebody a message.

Then we walked toward the river.

* * *

There was a period of my life during which I had read a great deal about the experience of time. I was interested in whether time seemed to pass more quickly when one was happy, whether boredom was principally an awareness of time, and why the memory of a very short period of one's life could occupy considerably more space than the memory of a very long one.

I had not, during that period, gone on many walks with women I liked.

Nadia and I walked for a couple of hours. At one point we ended up beside a fence looking at a horse. Neither of us knew why the horse was there. It was a perfectly respectable-looking horse, and we agreed that it had as much right to be there as we did.

She asked whether I'd ever been married.

"No."

"Nearly?"

"Probably nearer than I realized."

She nodded.

"I was married for four years."

"What happened?"

"Well, we were very good at deciding what to have for dinner."

I waited.

"And then we discovered there were other decisions."

I laughed. She did too.

"He was a nice man," she said. "I don't think either of us particularly wanted to be married to the other one."

"Did you know that when you got married?"

"No. We knew it when we got unmarried."

We passed a little boy flying a kite. The kite was shaped like a shark and was experiencing considerable difficulty in an environment for which sharks hadn't been designed.

I told her I was going to London.

"Have you been?"

"Once. For a conference. I saw a hotel, two lecture rooms, and the inside of an Underground station."

"That's a shame."

"Have you?"

"I lived there for a year. There's a little park near the house where I used to live. If you go, I'll give you the address."

"What's in the park?"

"Nothing especially. A bench."

"Sounds good."

"It was a good bench."

We got back to the building around four. She unlocked her bicycle. I felt that the day had arrived at a point at which I ought to say something. I was not sure what.

"Do you want to have dinner sometime?" I asked.

"Yes."

"Okay."

She put on her helmet.

"Did you think I was going to say no?"

"I hadn't really thought about it."

"You're a terrible liar."

She rode off, and I walked to my car.

I had left it in a place where I could see the tops of the buildings downtown. The sun was low by then, and some of the windows were bright gold. I stood there for a little while. I thought about the fact that in a few weeks I'd be getting on a plane to London, and that on Thursday I'd be playing music in Richardson, and that a woman I liked had just agreed to have dinner with me.

Nothing very extraordinary had happened.

Nevertheless, for the first time in a long while, I had the sensation that I was going somewhere.

* * *

At home I discovered an email from Elliot Finch.

It was the first email I'd received from him in seven years.

*Dear Adrian,*

*Miriam tells me you'll be joining us in London. I'm very pleased about this.*

*I wonder whether you would have time to come up to Cambridge beforehand. There is a consequence of your old argument that I should like to discuss with you in the absence of the usual audience.*

*Best wishes,*

*Elliot*

I read it twice. Then I searched my old email for our previous correspondence.

In the last message I had sent him, I had written three fairly long paragraphs defending an argument that I no longer thought was quite right. In his reply, Finch had written two sentences. The second was:

*I am not persuaded that the situation you describe could arise.*

I had been irritated by the sentence for seven years.

I looked at his new email.

Then I wrote back and told him that I would be delighted to come to Cambridge.
