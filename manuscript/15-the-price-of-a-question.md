## Fifteen — The Price of a Question

Simon had a friend who owned a building in the Design District. The building was divided into several spaces, one of which had recently been vacated by a man who made very expensive lamps.

"Look at the light," Simon said.

There was a long row of windows high up on one wall. Below them was a concrete floor with the outlines of things that had once stood on it. The room was cold and smelled a little of sawdust.

"What do you think?"

"I think it's where a guy used to make lamps."

"Well, it can be where a guy used to make lamps and a guy currently makes whatever it is you make."

I walked to the other end of the room. There was a small kitchen, two offices, and a door that opened onto a paved area in the back.

I had made the mistake, a few nights earlier, of telling Simon I was thinking about starting an institute. Within twenty-four hours he had formed the opinion that the main thing preventing me from starting an institute was the absence of a suitable building.

"How much?" I asked.

He told me.

"That's a lot of money."

"Yeah."

"Where would I get the money?"

"Well, that's the next question."

"Shouldn't that have been the previous question?"

He looked up at the windows.

"Probably."

We left the building and went to lunch.

I explained to him what I had in mind. I wanted somewhere people could come to work on difficult questions without first having to establish that somebody stood to make a great deal of money from the answers. It wouldn't have to be big. There could be a few people who were particularly good at mathematics, a few who knew how to build things, and some people who knew enough about the history of ideas to prevent us from spending six months rediscovering something that had been established in 1927.

"Do they have to come to Dallas?" he asked.

"No."

"Do they have to be in the same room?"

"No."

"Then why do you need a building?"

I looked at him.

"You're the guy who showed me the building."

"I know. It was a really good building."

He took out a pen.

"Let's start with the first year. How many people?"

"Three or four."

"Okay. What are they going to do?"

"Research."

"On what?"

I spent fifteen minutes answering the question. He listened, and at the end of it he asked me a question that made it apparent I had not answered it.

"What are they going to have done when the year's over?"

I got another piece of paper.

There was a kind of experiment I had wanted to conduct for several years, which involved setting a number of artificial systems the same task and allowing them considerable freedom in how they approached it. The difficult part was not getting the systems to produce results. The difficult part was determining what the results were evidence of.

I wanted to build a place where the claims could be made public in advance and where the evaluation would be independent of the people trying to make the systems look good.

I told Simon about it.

"Could you start with something small?"

"Sure."

"Then do that."

We ordered more coffee.

"And Adrian?"

"Yeah?"

"I've got a little money I could put into something. Not a building. Something."

"Simon."

"I don't mean I'm going to give you money because I like you. I mean I'd like to see if you can do it. Figure out how much it would take to do the little version."

I sat there looking at him.

He pointed at my piece of paper.

"Also, you haven't got a name."

"I've got about twelve names."

"How bad are they?"

"Pretty bad."

"Good. I'll think of one."

* * *

Matthew Bell was a man with a lovely speaking voice and a framed photograph of two children on a beach. He had made a career of taking complicated institutional problems and producing documents in which they appeared to have been adequately addressed.

At least that was the impression I formed of him during the first fifteen minutes of our meeting.

Miriam was there, and Peter, and a woman from the legal department named Susan. There was a plate of little cookies in the middle of the table. Matthew took one.

Peter brought up the Finch record.

"Do you remember reviewing this?" he asked.

Matthew leaned forward.

"Not specifically."

"Okay. Can you tell us what you would normally do in a case like this?"

He explained that the program had assembled contributions from its advisers, and that members of his team had been assigned to check the resulting documents against the source material.

"Did Professor Finch approve the contribution?"

"I wouldn't have been checking that."

"What were you checking?"

"Whether the statement was correctly represented in the source."

Peter opened the original generated contribution.

"Was this the source?"

"Possibly."

"So you were checking that the generated statement had been accurately copied?"

Matthew looked at Susan.

"Among other things."

Miriam spoke.

"Matthew, I need you to be quite clear about this. Was anybody checking that Elliot had actually said it?"

"I don't know. That wasn't our function."

"Whose function was it?"

"I imagine it would have been the responsibility of the program."

"Who in the program?"

He looked uncomfortable.

"I couldn't tell you."

I asked whether he understood why we'd called the meeting.

"Yes," he said. "I do."

"Then can you tell us how a person goes about correcting an attribution after it's been verified?"

He thought for a moment.

"They'd submit a correction."

"To whom?"

"To the program."

"And if the program doesn't accept it?"

He set the cookie down.

"Adrian, I'm not trying to be difficult."

"I know."

"We've got something like four thousand records in that project. If we had to contact an adviser every time somebody incorporated a sentence of their work into a document, we'd never get anything done."

"Well, I don't think you have to. But if you say they made a recommendation at a meeting, it seems to me that somebody ought to know whether they made a recommendation at a meeting."

"I agree."

He said it quietly and with such apparent sincerity that I didn't know what to say next.

Miriam asked Peter to bring up the report that Gabriel had sent me. Matthew read the quotation from Elliot. He sat very still for a little while.

"Oh," he said.

Susan asked whether anyone outside Northline had been informed of the issue.

"Elliot has," I said.

"Of course."

"And the people involved in the Brussels meeting need to know."

She looked at Miriam.

"We can arrange a communication through the appropriate channels."

"Fine," Miriam said. "Arrange one."

Matthew got up to leave. Then he sat down again.

"There's something you ought to know," he said.

He told us that the verification process had been changed the previous summer. Under the earlier procedure, the source type and the attribution status had been retained separately in the exported records. Under the newer procedure, a verified item could be presented without information about whether its source was generated or human-authored.

"Why?" Peter asked.

"I think it had to do with a client request. They wanted a simpler record."

"Which client?"

"I'd have to find out."

"Please do," Miriam said.

He left without finishing his cookie.

* * *

On Thursday night I told Walter that I might be going to Belgium.

"What for?"

"Work."

"That's good. You can buy me a little statue of that boy peeing."

Ray looked up from his guitar.

"Why?"

"I always liked that little guy."

We played for the next two hours. Toward the end of the evening Judy suggested that we make a recording of the piece we'd been playing.

We set up a phone in the middle of the room. Walter's granddaughter had been put in charge of pressing the button.

"What do you call it?" she asked me.

I thought about it.

"Westbound."

She considered the name.

"Okay."

She pressed the button.

It was not a particularly good recording. Walter played a note he didn't intend to play, and somebody opened a door in the middle of it, and there was a period of perhaps ten seconds during which I was playing considerably louder than I should have been.

When I got home I listened to it three times.

Then I sent it to Nadia.

*Can I have the version with the door?* she wrote back.

*That's the only version.*

*Good.*

* * *

The following afternoon Miriam asked me to come to her office.

She had a copy of the proposal Asha had sent me. She was enthusiastic about the work. She'd spoken to Asha herself and thought that Northline might be able to make a substantial contribution to the funding of the project.

"That's wonderful," I said.

"There'd be some conditions."

I sat down.

She explained that the institute was preparing a new program in the evaluation of automated decision systems. There would be research positions in London and Dallas, and an opportunity for me to direct some of the work. I would have a budget, a small team, and access to data I could not reasonably hope to obtain as an independent researcher.

I had the curious experience of being offered, for the second time in a few months, a job that seemed to have been designed by a person who knew precisely what I wanted.

"What's the condition?" I asked.

"The work would have to be coordinated with our assurance program. You'd be able to publish, but the findings would need to go through the usual review."

"Would we be able to publish findings about Northline?"

"Potentially. Subject to the relevant agreements."

"Even if you disagreed with the findings?"

"Adrian, this is a large organization. There are all kinds of people who disagree with one another."

"Would we be able to publish them if somebody in the assurance program disagreed with them?"

She put the proposal down.

"I don't know what you're planning to discover."

"Neither do I."

We looked at each other.

"Think about it," she said. "It's an opportunity to do something considerable."

I took the proposal back to my office.

There were two figures in the budget. One was the amount of money Northline was proposing to make available. The other was the amount Simon and I had arrived at over lunch, which I had written at the top of a piece of paper and stuck to the wall.

The first figure had two more zeros than the second.

I got up and looked out of the window.

It would have been considerably easier if I'd been offered a bad job.
