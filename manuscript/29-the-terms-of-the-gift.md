## Twenty-nine — The Terms of the Gift

In December a woman in California offered us a million dollars to investigate whether an artificial intelligence could suffer.

The letter was beautifully written. It referred to the work we'd been doing with Peregrine, to the questions Astrid and I had raised in a recent paper, and to the fact that there were very few institutions with the necessary combination of philosophical and technical expertise to undertake the investigation.

I read that last part to Nadia while she was getting dressed.

"Does she know there's a rooster?" Nadia asked.

"No."

"Well, don't tell her."

The offer came from a foundation called the Emmett Foundation, which had been established by two people who'd made a considerable amount of money developing educational software. One of them, Dana Emmett, wanted to meet us.

Fatima arranged a call.

Dana had a son who was studying philosophy and a daughter who, in her opinion, was much more sensible than her son. She explained that the foundation had become concerned about the possibility that some of the systems being developed in the coming years might be capable of experiencing things.

"What would you like us to establish?" I asked.

"Whether there's anything there that we ought to be worried about."

I told her that I thought that was a good question but that there was a significant possibility that at the end of three years we wouldn't know the answer.

"Well, I think it's important to try," she said.

I agreed.

She asked us to send her a proposal. Her assistant sent us the usual documents. It was Fatima who noticed the provision.

The foundation would be entitled to use the institute's name in connection with the promotion of its approved research initiatives. The language was very broad. I asked her to find out what kinds of initiatives were contemplated.

She came into my office the following morning and shut the door.

"Have you heard of a company called Continuance?"

"No."

She put her laptop on my desk.

It was a website advertising a service that enabled people to preserve some of the characteristics of their intellectual lives. The examples included a historian whose students would be able to consult a representation of him after his retirement, and a woman who had collected a great deal of information about a rare species of bird.

There was an attractive little video in which the historian talked to his representation.

I watched it.

"Okay," I said.

"Look at the investors."

Dana Emmett was one of them. There was another name on the list that I recognized.

Vincent Roos.

* * *

Fatima and I talked to Dana the following afternoon. She was quite open about the relationship. She'd invested in Continuance eighteen months earlier. She wanted to know whether there were moral problems with some of the things the company was planning to do.

"That sounds reasonable," I said.

"We think this is something that ought to be independently investigated."

"Absolutely. But we don't want the company's customers to see our name on its website and think we've determined that the service is morally unproblematic."

"Well, I don't think we'd ever suggest that."

"Then can we take out the provision?"

She said she would ask the foundation's lawyer.

A week later we received a revised document. The provision was still there, though the word *promotion* had been replaced with the word *communication*.

Fatima came over with a piece of paper.

"Here," she said.

She had written two sentences. One permitted the foundation to state accurately that it had funded the research. The other prohibited it from implying that we had endorsed any product.

"Send that," I said.

She did.

We didn't hear anything for six days.

In the meantime I had begun to develop an entire imaginary organization in my head. There was a woman in Montreal I wanted to hire. There was a young man in Ohio who'd written a paper I liked very much. I had made a list of experiments we would be able to conduct with the money.

I even knew which office the woman in Montreal would have.

One evening I told Simon about it.

"Adrian, have you received a million dollars?"

"No."

"Okay. How much of it have you spent?"

"None."

"How much of it have you spent up here?"

He pointed at my head.

"About two million dollars."

He laughed.

"Well, stop that."

* * *

On the Monday before Christmas, we learned that the foundation had withdrawn the offer.

The reason given was that there appeared to be a difference between the foundation's proposed arrangements and the institute's expectations concerning the relationship.

I was furious.

I went into the main room and told everybody that we'd turned down a million dollars.

Fatima looked up.

"No, we didn't."

"What?"

"They withdrew it."

"Because of the conditions we wanted."

"Yes. But we didn't turn down a million dollars. We turned down some conditions. We never had the opportunity to turn down the money without the conditions."

I stood there.

"Okay."

She went back to work.

June looked at me.

"Are you going to put that on the whiteboard?"

"No."

"Good."

I went out and got lunch.

* * *

The thing I hadn't anticipated about running a research institute was that there were going to be people whose lives were affected by my opinions about what constituted a good research institute.

That afternoon Peter asked me whether I had a minute.

His wife, Beth, had been offered a position with a company in Austin. It was something she'd wanted to do for several years. They had been discussing whether he could continue working with us if they moved.

"Of course," I said.

"Okay. That's good."

He looked at the desk.

"And how are we doing financially?"

"We're fine."

"How fine?"

I gave him an account of the funding. We still had the original commitment, and some additional money for particular projects. We had a little over two and a half years of planned operations left, assuming we didn't undertake any major new commitments.

"All right," he said.

"I don't know what will happen after that."

"Sure."

He got up.

"Look, I think you did the right thing with the foundation."

"Thanks."

"But if the next people who come along want to fund something that isn't the thing you'd most like to do, I think we ought to at least talk about it."

I nodded.

"We will."

He went back to his desk.

I sat there thinking about Beth. I'd met her a few times. She was very funny and had some fairly strong opinions about the arrangement of supermarkets. I knew practically nothing about the work she wanted to do.

I went over to Peter's desk.

"What sort of job is it?" I asked.

He told me.

It was a rather remarkable job.

By the time I went home, I was almost as excited about the job as he was.

* * *

We spent Christmas in Dallas. On Christmas morning Nadia gave me a little device that enabled me to record several parts of a piece of music and play them together.

I had wanted one for some time.

I went into the room at the back and played a little progression. Then I played something over it, and something over that. There were now three of me in the room.

Nadia came in.

"What are you doing?"

"Playing with myself."

"Well, don't say that to anybody."

I laughed.

She sat on the floor and listened for a while.

Then she asked if she could play something on the guitar.

I showed her how to make a sound with two notes. She made the sound several times. I began playing something over it. We were doing very well until I tried to tell her when she ought to make the sound.

"Don't do that," she said.

"Okay."

She made the sound when she wanted to.

It became considerably more interesting.

Later Simon came over for dinner. He brought a bottle of wine and a little package from Malcolm. The package contained a letter and a small photograph.

The photograph was of Malcolm's father standing in front of a house. On the back he'd written the date and the temperature.

The letter was addressed to both of us.

Malcolm had heard about the foundation. He wanted us to know that he thought the board had handled the matter well. He wasn't proposing to replace the lost funding. He thought it would be better for us to find a number of additional sources of support than to become more dependent on him.

At the bottom he'd written:

*Incidentally, I've been wondering whether you'd be interested in meeting some people I know who might enjoy working on a problem with you. They don't have any money. They have a boat.*

I handed the letter to Simon.

He read it.

"A boat?"

"Yeah."

"What kind of boat?"

"I don't know."

He looked at Nadia.

"You're going to have to go with him."

"Why?"

"Because if he goes by himself he's going to come back with a boat."

* * *

The following morning I got an email from Anika Weiss.

A commercial organization had made an offer to acquire certain assets of the Heller Centre's scientific discovery program. The offer included the software for managing research processes of the kind we had been investigating with Peregrine.

Our current collaboration was protected by the existing agreement, Anika said. Beyond that, some of the arrangements would have to be renegotiated.

I read the email standing in the kitchen.

The organization making the offer was Continuance.

I went outside.

The rooster was standing on the other side of the fence. I could see its head between two pieces of wood.

It looked at me for a little while.

Then it went off to do something else.
