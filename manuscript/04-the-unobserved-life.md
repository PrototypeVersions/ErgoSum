## Four — The Unobserved Life

On my first morning at Northline, I got an email from the university in Oregon informing me that it had been a pleasure getting to know me.

I knew what the rest of the email said before I read it. Nonetheless I read it, and then read it again, and experienced the familiar little pain of being rejected for something I no longer wanted.

Helen took me upstairs to see my office.

It was bigger than my living room. There was a desk that could be raised and lowered by means of a button, a view of downtown, a whiteboard, and a small refrigerator. There was also a plant that I subsequently established had belonged to my predecessor.

"Did somebody else have this job?" I asked.

"Not exactly this job."

"What happened to them?"

"He went to Switzerland."

"Oh."

"He had a wife in Switzerland."

"That's a pretty good reason."

"I thought so."

Helen handed me a key.

"You've got the room until five today. After that the key should work all the time. They're doing something to the locks."

I raised the desk, lowered it, then raised it again.

"Don't get addicted," she said.

There was something about Helen that I liked immediately and couldn't quite place. She never looked at her phone while she was talking to you. If you told her something mildly amusing she would sometimes laugh several seconds after you had said it, as though she had been thinking about whether it was actually amusing.

"How'd you end up here?" I asked.

"I used to teach high school."

"What subject?"

"History. Then I worked at a foundation. Miriam hired me from there."

"Do you miss it?"

"Teaching?"

"Yeah."

"Some days. I miss having an audience that will tell you when you're boring."

She looked at her watch.

"Miriam wants you in her office."

I left the desk halfway up.

* * *

The Garland review was twenty-seven pages long. Miriam gave me a paper copy and asked me not to take it out of her office.

She made herself a cup of tea while I read.

It was considerably less dramatic than I had expected. There were tables, graphs, details about the size and composition of the development, lists of recommendations. Northline had been engaged to estimate the likely uptake of several proposed relocation packages. Its client was supposed to use the estimates to budget for payments. The model had not been commissioned to establish whether anybody had consented to anything.

The internal review stated this in several different ways.

"Did you tell them?" I asked.

"Repeatedly."

"What happened?"

"They changed the wording in subsequent reports."

"And what happened to the people?"

"I don't know."

I found that answer more disturbing than if she had made an excuse.

I turned to the appendices. One contained an estimate of the number of residents who would accept the four-thousand-dollar offer. Another estimated uptake for six thousand, eight thousand and twelve thousand dollars.

"Why is there no uncertainty interval in this table?"

"It's in the technical report."

"Is the client given the technical report?"

"Yes."

"Do they have to read it?"

"No."

I looked out the window. Far below us somebody was taking a photograph of a building.

"Would you rather they made these decisions without any information?" Miriam asked.

"No."

"Because that's the choice in a lot of these places. Somebody makes a guess. Sometimes somebody makes a guess based on nothing more than which people he thinks look wealthy."

"I understand."

"We've done work that I'm extremely proud of. We identified a problem with the proposed location of a clinic in South Texas. They moved the clinic. People are getting medical care who otherwise wouldn't."

"I'd like to see that."

"I'll have Helen send it to you."

She put her tea down.

"There's something else. We ran a different set of simulations for Garland. Not for the client. For ourselves."

"Simulations of what?"

"Of people who were given offers."

"Isn't that what you did the first time?"

"No. The first time we used a relatively conventional model. This was a project Peter's group was developing. You'll hear a lot about it in the next few weeks."

She opened a drawer and brought out a thin folder.

"We call it the Observatory."

I liked the name. I thought it was an unfortunate thing to like at that particular moment, but I liked it anyway.

Inside the folder were transcripts. There were maybe forty lines on each page, with names omitted. Most concerned proposed relocation agreements.

**INTERVIEWER:** Would you be willing to move if you received a payment of twelve thousand dollars?

**RESIDENT 6:** Where would I move?

**INTERVIEWER:** You could choose.

**RESIDENT 6:** No, I mean where is there for me to move?

I turned the page.

"These are generated?"

"Yes."

"From what information?"

"That varies considerably by participant. For this pilot, interviews and questionnaires, with additional information about their circumstances. People volunteered."

"Did Alma Reyes volunteer?"

Miriam looked at me.

"How did you get that name?"

"I spoke to her attorney."

"Alma was not part of this project."

"Okay."

"We didn't have a simulation of Alma Reyes."

I believed her. I was aware, even as I believed her, that I didn't yet know enough about her to know whether believing her was a good idea.

The next transcript was about a man who lived with his brother. He accepted the first offer, then explained at length why he shouldn't have.

"How do you know whether any of this is good?"

"That's a question for you and Peter. The volunteers have since participated in actual interviews. There are some interesting results."

I leafed back through the documents.

"Did any of them see the generated interviews?"

"Some did."

"What did they think?"

"Some thought they were remarkable. Some thought they were offensive. One woman asked if she could speak to hers."

I looked up.

"Did you let her?"

"Yes."

"How'd that go?"

"Not well."

Miriam got up and went to the window.

"Adrian, I don't expect you to come in here and tell me that everything we're doing is wrong. There are plenty of people who can do that. I want you to find out where we're wrong."

It was a surprisingly beautiful distinction. I wished it hadn't been made by somebody who was paying me an enormous amount of money.

* * *

Peter worked in a room that was almost entirely devoid of evidence that human beings inhabited it. There were four monitors and a packet of crackers. The packet of crackers was the principal evidence.

He had a doctorate in statistics and a little boy who had recently learned to say the word excavator. I found out the latter before the former because the boy's daycare called while I was sitting there.

"Was it an actual excavator?" Peter said into the phone.

He listened for a moment.

"Well, tell him that's tremendous."

He hung up.

"Sorry."

"No, that's tremendous."

He showed me the Observatory.

Most of it was less mysterious than its name suggested. There were ordinary models underneath it, large amounts of data, and a lot of work that appeared to have consisted of getting pieces of software to stop doing things they weren't supposed to do.

I was pleased. For some reason I had been afraid he would show me a black screen with a little white circle in the middle.

"How did you generate my interview?" I asked.

He took his hands off the keyboard.

"Miriam hasn't told you?"

"No."

"That wasn't one of our regular evaluations. She wanted something specific."

"Which was?"

"An assessment of the recruitment process."

"For me?"

"For a number of candidates."

"Why did you send me the transcript?"

"I didn't."

"Helen did."

"Okay."

"And the second version?"

Peter frowned.

"What second version?"

I described the attachment I had received the previous evening.

"Do you have it?"

I took out my phone. He read both transcripts carefully. He went back to the first, then to the second.

"Can I forward these to myself?"

"I'd rather you didn't yet. Is there anything unusual about the second one?"

He looked at the timestamp.

"Not necessarily."

"What about the conversation with Simon?"

"Who is Simon?"

"A friend. He suggested that the transcript was a recruitment gimmick. That evening. The second transcript included his suggestion."

Peter sat very still.

"Those are the words he used?"

"Approximately."

"Did you mention it to anyone else?"

"No."

"Did you talk about it in a message? To him or somebody else?"

"No."

"Well," Peter said, "that's a pretty obvious interpretation of the first document. I don't see why a system couldn't have generated it."

This had occurred to me. It was also what Simon himself had said when I told him.

"Can you tell when it was generated?"

"Probably."

He searched for the record. After a minute he brought up a small table.

"There. First run. Wednesday, eighteen forty-two. That's the long one. There are a few shorter ones here."

"And the second?"

He scrolled.

"Hang on."

The table stopped.

"Friday morning," he said.

"Before or after Helen sent it?"

"What time did she send it?"

"Ten fourteen. Thursday night."

He looked at my phone again.

"I don't know," he said.

For a while we looked at the table together. Then he clicked on a different tab. I saw a blank panel and a line of gray text indicating that some items were not available to his account.

"Maybe there's another project," he said.

"Can you ask?"

"I can ask."

He picked up the packet of crackers.

"Want one?"

"Sure."

We ate crackers in front of the four monitors.

* * *

At four thirty I went back to my office and lowered the desk to an appropriate height. I wrote three things on the whiteboard.

1. What are we measuring?
2. What happens when we use the measurement?
3. Who gets to object?

I stood there looking at the board. I had written versions of those questions for a number of years, on a number of whiteboards, in buildings that I no longer had access to. The third question was new.

I took a photograph of it.

Then I wrote a fourth question.

4. Who sent the second interview?

There was a knock. Helen was standing in the doorway.

"How was your first day?"

"Good. Can I ask you something?"

"Sure."

"Where'd you get the revised interview transcript?"

"From the portal."

"On Thursday?"

"Yes."

"Who put it there?"

"I don't know. They come through automatically."

"Did you send it to me because Miriam asked?"

She hesitated.

"No."

"Why'd you send it?"

She came in and shut the door.

For the first time that day she looked tired.

"Because the first one had been marked as a refusal."

"I hadn't refused anything."

"I know."

"And the second one?"

She looked at the whiteboard.

"The second one had been marked as a correction."
