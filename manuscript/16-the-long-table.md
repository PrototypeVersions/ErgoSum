## Sixteen — The Long Table

It snowed in Dallas the week before I went to Brussels.

There wasn't much snow, but there was sufficient snow for everyone to have an opinion about it. The schools closed. Northline told us to work from home. Walter sent a picture of the ceramic frog in his front yard wearing a small white hat.

Nadia came over with groceries. We made soup and spent most of the afternoon at opposite ends of the table, working on things that had nothing to do with each other.

At about three she took her glasses off.

"Have you answered Miriam?"

"No."

"Are you going to?"

"I thought I'd do it after Brussels."

"Does she know that?"

"Yeah."

"Okay."

She went back to her work.

I was trying to construct a set of instructions for an experiment Simon had agreed to pay for. The idea was to give several artificial systems access to the same small mathematical environment and ask them to discover generalizations about it. A separate program would test the generalizations against examples the systems hadn't seen. Some of the examples would be easy to find. Others would be very difficult to find. We would distinguish between statements that had been tested, statements that had been disproved, and statements for which an actual proof had been supplied.

I had wanted to do something on a much larger scale, with famous unsolved problems. Simon had asked me whether anybody was going to give him his money back if we failed to solve a famous unsolved problem.

So we were starting with a collection of problems to which somebody knew the answers.

It was a good restriction. I resented it for about an hour and then began discovering things I could do with it.

"How much would you enjoy doing what Miriam wants you to do?" Nadia asked.

I looked up.

"A lot, probably."

"And how much would you enjoy doing this?"

"A lot."

She nodded.

"Well, you've got a nice problem."

I laughed.

Outside, two children were trying to make a snowman out of a quantity of snow that would have been insufficient for the manufacture of a reasonably sized snowball. Their father was helping them. He had gone into the house and brought out a saucepan, which they were apparently going to use as a hat.

I watched them for a little while.

Then I went back to the experiment.

* * *

Brussels was wet.

I realized on my first evening there that I didn't know very much about Belgium. I knew a few things about the history of the country, and a little about its political arrangements, and that there were certain kinds of food I ought to be eating. But I didn't have an imagined Brussels with which to compare the actual Brussels. I found this rather liberating.

Gabriel met me for dinner.

He was wearing a yellow scarf. I told him I liked it.

"My daughter bought it for me," he said. "She says I look like a person who would be difficult to see in the event of an accident."

We ate mussels in a restaurant down a little street near the center of the city. I asked him about the report that had quoted Elliot.

"The authors are aware of the problem," he said.

"Has it been corrected?"

"Not yet."

"Why not?"

"They're seeking clarification."

"From Elliot?"

"From the organization that provided the material."

"He's written to them. He says he didn't make the recommendation."

"Yes. I've seen his letter."

"Then what are we clarifying?"

Gabriel took another mussel.

"Tomorrow will be an interesting day for you."

"Why?"

"Because there are about twenty people coming who want to get a particular provision into a particular document. And there are about twenty different reasons why they want to get it into the document. Some of the reasons are rather good."

"Okay."

"If you succeed in demonstrating that this quotation is incorrectly attributed, they will say they don't need the quotation."

"I know."

"And if you succeed in demonstrating that a particular system is unreliable, they will say the provision applies only to reliable systems."

"Sure."

He looked at me.

"What would you like them to agree to?"

I began to tell him what was wrong with the proposed provision.

He shook his head.

"No. What would you like them to agree to?"

I stopped.

We spent the rest of dinner working on an answer to that question.

* * *

There were no flags in the room where we met the following morning. There was a long table, a great many electrical outlets, and a window that looked onto the side of another building. It wasn't a legislative session or anything resembling one. There were researchers, lawyers, civil servants and representatives of organizations that wanted their views to be known before the next version of a policy proposal was written.

A woman named Sabine chaired the meeting. She had spent much of her career in the administration of disability services. When she introduced herself, she told us that her principal interest was in getting things to people who needed them before the things ceased to be of use.

I thought that was a very good principal interest.

There were three people who represented companies that developed predictive systems. One of them was a man named Vincent Roos. He was younger than I was and had the sort of clothes that made me think about whether it was possible to buy clothes without looking as though one had bought them.

He gave a presentation about the benefits of automating certain administrative decisions. Most of what he said was quite sensible. There were people who waited months for services because somebody had to send them a letter, wait for a response to the letter, enter the response into a computer, and send them another letter.

He showed us an example involving a woman whose application for home assistance had been held up for eleven weeks because an office had been unable to obtain a particular document.

"Eleven weeks," Sabine said.

"Yes."

She shook her head.

Afterward I asked Vincent whether his system would have enabled the woman to obtain the assistance earlier.

"In that particular case, yes."

"Would she still have been able to decline it?"

"Of course."

"Good."

He looked at me.

"You sound surprised."

"No. I just think that's important."

"So do I."

It was a promising beginning.

Then we came to the paragraph about exceptional circumstances.

The lawyer sitting beside me read the paragraph out loud. She asked what was meant by an individual's preferences.

There were several answers. Vincent said that the systems his company developed were capable of identifying fairly stable patterns in a person's choices. A woman from a university in the Netherlands said there was an important distinction between what somebody had done under existing conditions and what they would choose under improved conditions. Somebody else asked what was meant by improved.

I thought of Alma.

I asked whether the provision would allow an institution to treat a decision to withdraw an offer as consistent with the interests of a person who hadn't yet received the offer.

Vincent said that would depend on the circumstances.

"Suppose the system is very reliable," I said. "It predicts, with ninety-nine percent accuracy, the people who would decline a particular offer. Can you use its prediction to decide not to make the offer?"

"I think that would depend on the offer."

"All right. Can we agree that the prediction alone doesn't establish that the person has declined the offer?"

"Of course."

"Could we put that in the document?"

There was a silence.

The lawyer beside me began typing.

Vincent leaned forward.

"The difficulty is that in some cases you're imposing a considerable administrative burden for very little benefit."

"Then let's specify the benefit and the burden. But let's not say the person declined the offer."

"Nobody's proposing that."

I took out the report that Gabriel had sent me.

I explained what Elliot had told me. I showed the group his written denial and the correction that Northline had finally issued the previous week. Gabriel had arranged for both documents to be circulated before the meeting, with Elliot's permission.

The woman from the Netherlands found the relevant paragraph in the report.

"Oh," she said.

Vincent looked at the documents for a while.

"Well, that's unfortunate," he said.

"Yes."

"But I don't think the position depends on Finch having said it."

"No. It doesn't."

I sat back.

Gabriel smiled at me from the other end of the table.

We had finally arrived at the meeting he and I had been discussing the night before.

* * *

We broke for lunch at one.

Sabine and I ate sandwiches beside the window. She asked me whether I had ever worked in the administration of a public service.

"No."

"Have you ever been responsible for deciding which of two people should receive something when there was only enough of it for one of them?"

"Not professionally."

"Well, it's a terrible thing to have to do."

"I imagine it is."

She told me about a man who had worked in one of her offices. He had spent a substantial part of every day explaining to people why they weren't going to get things that would have made their lives considerably better.

"He was a lovely man," she said. "And he used to go home and be horrible to his wife. He told me this himself. He said he had used up all his kindness by five o'clock."

She looked down at her sandwich.

"I would be very pleased if some of his work could be done by a machine."

"Sure."

"But I wouldn't want to live in a world in which the only person who knew what I needed was a machine."

We finished our sandwiches.

"Do you know what I think would be useful?" she said.

"What?"

"Something that tells me what I haven't asked."

I liked that very much.

When we went back to the table, I told the others what Sabine had said. She objected that I had made it sound considerably more intelligent than it had sounded when she'd said it.

We began working on some alternative language.

It wasn't dramatic. There were discussions about the words *solely* and *materially*. Somebody wanted to move a sentence into a footnote. The lawyer beside me wanted to know whether the term *decision* had already been defined somewhere else.

By four o'clock we had managed to produce a paragraph which stated that a forecast of a person's response did not, by itself, constitute the response, and that records containing forecasts should distinguish them from actual expressions of consent or refusal. There was also a proposal to require an accessible means of contesting materially consequential inferences about an individual.

Nobody had agreed on all of it. It would be taken back to other groups, and there would be further revisions. It might end up as a footnote. It might disappear altogether.

But it existed.

I had a copy of it in my bag.

* * *

That evening Vincent asked me to have a drink with him.

We went to a bar where the list of beers was nearly as long as the proposed framework we had been discussing.

He asked if I was planning to remain at Northline.

"I don't know."

"They've been doing some impressive work."

"Some of it."

He smiled.

"We used to be a client of theirs. Before we developed our own systems."

"What sort of work?"

"Evaluation. We were trying to standardize the reports that went to our customers."

I put my glass down.

"Did you ask them to change the way generated material was identified in their reports?"

He looked at me.

"I don't recall."

I didn't say anything.

"Well," he said, "we certainly asked them to make the reports more usable. There were a lot of different statuses. Nobody understood what they meant."

"Did you want the distinction between human and generated contributions removed?"

"No. Why would we want that?"

I told him, in general terms, what Matthew Bell had told us.

Vincent listened.

"That's a rather serious allegation," he said.

"It's not an allegation. I'm asking you a question."

"Well, if somebody's told you we asked them to misrepresent their data, I'd be interested in knowing who."

"Nobody told me that."

He picked up his glass.

We sat there for a while.

Then he said, "You know what I think the problem is with your approach, Adrian?"

"What?"

"You want every person to be an exception."

"No."

"You want to build systems that keep being interrupted by the people the systems are supposed to serve."

"Sometimes."

"That's an incredibly expensive thing to do."

I thought about the report Helen had given me.

"Well," I said, "I wouldn't be here if somebody hadn't interrupted one of yours."

He smiled, though I don't think he was amused.

He paid for the drinks. I offered to pay for mine, and he said I could get the next one.

Outside, it had stopped raining.

We shook hands.

* * *

Back at the hotel I called Nadia.

She was sitting in her car outside a grocery store. I told her about the day, and she asked me whether the people at the meeting had agreed to anything.

"A paragraph," I said.

"Just one?"

"Yeah."

"How many people were there?"

"About twenty."

"That's pretty good."

After we hung up I opened my laptop. Simon had sent me an email with a spreadsheet attached. The spreadsheet was called THINGS ADRIAN THINKS ARE FREE.

It included electricity, software, computing resources, his own time, my time, and a number of other things I had given insufficient consideration to.

At the bottom was a note.

*If we keep it this size, I'll cover the expenses for six months. Then we look at what you've done.*

I read it with an enormous feeling of gratitude.

Then I opened the message Miriam had sent me before I left Dallas.

I read the offer again.

I began writing a reply. I stopped twice. At one point I closed the laptop and went down to the lobby, where I bought a bottle of water that I didn't want.

Then I came back upstairs and finished the message.

I told her I was grateful for the offer. I told her that I wanted to continue doing the research we had discussed. I told her that I couldn't undertake to make the publication of our findings contingent on approval by the program whose work we would be evaluating.

I asked if there was a way of arranging the position on different terms.

I sent it.

I had not resigned. I had not started an institute. I had a small amount of money promised to me by a friend and an experiment I hadn't run.

But I had finally sent an email in which I hadn't tried to work out what the person receiving it would want me to say.
