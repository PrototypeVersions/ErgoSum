## Twelve — The Open Door

I had written a talk that lasted twenty-eight minutes. I had been given thirty, which meant that if I delivered it at the speed at which I had practiced it, and nobody asked me a question, and I didn't drop anything, and there wasn't a fire, I would have two minutes left.

I sat in the apartment on the morning of the second day and deleted most of it.

There had been a rather impressive introduction concerning the history of the philosophy of measurement. There had been a diagram with five arrows, two of which I'd added because the diagram hadn't looked sufficiently like a diagram. There had been quotations from three people I admired, none of whom had said anything particularly helpful about the problem I was going to discuss.

I kept two slides.

The first contained a picture of a door.

On the second I had written three questions.

I arrived at the meeting with about eight minutes to spare. Peter was standing in the hallway with two cups of coffee.

"Which one do you want?" he asked.

"What's in them?"

"Coffee."

"Well, that one."

He gave me the one in his right hand.

"That's the one I wanted you to choose," he said.

"Why?"

"Because there's considerably less coffee in it."

I looked in the cup.

"You bastard."

He gave me the other one.

"Are you ready?"

"More or less."

"Good. I have some results."

The records Louise had supplied the previous afternoon had enabled him to establish how the synthetic contribution had entered the conference documents. The source had been marked as generated material in the original workspace, but that distinction had disappeared when the material was incorporated into the document people actually read.

"Okay," I said.

"And we've got a couple of examples from other projects where something similar seems to have happened."

"Finch's?"

"Not yet."

He took a drink.

"There's one involving a committee in Brussels. And one from the appointments system."

"Mine?"

"Somebody else's. We're waiting on the older records."

"Do you think it's the same problem?"

"I think it's a family of problems."

We went into the room.

* * *

Elliot gave his talk before mine. It was about the circumstances in which it was reasonable to attribute a belief to somebody on the basis of things they had said. He mentioned Ruth only once. The passage he quoted from her work was about the difference between evidence for a person's assent and the occurrence of assent itself.

There was a nice discussion afterward.

Then it was my turn.

I stood up and put the picture of the door on the screen.

"Imagine," I said, "that you're responsible for a building with two entrances. You're asked to predict how many people will come through each entrance tomorrow."

I told them they could use any method they wanted. They could use historical data, send out questionnaires, build simulations of the people who lived in the neighborhood. They could make quite sophisticated predictions.

Then I asked them to imagine that the building manager had a policy of locking whichever entrance was predicted to have the fewest visitors.

Asha smiled.

"Now, suppose you're particularly interested in improving your accuracy. There are two things you can do. You can improve your knowledge of the people coming to the building, or you can persuade the building manager to lock more doors."

Gabriel said, "Depending on exactly how you score the prediction."

"Yes. And we ought to be quite precise about that."

I explained that this wasn't a new paradox or even a particularly difficult problem in statistics. People who studied causal inference, feedback and performative prediction had been thinking about related problems for years. What interested me was the way the purpose of a predictive system could change while the system continued to be evaluated in much the same way.

I put up the other slide.

*What would the person do?*

*What can the person do?*

*What is the person permitted to do?*

"I think we have several different research programs here," I said. "They don't necessarily have the same goals. And one of the things I'd like us to avoid is congratulating ourselves on how well we've done on the first question when what we've actually done is change the answer to the second or third."

A man from a consulting firm raised his hand.

"Surely the point of prediction is to enable intervention."

"Often, yes."

"Suppose we predict that someone will develop a disease, and on the basis of the prediction they're given treatment and don't develop it. That seems like a good outcome."

"Absolutely."

"So we shouldn't be trying to prevent predictions from influencing outcomes."

"No. We should be trying to find out what the influence is. And we should be able to distinguish a successful treatment from a failed prediction."

I took a drink of water. The water was astonishingly cold.

"What I'm proposing is that we begin with a specification of the circumstances in which the prediction is meant to be correct. That might include the availability of a service, whether a person knows about it, whether they've been shown the prediction, whether an institution acts on it. Then we have to establish separately what happens when those circumstances change."

The man nodded, though not enthusiastically.

There were several other questions. A woman asked how I would deal with differences in people’s preferences over time. I said that I didn't think a change of preference should automatically count as a failure of the representation, any more than a person growing taller ought to count as a failure of a photograph.

Elliot objected to my analogy.

I was extraordinarily pleased that he objected to my analogy.

We spent five minutes disagreeing about it.

At some point I looked at the clock and realized I had been standing there for forty-three minutes. Louise was sitting at the end of the table with her hands folded. I apologized.

"That's all right," she said. "We moved lunch."

I sat down.

It was, by a considerable distance, the best time I'd ever had giving a talk.

* * *

Afterward Asha came over to my chair.

"Would you mind sending me the slides?"

"Both of them?"

"Both of them."

She asked if I'd be interested in developing a project with her group. They were working on a study of participation in screening programs and had access to a setting in which some of the relevant questions could be investigated without changing anybody's eligibility or withholding services.

I said I would be very interested.

Then Gabriel came over. He had been discussing something with Miriam during lunch and wanted to arrange a call after Christmas.

"The difficulty," he said, "is that there are people on these committees who will listen to somebody like you in a way that they don't listen to somebody like me."

"Why?"

"Because you have the great advantage of not knowing which things they're tired of hearing."

I wasn't sure whether I had just been offered an opportunity or received a diagnosis. I gave him my email address.

Miriam waited until everyone else had gone.

"You enjoyed yourself," she said.

"Yeah."

"Good."

She picked up one of the notebooks I had left on the table.

"Have you talked to Helen today?"

"No."

"She had her meeting."

"How'd it go?"

Miriam put the notebook down.

"Not well. She's been placed on administrative leave."

"What for?"

"There are concerns about the disclosure of candidate records."

I looked at her.

"To me?"

"Among other things."

"She disclosed my own record to me."

"I know."

"And that's what enabled you to interview me."

"I know that, too."

"Well, what are we doing?"

"Adrian, there's a process."

"Is it a good process?"

She looked tired.

"At the moment I'm trying to make sure there is one."

I stood up.

"Can I talk to her?"

"Of course. She's not in prison."

I gathered my things.

At the door she called my name.

"Adrian, this isn't a particularly good time to start making enemies."

I thought about the day I'd had. About Asha, and Gabriel, and Elliot, and the fact that I had spent a very long time wanting precisely the things that were now beginning to happen to me.

"No," I said. "It really isn't."

* * *

I called Helen from the pavement.

It was morning in Dallas. I could hear birds in the background.

"Where are you?" I asked.

"In my backyard."

"Are you okay?"

"Yeah. I'm pulling weeds."

She told me about the meeting. She had been asked to explain the circumstances under which she'd contacted certain candidates, and in particular how she had obtained the second version of my interview. She had asked to be shown the relevant policy. They were going to get back to her.

"Do you want me to write something?" I said. "About what happened?"

"That would be good. Just your account of it. Nothing about what you think it means."

"Okay."

"And Adrian?"

"Yeah?"

"How's London?"

"Wonderful."

"Good. I want you to have a wonderful time in London. Don't sit in your apartment writing emails about me."

I promised to go out.

I wrote the account first. I included the dates, the things I could establish directly from my correspondence, and the things I remembered Helen saying. I was tempted to add a paragraph at the end. I deleted it.

Then I sent it to her.

* * *

Nadia's park was about a forty-minute walk from the apartment. I got there just as it was getting dark.

It was an extremely ordinary park. There were some trees, a little paved area with a few benches, and a fence that looked as if somebody had painted it the previous summer. Beyond the fence were houses with lights on in their windows.

I took a photograph of one of the benches and sent it to Nadia.

She wrote back almost immediately.

*Wrong bench.*

I sent her another photograph.

*That's it.*

I sat down.

*Well?* she wrote.

*Pretty good bench.*

*I told you.*

I called her.

She was at her office. The people in the office next to hers had gone out to lunch, and she was trying to finish something before they got back.

"How did the talk go?" she asked.

"Really well."

"Really?"

"Yeah. I think it was the first time in my life I knew, while I was talking, that I had something to say."

"Oh, Adrian."

"Yeah."

There was a little silence.

"Hey," she said. "Would you like to come with me to El Paso over New Year's?"

I hadn't been expecting that.

"My sister's doing something at her house. It's not particularly exciting. There's going to be a lot of food, and several dogs."

"I'd love to."

"Okay."

We talked a little longer. Then she went back to work.

I stayed on the bench.

A man came through the park with a dog. The dog had apparently discovered something of immense interest at the base of a tree. The man waited. After a while he took his phone out, looked at it, put it away, and continued waiting.

I thought about how much of my life I had spent in a state of readiness for something else. It wasn't that I regretted those years, exactly. A number of things I was enjoying now were things I wouldn't have been able to enjoy if I hadn't spent those years in the particular way I had.

But I had never known, when I was spending them, quite what they were going to be for.

I sent Simon a picture of the park.

He asked me if I had bought an academic gown yet.

I told him I hadn't.

He said not to bother coming back.

* * *

On the last evening of the conference, Elliot and I went to a small concert. Asha had given us the tickets because she had to get back to Edinburgh. There was a quartet playing in a church, and it was cold enough inside that several people kept their coats on.

During the second piece I looked over at Elliot. He was sitting with his eyes closed. I thought of Ruth and the photograph of the fish, and the box in Cambridge, and the years in which he'd had somebody beside him with whom he could go to a concert without having to arrange to go to a concert.

I was glad Asha had given us the tickets.

Afterward we walked toward the station.

"Did you like it?" he asked.

"Very much."

"Good."

He stopped outside a bookshop that had closed for the evening.

"You know, I've been thinking about leaving Northline."

"Have you?"

"Yes. I had rather decided on it before you came."

He looked at a book in the window.

"I think I might stay for a bit longer."

"Why?"

"Well, there are suddenly some rather interesting things to do."

He smiled.

"Will you come back?"

"To England?"

"Yes."

"I hope so."

"Good."

He got on his train, and I went back to the apartment.

There was an email from Peter waiting for me. Its subject was simply *Finch record*.

He had been given permission to inspect the source of the minutes Elliot had received. There was indeed a generated contribution in the system. But Peter had found something in the associated records that distinguished it from the incident at the conference.

A reviewer had been asked to verify the attribution.

The reviewer had marked the contribution as verified.

Peter had requested further information about who had performed the review.

I read his email again.

Then I took out my phone and sent Elliot a message.

*Are you still on the train?*

He replied a minute later.

*Yes. Why?*

I sat down at the little kitchen table.

For the first time since I had arrived in England, I wanted to be in Cambridge instead of London.
