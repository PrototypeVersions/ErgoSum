## Forty-three — A Very Inconvenient Result

The library opened on a Saturday in April.

There was a woman at the entrance giving people little maps of the building. A boy of about eight had taken three of them. He was trying to fit one inside another in a manner that would enable him to produce a map with more rooms on it.

I asked him if he needed any help.

"No," he said.

I went to find Nadia.

She was standing in the room with the table. There was a man sitting at the table reading a newspaper. At the other end of it there were two women looking at something on a computer. A little girl had put a number of colored pencils in the space between them.

"Well?" I said.

Nadia looked around the room.

"It's a little loud."

"Is it?"

"Yeah."

She smiled.

"I think we can do something about that."

We went outside.

There were people sitting under the trees. One of them was Dennis, who'd been responsible for establishing whether the truck could go somewhere other than where the truck was originally going to go.

"Hey," he said.

"Hey."

"Nice trees."

Nadia laughed.

We went over to him.

* * *

By this time we had obtained the results of the study.

The independent research team had identified a difficulty with the way we were proposing to interpret one of the measures. We had been asking people at various stages of the study to identify interests they'd acquired since entering it.

It was fairly easy to establish that a person had expressed an interest at the end of the study that they hadn't expressed at the beginning.

It was less easy to establish that they hadn't been interested in the thing at the beginning of the study.

And there was a further difficulty.

One of the systems asked people more questions about their interests than the others did. As a consequence, the people using that system had received considerably more practice in describing the kinds of things they were interested in.

"Whose system?" I asked when Celia told me about the difficulty.

"Ours."

"Oh."

We spent several days discussing it with the team. We retained the original analysis and made clear what it could and couldn't establish. There were also going to be some additional analyses, identified as such, based on interviews conducted in the same manner in all three groups.

At the end of those discussions I asked Celia whether she thought we had learned anything about the discovery of new interests.

"Yes," she said.

"Good."

"Just not exactly the thing we thought we were going to learn about it."

* * *

The results arrived on a Tuesday morning.

I went to the institute early. Fatima had arranged for us to meet at ten, but Peter and June were already there, and Beatrice was on the screen at the end of the table.

Peter had printed out the summary.

There were three or four things in it that I was very pleased to see. There were another three or four things I would have been very pleased to see if the study had been conducted by somebody I didn't know.

Continuance had done better than we had at enabling people to make progress toward their original goals. There was evidence that our system had helped some participants explore possibilities they might otherwise have overlooked, although the evidence was considerably less decisive than I'd hoped it would be. There were also participants who found the number of possibilities burdensome.

The simple search interface had been particularly useful to people who already knew quite a lot about the things they were looking for.

And there was a result that we hadn't anticipated.

People using all three interfaces had occasionally arrived at decisions they later wanted to reconsider. We had arranged for each of the interfaces to provide access to certain records of the process by which they'd arrived at those decisions. We wanted to establish whether the people could make effective use of the records when they reconsidered something.

June had done a great deal of work on that part of our system.

It was one of the things our system had done well.

But the people for whom it made the greatest difference were not necessarily the people who were most concerned about being influenced by the system. A number of them wanted the information because they'd been discussing their decisions with somebody else.

There was a woman who'd been considering taking a course. Her employer had asked her whether she'd looked at another course. She wanted to know whether she'd rejected the other course or whether she'd simply never seen it.

There was a man whose wife wanted to know why he was considering a particular job. He'd been trying to explain the various things he'd taken into consideration. He went back to the record with her.

Some of the people using the records wanted to find out what the system had done. Some wanted to find out what they'd done. Some wanted to have a conversation about the decision with somebody who hadn't been there when they made it.

I put the paper down.

"That's interesting."

"Yeah," June said.

She was smiling.

I picked the paper up again.

There was one result of which I was considerably less fond.

We had made it possible for people using our system to specify which information they wanted the system to retain. We'd made a number of the controls readily available, and we'd attempted to explain what would happen if people used them.

It appeared that we'd been much better at making the controls available to people who already understood the kinds of things the controls were for.

"Well," I said, "that's not good."

"No," Celia said.

There was a discussion about the limitations of the evidence. There were uncertainties arising from the people we hadn't been able to follow up with. Some of the differences between the groups were too small to support a confident conclusion.

By lunchtime we had a fairly good idea of the things we could say.

I was beginning to like the results.

* * *

Vincent called in the afternoon.

"Well," he said, "we're both going to have quite a difficult time producing a press release."

"Yeah."

He laughed.

"Do you know what I'd like to do?"

"What?"

"I'd like to make your records available in our system."

I looked at the report on the table.

"You mean the provenance stuff?"

"Yes."

"Okay."

"And I'd like to have some of your people help us work out how to make it useful to a person who doesn't particularly want to know how any of the software works."

"That sounds good."

"Would you be interested?"

"I would. But you ought to talk to June."

"Good. I'll talk to June."

He wanted to know whether we'd be willing to make use of some of the capabilities of his system in the next version of ours.

"Possibly," I said.

"Well, I'd be happy to have a discussion about it."

"Okay."

We talked about the report for another ten minutes.

Before we hung up, he asked if I'd been pleased with the result.

"Some of it."

"Good."

"How about you?"

"Some of it."

He laughed again.

"I suppose that's what we were going to have to say."

* * *

It was Celia who wrote most of the statement we put on the website.

She described the differences between the systems, the limits of the investigation and the things we'd learned from the people who'd taken part. She made clear that we'd found no single system that performed best on all the outcomes we considered.

I read the draft.

"Can we put something in it about the paper?" I asked.

"What about the paper?"

"Well, about the argument."

"Sure. What did the study establish about the argument?"

I thought for a moment.

"Nothing about whether it's valid."

"Right."

"But I think some of the results illustrate why the distinction we're making matters."

"Okay. Say that."

I wrote a little paragraph.

She read it.

"That's fine," she said.

I had wanted very much, for quite a long time, to conduct an experiment that would establish that a particular kind of institution ought to exist.

It turned out that we had conducted an experiment that gave us some fairly good reasons to change the institution that did exist.

I found myself hoping that we would have the opportunity to do it again.

* * *

The announcement attracted quite a bit of attention.

There were some articles about the effectiveness of Continuance's technology. There were some articles about the potential of the technology we'd developed at Open Question. There was a very good article by a woman in Toronto who had spent a considerable amount of time talking to the independent researchers.

And there was a headline about the dangers of using AI to broaden people's interests.

I sent that one to Astrid.

*Oh, good,* she wrote. *You've finally established that giving people things they don't want isn't always a good idea.*

I laughed.

Then she sent me another message.

*Seriously, I think this is very useful. When are you coming to Norway?*

I wrote back that I didn't know.

I had been invited to a number of places. There was a seminar in Berlin, a conference in Vancouver, and an invitation to spend part of the following year at a university in England.

I was pleased to have been invited to all of them.

I was also pleased not to be going anywhere that evening.

I took my bicycle out of the back of the house.

Nadia was sitting in the yard.

"Where are you going?" she asked.

"I don't know."

"All right."

"Want to come?"

She looked up at the sky.

"Yeah."

She went to get her bicycle.

* * *

We rode to a place near the lake and got something to eat.

There was a man sitting at a table beside ours with a little boy. The man had a notebook in front of him. The boy was drawing a picture on the back of a menu.

The man asked him what the picture was.

The boy turned the menu around.

"That's you," he said.

"Oh."

"And that's me."

"What's that?"

"That's the place we're going."

I looked at the picture. The place they were going appeared to be a building with a very large number of windows.

"Have we been there before?" the man asked.

"No."

"Okay."

He looked at the picture.

"Well, how do we get there?"

The boy got the pencil out again.

Nadia asked me if I wanted another drink.

"Sure."

She went to get one.

I sat there looking out toward the water.

For the first time in several years I had a fairly good idea of what I wanted my next philosophical paper to be about.

I took my phone out.

Then I put it back in my pocket.

There was no particular need to write the paper that evening.
