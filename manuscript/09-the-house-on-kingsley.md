## Nine — The House on Kingsley

Lena Ortiz called me while I was standing in a department store trying on a coat.

"Have you got your job?"

"Yes."

"Congratulations. What are you doing?"

"I'm looking at myself in a mirror."

"Could you do that somewhere else?"

The coat was dark blue and had cost considerably less on the website than it appeared to be about to cost in the store. I took it off.

"What's happened?"

"Alma's agreed to meet you. If you still want to meet her."

"Of course."

"She's going over to the house this afternoon. The owner gave her an hour to get the rest of her things."

I looked at the coat. I had liked it right up until that moment.

"Where is it?"

* * *

The house was on a narrow street off Kingsley Road. It had a low roof, a basketball hoop with no net, and a rosebush that had been left to develop its own ideas about the front porch. At the curb there was a little moving truck.

Alma Reyes was standing in the garage with a young man who was trying to put a dresser in the truck. She was wearing a blue cotton dress and running shoes.

Lena introduced us.

"Are you with the company?" Alma asked.

"I work for a company that did some research for them."

"Well, that's a long way around to yes."

"I suppose it is."

She gave me a look, then pointed at the other end of the dresser.

"You can get that side."

The dresser was heavier than it had any business being. The young man was Alma's nephew, Daniel. We managed to get the thing into the truck without dropping it on anybody. Then we went back for a little wooden table.

There were cardboard boxes in the living room, most of them with the names of things written on them in Spanish. One said CUADROS. Another said COCINA. On the wall above the place where the television had been was a little rectangle of paint much lighter than the paint around it.

"Everything from here?" Daniel asked.

"Not the curtains," Alma said.

"You want those?"

"No. They're theirs."

He started taking the boxes out.

I asked Alma how long she'd lived there.

"Twenty-three years."

"Did you like it?"

"Sometimes."

She looked around the room.

"The bathroom never did get warm."

She showed me a cupboard that wouldn't close properly. She opened it and shut it a few times, as though demonstrating a problem to a man from the hardware store.

"I told them about this in 2011," she said. "Now I don't have to tell anybody."

We went out to the porch.

A woman across the street was taking her trash cans in. When she saw Alma, she came over. The two of them talked for several minutes. They arranged to go to lunch together. The woman went home and came back with a bag of oranges.

Alma gave one to Lena and one to me.

"That's what you do when somebody gives you too many oranges," she said.

I put mine in my coat pocket. It was the old coat.

"Do you mind if I ask about the offer you received?" I said.

"No."

"What did you think when you first got it?"

"That they were going to make me leave."

"Even though it said you could accept or decline?"

"Well, yes."

She told me that she had read the letter, called the number at the bottom of it, and spent a long time trying to get the person on the telephone to tell her what would happen if she didn't take the money. Nobody had given her an answer she understood.

"Would you have accepted a larger amount?" I asked.

Lena gave me a look.

Alma considered the question.

"How much larger?"

"I don't know. Twelve thousand dollars."

She laughed.

"Is that what they're offering?"

"No."

"Then why are you asking?"

"I wanted to know what the difference would have made."

"It would've made eight thousand dollars' difference."

I liked her enormously at that moment, although I deserved it considerably less than I would have liked to.

She sat down on the porch step.

"Look," she said. "I wanted to stay here until my lease was up. Then I could go look for somewhere I wanted to live. They told me they needed to know right away. Why? I didn't need to know right away."

She looked across the street at the neighbor's house.

"Maybe I would have moved. Maybe I would have talked to that lady over there about getting a place together. We used to talk about that. We could've had a lot of fun."

She smiled at the thought.

"But then it all had to happen on a Wednesday."

Daniel came out with another box. Alma got up to help him. I stood there for a moment with the orange in my pocket.

Lena touched my arm.

"Come on, philosopher. There's a microwave."

* * *

The man who had been sent to collect the keys arrived at four. His name was Greg. He was younger than Daniel and had evidently been given very little information about what he was doing there.

Alma asked whether she would be getting her security deposit back.

"You'd have to talk to the office," he said.

"You're from the office."

"Well, I'm not from that part of the office."

He handed her a form.

Lena took it before Alma could sign it.

"What is this?"

"Just the move-out form."

"There's a release in it."

"I don't know about that."

Lena turned the paper around and pointed at a paragraph.

"That's a release."

Greg read the paragraph.

"Oh," he said.

He made a phone call. While he was on hold, Alma went to the garage and came back with a small clay pot. It contained the remains of a plant.

"You taking that?" Daniel asked.

"I don't know."

He took it from her and put it in the truck.

Greg got off the phone.

"They said you don't have to sign it."

"Good," Lena said.

"But I do need the keys."

Alma took out a key ring. There were three keys and a little silver shoe on it. She worked one of the keys loose with her thumbnail.

Greg held out his hand.

"Thank you, ma'am."

She gave it to him. Then she went back to the truck.

It was an exceedingly ordinary thing to watch. I don't know what I had expected. Some particular expression on her face that would enable me to understand the whole event, perhaps. Something that would have made it possible for me to go back to Northline and tell them what the event was like.

There hadn't been anything of the sort.

There had been a woman giving a key to a man.

* * *

On Monday morning I asked Miriam whether Northline could provide an independent account of the Garland work for use in the ongoing dispute. Not the data on individual participants, not anything that would violate their agreements. Just an explanation of what the estimates did and did not establish.

"That would be a legal matter," she said.

"I thought it might be."

"I can put the question to our counsel."

"Thank you."

She looked at me.

"Have you spoken to Ortiz again?"

"Yes."

"Adrian, you do understand that some of the work we do is confidential."

"I haven't told her anything confidential."

"Good."

She took off her glasses.

"There's a difference between helping someone and wanting to be the person who helps someone."

I didn't say anything.

"Don't promise her things on our behalf," she said.

"I haven't."

"Okay."

I went back to my office.

The annoying thing about the remark was that I couldn't simply classify it as one of the things people said when they didn't want you to do something. I had gone to see Alma in part because I had wanted to be able to say to myself that I was the sort of person who went to see Alma. I had also gone because Lena had asked me to go. And because I wanted to know what had happened. And because I had been rather taken with Lena the first time I met her.

There were a great many things going on in a person at once. If you had asked me to put them in order of importance, I would have made something up.

I sent Lena a message telling her what Miriam had said about the request.

*Thanks,* she wrote. *Daniel wants to know if you're available next time he moves. I told him you're very expensive.*

I laughed.

Then I sent her another message.

*Tell Alma thank you for the orange.*

* * *

Nadia came over for dinner the evening before I left for London. I'd been planning to cook something fairly ambitious until she found out what I was planning to cook.

"Do you know how to make that?" she asked.

"I've watched somebody do it on the internet."

"Do you own the thing he was doing it in?"

"No."

"Let's make pasta."

She made the sauce. I was entrusted with the water.

We ate at the table by the window. I told her about Alma and the house. Nadia had been to enough houses that were about to be torn down to know exactly what I meant about the rectangle of lighter paint on the wall.

"The pictures are always the last thing," she said.

After dinner I played her a little of the thing we had played at Walter's. She asked me to do it again. Then she went over to the window and looked out at the street while I played.

When I finished, she turned around.

"That's lovely."

"Well, there are supposed to be about four other people doing things."

"I like it like that."

I put the guitar down.

She came over and kissed me.

I had imagined a number of ways in which that might happen. In all of them I had said something beforehand. Generally something very good.

In the actual event I said nothing at all.

Later she helped me pack. She took three books out of my bag and put one back in.

"You're going for ten days."

"I know."

"Are you actually going to read all these?"

"No."

"Then why are you taking them?"

"Because I might read any one of them."

She laughed.

I had bought the blue coat at last. She made me put it on over the clothes I was wearing, considered the result, and turned down the collar.

"There," she said.

"Do I look like somebody who should be going to Cambridge?"

"You look like somebody who bought a coat."

I kissed her.

At the door she gave me the address of the park in London. She had written it on the back of an envelope.

"Go if you feel like it," she said.

I put the envelope in my pocket.

Then she left, and I went around the apartment doing all the little things one does before leaving a place. I took out the trash. I unplugged the amplifier. I watered the plant I had been trying, with mixed success, to keep alive.

At midnight I got into bed.

It was a very odd thing. For years I had wanted to be on my way somewhere else. Now that I was, I found that I was also looking forward to coming back.
