## Twenty — The Unnecessary Thing

On the Saturday after we got back from New Mexico, Nadia took me to look at the library site.

I left my phone in the car. I had announced that I was going to leave my phone in the car, and having done so I was slightly irritated by how much I wanted credit for it.

Nadia had arranged to meet a man named Dennis, who was responsible for certain practical matters concerning the site. He was waiting for us beside a fence.

There was very little to see. A patch of dirt, a parking lot, the municipal water office and three trees.

Dennis pointed at one of the trees.

"That one's the problem."

"Why?" I asked.

"Because the truck needs to come through here."

Nadia took a drawing out of her bag.

"What if the truck comes through here?"

Dennis studied the drawing.

"How big a truck?"

"The truck you just told me about."

He looked at it again.

They went on like this for a while. I walked around the site. There was a little bird hopping along the fence. Beyond the parking lot I could see a laundromat and a shop selling tires.

A woman came out of the laundromat carrying a basket of clothes. A little girl followed her. The girl was reading a book. She managed to read it while walking across the parking lot, which was something I'd been quite good at doing as a child and had been told not to do for a number of perfectly good reasons.

I went back to Nadia.

"How's it going?"

"Pretty well."

Dennis had established that there was a different place for the truck to go. He wasn't enthusiastic about the different place, but he was willing to investigate it.

After he'd left, Nadia and I stood by the fence.

"Do you know what I really want?" she asked.

"What?"

"I want a room with a big table in it."

"Okay."

"Just a room with a big table. People can come in and sit down and make something, or talk about something, or spread a bunch of stuff out that they don't have room to spread out at home."

I looked at the drawing.

"Is there one?"

"Not at the moment."

"Why not?"

"Well, every room has to have a function."

"That seems like a pretty good function."

"Yeah. Try putting it in a budget."

She folded up the drawing.

I told her about Malcolm and the meeting we'd had in New Mexico. I told her I was increasingly convinced that we ought to have a physical space for some of the work we were going to do, though I didn't want it to be necessary for everybody to live in the same city.

"Do you know what I'd like the place to look like?" I asked.

"Let me guess."

"What?"

"A room with a big table."

I laughed.

"Yeah."

She smiled.

"Well, don't make me design it for free."

* * *

Beatrice came to Dallas in March.

She had been working on the mathematics of our counter game. The original result had turned out to be a special case of something much more interesting, and she wanted to spend a few days with me going through some of the experiments we had been running.

I met her at the airport.

"How many problems are we working on now?" she asked.

"About nine."

"How many were we working on when I left my house?"

"About seven."

"Okay. Stop that."

She was a small, energetic woman who carried a black backpack that appeared to contain enough mathematics to get her through a fairly lengthy natural disaster.

We worked at my kitchen table for three days. Simon came over one evening with dinner and asked us whether we'd discovered anything.

"Yes," Beatrice said.

"What?"

"That your friend doesn't eat lunch."

Simon looked at me.

"Well, I've been telling you that for years."

We'd begun an experiment in which the mathematical systems were given a small collection of results and asked to investigate what could be obtained from them. Beatrice had put a great deal of thought into what it would mean to give the systems freedom in the course of the investigation. We didn't want them to have unlimited computing resources. We didn't want them to be able to spend all their time on an extremely easy problem and then declare themselves astonishingly successful. On the other hand, we didn't want to give them so many instructions that the only thing they could do was carry out our research program.

One afternoon a system asked for permission to abandon a line of inquiry.

It wasn't the first time something of the sort had happened. The systems were capable of producing quite ordinary statements about their plans and the difficulties they'd encountered. I had expected them to do it.

This particular statement interested me because it contained a suggestion about what the system would do instead.

It wanted to construct examples of a particular mathematical object and give them to another system. It proposed that the other system be asked to infer the rules by which the objects had been constructed, without being given those rules.

"That's quite a good experiment," I said.

"Yes," Beatrice said.

"Should we let it do it?"

"Have you got the money?"

"Yeah."

"Then let's see what happens."

We allowed it a portion of the remaining computing budget.

For the next two days we followed the exchange. There were some interesting results, some rather uninteresting results, and one example on which the systems repeatedly disagreed.

Then the system that had proposed the experiment produced a request.

It wanted us to preserve the record of the exchange.

"Well, we do that anyway," Beatrice said.

"Yeah."

"Tell it that."

I did.

The system returned a short explanation. It said that it wanted the record to be available to subsequent researchers, since it had enjoyed the investigation and would like to see the work continued.

I read the sentence again.

Beatrice looked over my shoulder.

"Oh, dear," she said.

"Yeah."

"What are you going to do with that?"

"I don't know."

We sat for a while.

There was nothing remarkable about the system's ability to produce the sentence. It had been trained on an enormous quantity of material produced by people who wrote sentences of that sort. We had asked it to describe the progress of its research, and it had done so in the manner of a researcher.

Nonetheless, I was interested in a question that was not quite the question of whether we ought to believe it.

"Suppose it hadn't said it enjoyed the investigation," I said. "Suppose it had just asked us to keep the record."

"Okay."

"Would we have any less reason to preserve it?"

"No."

"Then why is that sentence bothering me?"

Beatrice considered this.

"Because you've spent a good deal of your life wanting somebody to keep a record of an investigation."

I laughed.

"Probably."

"Look. I don't think we should throw the record away. I also don't think we should start congratulating ourselves on having made a friend."

"Agreed."

She went back to the other end of the table.

A little later I opened a notebook and wrote the word *enjoyment* at the top of a page.

I didn't write anything else.

* * *

On the evening before Beatrice left, the four of us went out to dinner. She hadn't met Nadia before. I had been looking forward to their meeting and was slightly disappointed by how quickly they established that they could have a very good conversation without me.

Simon and I ordered drinks.

"Have you talked to Malcolm?" he asked.

"Yesterday."

"Anything new?"

"He's happy with the proposal in principle. He wants his lawyer to look at the arrangements."

"Good."

"And he wants us to come back out there in April."

"Fine."

Beatrice looked over at us.

"By the way," she said, "you've got to stop saying you're going to give these systems freedom."

"Why?"

"Because it's going to mean something different to each person you say it to."

"Well, what should I say?"

"Say they're being allowed to choose what to investigate next. That's what you're actually doing."

"Okay."

Nadia put her glass down.

"Can I ask something?"

"Sure."

"If you could build a thing that had a really good time doing mathematics, would you?"

I thought about it.

"Yes, probably."

"Even if it wasn't very good at mathematics?"

Simon laughed.

I thought about it some more.

"That's a different question."

"Well, it's the one I asked."

There were a number of things I could have said. I could have explained that we didn't know whether such a thing could be built, or that we didn't know what would count as evidence of having built it. Both were important.

But neither was an answer to her question.

"Yeah," I said. "I think I would."

She picked up her glass.

"Good."

We went on to talk about other things. Beatrice told us about her neighbor in Albuquerque, who had attempted to grow a tree in a bathtub. Simon had a number of questions about the bathtub.

I kept thinking about Nadia's question.

I realized that in practically all of the conversations I'd had about what an artificial system might want, there had been an assumption, usually an unspoken one, that we would be interested in its wants because of the things they might cause it to do.

We would want it to want the right things so that we could get it to do the right things.

I wondered what would happen to the conversation if we took away the part about getting it to do something for us.

* * *

The next day I drove Beatrice to the airport. When I got home there was a message from Lena Ortiz.

Alma's dispute had finally been settled. There was a payment, the return of her deposit, and a written acknowledgment that she had not agreed to the relocation offer. The agreement contained no determination of whether Northline's research had contributed to what had happened.

I called Lena.

"Is Alma happy with it?"

"She signed it."

"Okay."

"She wants to be done with it."

"Good."

"She found a new place, by the way. A little duplex."

"Where?"

"Not far from her old house. She's going to have her neighbor over for dinner."

I smiled.

"That's wonderful."

"Yeah."

Lena told me that she'd been working on another matter involving a system used to screen applications for assistance. She wondered if I knew anybody who could explain the evaluation report.

"I might," I said.

"Good. I'll send you the public documents."

We talked a little longer.

Then I went into the kitchen, took out my notebook, and looked at the page with the word *enjoyment* on it.

I wrote another word underneath.

*Opportunity.*

At three that afternoon I met with Miriam.

She wanted an answer to the revised offer. I had been hoping to give her one after the arrangements with Malcolm had been finalized, but the arrangements with Malcolm could take months, and I had begun to appreciate that it wasn't particularly fair to ask Miriam to organize a research program around somebody who wasn't sure he wanted to be part of it.

I told her I wouldn't take the new position.

She nodded.

"Are you leaving?"

"I think I will. Not immediately. I'd like to finish the things I've been working on."

"How long?"

"Three months?"

"All right."

I had prepared a number of arguments in defense of my decision. I didn't have to give any of them.

Miriam walked to the window.

"I think you're going to find that it costs a great deal of money to do the things you want to do," she said.

"Probably."

"And that people have a very strong interest in what happens to the money they give you."

"Yeah."

She turned around.

"Don't be under the impression that you'll be free of those problems."

"I won't."

She looked at me for a while.

"Would you let me come and see what you're doing sometime?"

"Of course."

"Good."

There was a silence.

"Adrian, you are a very good researcher."

I hadn't expected her to say that. I didn't know how much I'd wanted her to say it until she said it.

"Thank you."

She picked up her glasses.

"Well, you've got three months. Go and do some research."

I went back to my office.

The photograph of the chairs had arrived that morning. I had put it on the wall beside the window.

I stood there looking at it.

Then I sat down at my desk and wrote to Malcolm.

I told him there was a second research program I wanted to discuss, one that I hadn't included in the proposal. I told him I didn't know how much of it would be feasible. I told him that a good deal of the initial work would probably involve establishing what the questions were.

At the end of the email I asked him something that I had never imagined asking a man who was considering giving me money.

Would he be interested in funding research that might ultimately establish that there were certain things we ought to build, even if they weren't of any use to us?

I sent the message.

Then I went home and played the guitar.
