## Twenty-three — The First Day

At seven on the morning of the first day of the institute, a man named Julio was on his hands and knees in the kitchen trying to get water to come out of a tap.

"Are you the owner?" he asked.

"No. We rent the building."

"Are you the boss?"

"Sort of."

"Well, have you got a bucket?"

I went to look for a bucket.

It was July. We had moved into the building in the Design District two weeks earlier, and in the intervening time had discovered a number of things about the building that had not been apparent to us when nobody was trying to use it. The light switch in one of the offices controlled a light in another office. There was a little square window at the back that could be opened but apparently not closed. And somebody, at some point in the history of the building, had put a perfectly good kitchen sink in a place where there was no water.

I found the bucket.

"That's a trash can," Julio said.

"Will it work?"

"Yeah."

I went back into the main room.

There were six desks, a couch, four lamps that the previous occupant had left behind, and an exceptionally large wooden table. We'd bought the table secondhand from a church. It was not in especially good condition, which meant that nobody was going to have to be the first person to do something to it.

I sat down at one end.

For a few minutes there was nobody in the room but me.

I thought of all the buildings in which I'd been given a place to sit over the years. There had been beautiful buildings and terrible buildings, rooms in which I'd accomplished a great deal and rooms in which I'd spent entire afternoons trying to give the impression that I had accomplished a great deal. There had been a room with no window in which I'd been very happy. There had been a room with three windows in which I'd been miserable.

I looked at the chairs around the table.

I had hoped, when I was younger, that I'd have a room of my own at some point in my life.

This was better.

Then Simon arrived with a box of doughnuts and told me that I was sitting in the wrong chair.

* * *

There were five of us to begin with.

Beatrice would be coming to Dallas for a few days every month. Peter had decided to join us after arranging things with Northline and his family. We had hired a programmer named June Han, who had spent the preceding three years developing software for the analysis of satellite images, and a woman named Fatima Nasser, who would be responsible for running the place and had already prevented us from making a number of expensive mistakes.

Fatima had a rule about meetings. If the person who called the meeting couldn't say what it was supposed to accomplish, the meeting didn't occur.

I had been looking forward to being the boss of an organization until I encountered this rule.

At ten we sat around the table.

I had prepared a few remarks. I said something about the kinds of questions we were going to investigate, and the importance of being able to establish when our work hadn't gone well. I thanked Simon. I thanked Malcolm, who was joining us on the screen. I said that I hoped it would be possible for us to produce things that none of us would have produced if we'd gone on working separately.

Then I said I thought we should have a conversation about the kinds of projects we wanted to pursue.

"What do you want to work on?" I asked June.

"Well, I've got something I'd like to finish."

"What's that?"

She told us about a piece of software she'd begun writing during her previous job. It was a tool for recording the provenance of computer-generated claims. She'd written a new version of it on her own time, from scratch, and wanted to make it possible for somebody examining the output of an experiment to follow the history of the individual claims without having to reconstruct the history from several thousand pages of logs.

It sounded extremely useful.

"How long would it take?" I asked.

"I don't know. A couple of months?"

"Great."

She looked at me.

"You're not going to tell me what else I should make it do?"

"I've got a few ideas."

"Of course."

She smiled.

Peter wanted to continue the work we'd begun at Northline on the evaluation of predictions. Beatrice wanted to know whether there was a way of enabling the systems to recognize that a mathematical problem they were investigating was related to a problem someone else had investigated in a completely different form.

We decided that we would spend the first few weeks establishing a common set of procedures for documenting the work. We would also allow ourselves time to explore things for which there wasn't yet a procedure.

At eleven thirty Fatima announced that she was going to take us all out for lunch.

"Who's paying?" Simon asked.

"The institute."

"Oh," I said.

Everybody laughed.

I was going to have to get used to this.

* * *

My last day at Northline had been the previous Friday.

There had been a cake, and a card on which Peter had written that he hoped we'd get the opportunity to work together again someday. Miriam had given me a book about the history of public health. Inside the front cover she'd written something that I didn't read until I was home.

*Adrian — if you ever discover something I don't want to know, please tell me. M.*

The review of Northline's work for Vincent's company was going to continue after my departure. Some of the findings had already been communicated to the people affected by them, and there were plans to release a public account of the relevant failures without exposing individual records. I didn't know what the account would eventually look like.

On the way out of the building I passed the desk where Helen had worked. There was a man sitting there whom I didn't know. He was on the telephone, explaining to somebody that he would have to look into something.

I hoped he'd look into it.

I took the elevator downstairs and carried the plant from my office to the car.

The plant was now on a windowsill at the institute. June had looked at it on the first day and told me what kind of plant it was.

I had owned it for eight months.

* * *

Nadia came over that afternoon with a little package.

It was a sign made from a piece of wood. It had a question mark on it.

"That's all?" I said.

"That's all."

"It's beautiful."

"I know. I made it."

We put it on the wall by the entrance.

She had news about the library. The council had approved the revised project. She'd called me the previous evening while I was in the middle of an exceptionally uninteresting conversation about insurance, and I'd gone outside to talk to her.

There would be a library.

There would even be a room with a big table, though she wasn't entirely satisfied with the location of the electrical outlets.

"Come and see the plans," she said.

"When?"

"Tonight."

"Okay."

She looked around the main room.

"How's it feel?"

"Wonderful."

"Good."

She took my hand.

"Can I ask you something?"

"Sure."

"Can we go somewhere this summer?"

"Of course."

"Somewhere you don't have a conference."

"Yeah."

"And somebody doesn't have a book they want to show you."

"Okay."

"And we don't have to look at any buildings I worked on."

I laughed.

"Where do you want to go?"

"I don't know. Somewhere with water."

We decided we'd look at a map that evening.

She went back to her office. I watched her through the window until she got into her car.

Then I went to look for Fatima.

I wanted to find out how vacations worked.

* * *

The first visitors to the institute were not mathematicians or philosophers. They were Ray, Walter, Judy and Walter's granddaughter, who had been informed that there was going to be a party on Friday.

I had invited practically everybody I knew. There were people from Northline, people from the workshop, some of Nadia's friends, Lena, Helen and Leah, and a number of people Simon seemed to know from entirely different periods of his life.

Malcolm came from New Mexico.

He stood in the doorway for a little while looking at the room. Simon went over to him.

"Well," Simon said, "there's the building."

Malcolm laughed.

"Yeah."

"Do you want to see the kitchen?"

"Sure."

I had never seen Simon so pleased to be able to demonstrate a functioning tap.

We played music that evening. Curtis from the furniture workshop had brought a little amplifier, and Walter had brought a bass. I played Westbound, and then we played some other things. At one point a man I didn't know came over and asked whether he could play my guitar.

"Sure."

He was very good. He played the piece differently from the way I played it. For a while I had the rather enjoyable experience of sitting in a room full of people I liked, listening to something I'd started and no longer had to do anything about.

I went outside.

It was still warm. The sky above the buildings was dark blue. I could hear the music through the open door.

Helen was standing by the wall with a paper cup.

"Well," she said, "this is nice."

"Yeah."

"Do you remember that time you got a phone call in a guitar shop?"

"I do."

She looked in through the doorway.

"You were very suspicious."

"Well, it was a weird phone call."

"It was a very weird phone call."

We laughed.

She told me about the review of the candidate records. Several people had been contacted and given an opportunity to have their applications reconsidered. Some hadn't responded. Others had found jobs elsewhere. The young man in New Mexico had eventually been located through a university address. He'd begun a doctoral program.

"Does he know what happened?" I asked.

"Yes. He asked for a copy of his file."

"Good."

She took a drink.

"I'm teaching a lesson next week about things that never happened."

"Like what?"

"Well, we have some accounts of events that people have written on the basis of accounts of events written by other people. I'm going to have the kids try to find the first account."

"That's a good lesson."

"I hope so."

We stood there for a while.

Then she looked at me.

"You're going to have to go in there at some point. There's a cake."

I went in.

The cake had a question mark on it. It was a very large question mark, and everybody had a different opinion about which part of it I ought to cut first.

* * *

On Monday morning Beatrice showed me something on her computer.

A person using the name Peregrine had submitted a comment on the public record of our mathematical experiments.

We received a certain number of comments. Most were requests for clarification. A few were suggestions for additional experiments. This one identified a connection between one of the systems' unsuccessful conjectures and a problem Beatrice had encountered twenty years earlier.

"Is it a good connection?" I asked.

"Yes. It's a very good connection."

"Do you know who it is?"

"No."

She showed me the comment. It contained a brief argument and a reference to an unpublished set of lecture notes.

"I have those notes," she said.

"Where?"

"In a box in my house."

"Are they on the internet?"

"Parts of them. I don't think that part is."

"Maybe somebody else who took the course."

"Maybe."

She looked at me.

"Adrian, I wrote the notes."

"Oh."

She scrolled down.

There was a question at the bottom of the comment.

*Would you be interested in investigating the problem together?*

Beatrice had already written a reply.

*Very much.*
