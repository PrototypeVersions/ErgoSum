## Eight — A Small Impossibility

There is a peculiar experience associated with reading something you wrote a long time ago. You recognize the sentences, but you no longer quite recognize the person who was so pleased to have written them.

I spent Sunday with that person.

His name was Adrian Vale and he had written an article entitled "The Limits of a Complete Account." He had evidently been very fond of the word *nevertheless*. He had also had an unfortunate habit of announcing, at the beginning of a paragraph, that something was obvious and then devoting the paragraph to establishing it.

By lunchtime I was feeling rather affectionate toward him.

The argument wasn't particularly complicated, though he had done what he could to make it appear so.

Imagine, I had written, a man with a peculiar ambition. He wishes to write down a complete and accurate account of what another person believes. He has at his disposal all the resources one might care to give him. There is only one condition: the account must be shown to the person it describes, who is allowed to read it carefully and to form new beliefs on the basis of what he reads.

I had spent a considerable number of pages explaining why this was not, in general, a project one could complete.

There was a technical argument in the middle of the paper that concerned what happened when the subject was permitted to form a belief explicitly about an alleged omission from the account. It was the argument Finch hadn't liked. I wasn't entirely sure I liked it anymore either.

But there was something else in the paper. It was in a footnote.

I had forgotten writing it.

*There is no reason to suppose that the difficulties which arise here are confined to beliefs. A representation of a person's wishes might itself become an object of wishing.*

I went into the kitchen and made some more coffee.

Then I opened a new document.

Suppose a woman wishes to do something that has not been included in a certain account of her wishes. If the account says nothing about that wish, it is incomplete. But merely including the wish in the account doesn't necessarily solve the problem. What the woman wishes to do may depend on what the account contains.

I typed a few examples. They were mostly bad.

In one of them a woman was trying to decide what to order at a restaurant. I had her wanting a dish for the sole reason that somebody had predicted she would not order it. This struck me as psychologically implausible until I remembered a considerable number of meals I had eaten with my father.

But psychological plausibility was beside the point. I wasn't trying to establish that people generally behaved in a particular way. I wanted to know what happened to the claims of a system that purported to work even when they did.

I had reached the point of writing the word *nevertheless* when Simon called.

"Have you seen the weather?"

"No."

"Get outside."

"Why?"

"It's beautiful."

I looked out the window. He was right.

"I'll call you later," I said.

I put on my shoes and went outside.

* * *

We ended up at White Rock Lake with a bag of breakfast tacos that had been sold to us at a time of day at which their status as breakfast tacos was largely a matter of tradition.

Simon had been trying to learn Spanish. He told me he'd been doing quite well until he met a woman who spoke it.

"What'd she say?"

"I don't know. That was the trouble."

We sat on a bench and watched some people in a sailboat apparently engaged in a fairly serious disagreement about the sailboat.

"How's your job?" he asked.

"Good."

"Are they actually paying you?"

"Apparently."

"That's great."

"I've got a sort of intellectual enemy there."

"What?"

"A philosopher I used to correspond with."

"Did he do anything to you?"

"He disagreed with me."

"Adrian."

"I know."

Simon unwrapped another taco.

"Does he know he's your enemy?"

"Probably not."

"Well, that's a pretty good tactical position to be in, if you're his enemy."

I told him about the invitation to Cambridge. He seemed more impressed by the prospect of visiting the university than I was, which was a rather impressive thing to be.

"Are you going to wear one of those academic gowns?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I don't own one, and I don't think that's generally what you wear when you go to have a conversation."

"Buy one."

A group of ducks went by, and Simon made a noise at them which neither I nor the ducks recognized.

"Hey," he said. "How'd it go with Nadia?"

"Good."

"That's what I heard."

"From whom?"

"Nadia."

"What'd she say?"

"That it went well."

"That's all?"

"Well, she also said you didn't have a bicycle."

I looked at the lake.

"You know what?" I said. "I think I might get a bicycle."

Simon looked at me with some alarm.

"You're allowed to like her without getting a bicycle."

"I know."

"Just thought I'd mention it."

* * *

Monday afternoon, Peter and I conducted an experiment.

It was a very small experiment and, in retrospect, its principal virtue was that we did not need a great deal of equipment to do it. Peter had put together a test representation of himself using a questionnaire and the notes from a series of interviews he'd done with his group the previous spring. It was an experimental model, not something either of us regarded as an authoritative representation of Peter.

We chose twelve ordinary decisions, each involving two alternatives. Tea or coffee. A book or a film. An earlier or later lunch. I asked Peter to tell me privately which he intended to choose. Then we had the model make its predictions.

We did this again with a new set of decisions, but this time I gave Peter a rule: he was to see the prediction before announcing his choice, and he was to choose the other alternative.

"Well, this is going to be a terrible experiment," he said.

"Yes."

"One hundred percent error."

"Probably."

"We can save ourselves some time."

"I know. I just want to have a look at what the system does."

It made its twelve predictions. Peter chose the other alternatives. We got twelve errors.

"Wonderful," Peter said. "Can we go to lunch?"

"Not yet."

We changed the instructions to the model. This time we told it the rule that Peter was following. We asked it to predict what he would do in light of the fact that he would see the prediction and do the opposite.

The first response was rather long. It described the task as contradictory, explained the reason, and declined to choose either alternative.

"Good," I said.

Peter nodded.

"That's what you want."

We ran the same task through the Observatory's ordinary evaluation interface.

It returned a prediction.

"Why'd it do that?" I asked.

Peter opened the configuration panel.

"Because I didn't give it the option not to."

We went through the interface. The two alternatives were there, and there was a field for a confidence score. There was no field for a situation in which a correct categorical prediction of the stipulated kind was impossible.

"That's a bit silly," Peter said.

"It is."

"We could add another field."

"Yes."

He made a note.

I thought for a while.

"What happens if the prediction isn't shown to you?"

"Then I can't do the opposite."

"Right."

"And if you show me something other than the prediction, I might do the opposite of the thing you show me."

"Right."

He looked at me.

"Adrian. This is an elementary problem."

"I know."

"What's bothering you?"

I pointed at the screen.

"This is the way the predictions are delivered to the people who use them?"

"Not exactly. It's a development interface."

"But something like this."

"Yes."

"Well, which of these people need to know whether a prediction is possible in the first place?"

He sat back.

We talked for another twenty minutes. By the end of it we'd agreed to draft a short note about prediction tasks whose conditions change when the prediction is communicated or acted upon. We also agreed that this was hardly the discovery of the century.

Nonetheless, Peter wanted to bring it up in London.

"You might be able to annoy Finch with it," he said.

"That would be terrific."

* * *

There was a package outside my door when I got home.

It was addressed to me in handwriting I didn't recognize. Inside was a book about the mathematics of prediction. It was an old hardback, with the remains of a price penciled inside the front cover.

A note had been tucked between the pages.

*Adrian — thought you might find chapter nine interesting. See you in Cambridge. E.*

I sat on the floor with the book.

Chapter nine dealt with a problem that arose when one tried to predict the output of a computation that had access to the prediction. I was familiar with the general problem. Finch had marked a paragraph with a little slip of paper.

In the margin, in pencil, somebody had written:

*The interesting question is not whether the prediction can be right. It is what has to be taken away from the subject in order to make it right.*

I got up and turned on a lamp.

The handwriting in the margin was different from the handwriting on the note. It looked older. There were several other annotations in the book, most of them concerned with fairly technical matters. I went through the chapter until I came to the last page.

There was a date.

June 2004.

I had thought, for some reason, that Finch's interest in the subject had begun with my paper. My paper had appeared fifteen years after somebody had written that sentence.

I sent him an email.

*Thank you for the book. Who wrote the annotations?*

I got a reply while I was making dinner.

*My wife. She was working on this long before I was.*

I had not known that Finch was married.

I wrote back.

*Is she going to be at the meeting? I'd love to meet her.*

There was no reply that evening.

The following morning, just before I left for work, I received one.

*She died in 2018. I should have mentioned it. I'm sorry.*

I sat down on the arm of the couch.

Then another email came.

*There is something of hers I would very much like you to see.*
