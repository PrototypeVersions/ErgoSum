# ERGO SUM

## One — The Interview

The first time I was asked to explain something I hadn't done, I was sitting in a guitar shop on Greenville Avenue with a hole in my sock.

I knew about the hole because the man fixing my guitar had dropped a screw, and when I bent down to help him look for it, my jeans rode up. It was a fairly impressive hole. There was virtually no sock left between my big toe and the general public.

"You find it?" he said.

"No."

"Don't worry about it. I've got a hundred of them."

He was seventy or so and had the beautiful gray hair of an irresponsible person who had somehow made it to seventy. His name was Ray. He had once told me that he had played with a famous blues singer, although the dates had been difficult to reconcile with the singer's death. I had learned not to press the matter. The important thing was that Ray could make a guitar sound the way you had imagined it would sound when you bought it.

"Play something," he said, handing it back to me.

I played four chords, each of which I liked considerably better than the one before it, and then a fifth chord that ruined everything.

"Again," Ray said.

I played the four chords.

"There you go."

My phone rang.

I had been waiting for a call from a university in Oregon. The previous Thursday, a woman on a computer screen had asked me to describe the relationship between my research and the needs of an undergraduate curriculum. I had told her that I thought undergraduates ought to spend more time having encounters with ideas they could not immediately make use of.

She had smiled. There had been five other people on the screen, and none of them had smiled. Since then I had developed about fourteen superior answers to her question.

"Adrian Vale?" a woman said.

"Speaking."

"This is Helen Park. Is this a good time?"

"For what?"

"Do you have a minute to discuss your decision?"

I put the guitar in its stand.

"Which decision?"

"The Northline appointment."

I looked at Ray. He was cleaning his glasses with a little square of blue cloth.

"I'm sorry," I said. "I don't know what that is."

There was a pause. Not a bad-connection pause. The kind in which you could practically hear somebody looking at a piece of paper.

"You met with our director on Tuesday."

"No, I didn't."

"At the Adolphus."

"I haven't been to the Adolphus in years."

"Mr. Vale, you're a philosopher?"

"On good days."

"And you worked on machine perception?"

"Yes."

"Then I'm speaking to the right person."

"That depends on what you want."

Another pause.

"I'm going to send you something," she said. "Would you read it and call me back?"

I gave her my email address, although she already had it. When we hung up, Ray had found the screw. It had stuck to the magnet on one of his tools.

"Job?" he asked.

"Possibly someone else's."

"Those are the good ones."

He charged me twenty dollars less than he had originally quoted. Outside, the November afternoon was doing its peculiar Dallas impersonation of early September. A man in a football jersey was trying to coax a tiny white dog into a parked truck. The dog had decided to devote its life to other things.

I carried my guitar to the car and opened the email.

The attachment was a PDF, nine pages long. At the top of the first page was a photograph of the front of the Adolphus, one of those institutional photographs in which a perfectly ordinary street has been made to look like the capital of a minor empire.

**NORTHLINE — EXTERNAL APPOINTMENTS**

**Candidate: Adrian Vale**

**Interview record 771-B**

There was my name, spelled correctly. Below it was a short biographical summary that had evidently been assembled from material anyone could have found on the internet. It got my academic work mostly right, though it credited me with a book I had not written and described my time in a research lab as a professorship. I liked the professorship.

The interview had allegedly lasted fifty-three minutes.

I scrolled.

**DIRECTOR:** What do you regard as the central problem with a system capable of predicting human decisions?

**VALE:** I suppose it depends on what you mean by capable.

**DIRECTOR:** Suppose it gets them right.

**VALE:** All of them?

**DIRECTOR:** Enough of them to matter.

**VALE:** Then I would be interested in the ones it gets wrong.

That was something I might have said. It was not especially original, but the evasiveness was right.

The next few exchanges were about prediction, error, and the rather embarrassing fact that people had begun talking about the arrival of intelligent machines without agreeing on what intelligence was. I had heard versions of these conversations for years.

On page five it got strange.

**DIRECTOR:** Do you think you would enjoy working here?

**VALE:** I think I would enjoy working somewhere.

**DIRECTOR:** That's not quite what I asked.

**VALE:** No. But it's the answer to a number of questions you haven't asked.

I put the phone down. Through the windshield, I could see the white dog standing on the pavement. Its owner was now sitting on the running board of his truck. They seemed to have reached some kind of accommodation.

I picked the phone up again.

The interview ended with an offer. The salary was sufficiently absurd that I went back and counted the digits. There was a research budget, no teaching obligation, and a provision for pursuing independent work. I had spent years trying to explain to people why a position of precisely this sort ought to exist.

In the transcript, I turned it down.

I read the last exchange three times.

**DIRECTOR:** You don't have to decide today.

**VALE:** I think I do.

**DIRECTOR:** Why?

**VALE:** Because I don't know what happens to the people who say no.

Below this was the word **DECLINED**.

I called Helen back.

"Where did you get this?"

"From the appointments office."

"Who wrote it?"

"It was generated automatically."

"From a recording?"

"No."

"Then it isn't an interview record."

"No," she said. "It isn't."

I waited for the rest of the sentence.

"Can you come downtown tomorrow morning?"

"What is Northline?"

"An institute."

"An institute of what?"

"That's a surprisingly difficult question to answer."

"Well, give it a shot."

She laughed, very briefly. I hadn't expected that.

"We're a private research organization working with several public institutions. Among other things."

"What other things?"

"I'd rather explain in person. There's a meeting at nine. I'll leave your name at the desk."

"With your director?"

"Yes."

"Is he the person I'm supposed to have met?"

"She's the person."

"Okay."

"And Adrian?"

"Yeah?"

"Please don't send the attachment to anyone."

I looked at the file again.

"You just sent it to me."

"I know."

"Am I not supposed to have it?"

"No," she said. "You are."

She hung up.

For a while I sat in the car listening to the air conditioner. Then I went back into the guitar shop.

Ray looked up.

"Don't tell me."

"Do you remember what I played?"

"Sure."

"Could you play it?"

He picked up a guitar from behind the counter and played something that was not exactly what I had played, but was rather better. He changed the bass note on the third chord. He did something with his little finger that I could not have done in my present evolutionary condition.

"Like that," he said.

"What would you have played after it?"

He played a fifth chord.

It was beautiful.

"Yeah," I said. "That's what I was looking for."

* * *

My apartment was in an old building east of downtown, where the windows in the living room didn't quite close. In the summer the air conditioner made an extraordinary effort to cool a significant portion of East Dallas. In the winter I wore sweaters indoors. I liked the apartment. It had three windows, wooden floors, and a pecan tree close enough to one of the windows that squirrels occasionally stopped by to look at me.

At six, my friend Simon came over with two hamburgers and an argument.

He was an electrical engineer by training who had made enough money doing things with warehouses to spend his afternoons explaining why other people were doing the wrong things with warehouses. We'd known each other for eleven years. He had a habit of answering the question he wished you had asked rather than the one you actually asked. This was irritating in ordinary life but made him exceptionally good company.

I told him that someone had offered me a job.

"Great," he said.

"I haven't been offered it yet."

"Less great."

"But apparently I've already turned it down."

He took another bite of his hamburger.

"How much?"

I told him.

"Well, tell them you've reconsidered."

"Simon."

"What?"

"It doesn't bother you that they seem to have invented the interview?"

"I'd invent a little interview for that kind of money."

I didn't show him the document. Instead I described the least interesting parts of it. He listened while dismantling a paper napkin into a number of increasingly small rectangles.

"Maybe they're recruiting you," he said.

"That's what she said."

"No. I mean maybe the transcript *is* the recruitment. It's a way to get your attention. They know you like this kind of stuff."

"Who do you think they are?"

"People with money who know you like this kind of stuff."

"But why have me refuse?"

"Because otherwise you'd have nothing to think about. You'd just go to the interview."

I hated this explanation because it was a perfectly good one.

Simon examined the napkin rectangles.

"Have you looked them up?"

I had. Northline occupied several floors of a building downtown. It had been founded five years earlier by a woman named Miriam Sloane, whose name I vaguely recognized from an article about infrastructure planning. There were no photographs of the researchers, no description of their work beyond a few elegant paragraphs about decision science, and no jobs listed.

I told him this.

"You're going, though," he said.

"I haven't decided."

"Of course you have."

He pointed at my guitar.

"Did he fix it?"

I plugged it into the little amplifier by the window and played Ray's five chords, or as much of them as I could reproduce. Simon stopped arranging his napkin rectangles.

"That last one," he said.

"I know."

"Play it again."

I did.

He stayed for another hour. We discussed whether the human ear liked a particular sequence of sounds because the sequence possessed certain properties or because people had grown up hearing sequences with those properties. Eventually he said he ought to go home, and then we talked for another twenty minutes in the doorway.

After he left, I looked up the address of the meeting.

At ten fourteen, I received another email from Helen.

There was no text, just a revised attachment.

The transcript was now ten pages long.

Most of it was unchanged. The salary was the same. The photograph was the same. I was still wearing, in whatever imaginary room they had put me, the blue shirt that I actually owned.

The final page was new.

**DIRECTOR:** You can take the evening to think about it.

**VALE:** I already have.

**DIRECTOR:** And?

**VALE:** A friend of mine suggested something.

**DIRECTOR:** What did he suggest?

**VALE:** That the point of all this is to get my attention.

**DIRECTOR:** Has it?

I shut the laptop.

Out on the street a car went past playing music loudly enough to rattle its own license plate. A squirrel, or something considerably less welcome, moved along the roof.

I opened the laptop again.

The last line was not an answer to the director's question. It was a timestamp.

Tomorrow, 9:17 a.m.

I set my alarm for seven.
