## Thirty-three — The Vote

When I got home from Europe, there was a bicycle in the hallway.

"Is that yours?" I asked Nadia.

"No. It's yours."

"Did I buy a bicycle?"

"I bought you a bicycle."

I looked at it.

"Why?"

"Because you said you'd like to be able to ride to the lake."

"Oh."

"Also, it's your birthday."

It was not my birthday. My birthday had been two days earlier. I had spent a substantial portion of it at an airport, where a man had sung Happy Birthday to a woman named Jennifer. I had thought it was very nice of him.

Nadia had called me that morning. Simon had called me. Helen had sent me a picture of a cake. I had forgotten the date on which all these things had occurred.

"Thank you," I said.

"You're welcome."

She showed me where she'd put the helmet.

Then she went to the kitchen.

I stood in the hallway looking at the bicycle. I had a sudden desire to go somewhere on it. This was perhaps the first desire I'd had since the conversation with Vincent that I didn't immediately subject to some fairly unpleasant examination.

I took it outside.

The tires needed air. I found the pump Nadia had bought and put air in them. Then I rode to the end of the street.

At the end of the street I turned around.

I came back to the house.

"How was it?" Nadia asked.

"Wonderful."

"Good. Dinner's in ten minutes."

* * *

The board had scheduled a meeting for the following Tuesday. In preparation for it, Fatima sent everyone three documents. One was Vincent's proposal. Another was an account of the institute's finances. The third contained the conditions under which we thought it might be possible to work with Continuance.

She asked me to write an additional document describing the research I thought we'd be able to undertake if the proposal went ahead.

I wrote it.

It was an extremely good document. There were questions in it that I had wanted to investigate for years. There were experiments for which we would be able to recruit people with kinds of expertise we didn't have. There were provisions for giving outside researchers access to the results. There was a plan for extending the mathematical collaboration.

I read it again and wanted very much to work at the institute described in it.

I sent it to Fatima.

An hour later she came into my office.

"Is there another version of this?" she asked.

"No."

"Could you write one that doesn't involve Continuance?"

"Well, we wouldn't be able to do most of it."

"Then don't put most of it in."

I looked at her.

"I want to know what you'd do with the money we've got."

"Oh."

She went back to her office.

It took me considerably longer to write the second document. There were a number of things I had to give up in the course of writing it. But there were also some things I had forgotten I could do.

By the time the meeting began, there were four documents.

* * *

June had asked if she could speak at the meeting.

She wasn't a member of the board. Neither was Peter. But we had agreed that both of them, and Beatrice, would have an opportunity to talk about the proposed arrangements before the board made a decision.

June brought her laptop.

"I want to show you something," she said.

She had been working on the software she'd proposed on her first morning at the institute. The program could associate a claim with the material from which it had been derived and record changes to the claim as it was passed from one system to another.

She'd made a version that worked with a number of the experiments we were currently conducting.

On the screen there was a simple statement concerning the result of a mathematical investigation. It was possible to select any part of the statement and look at the evidence for it.

"This is really good," Simon said.

"Thanks."

She showed us another example. This one concerned a claim that a particular person had expressed an interest in a subject. It was an invented example that she'd created for the demonstration.

The evidence for the claim consisted of a record of the person reading a number of documents that concerned the subject. It didn't include any statement of interest on the part of the person.

"What I'm trying to do," June said, "is make it possible to keep those two things separate. And to keep them separate even after the information's gone through about six other systems."

She moved the cursor.

"The way this normally works is that somebody decides what sort of information the customer wants, and you make the system produce that sort of information. I'm trying to make it possible for the customer to find out what sort of information the system actually has."

She shut the laptop.

"Anyway, I'd like to finish it."

"Of course," I said.

She looked at me.

"Even if it makes one of Vincent's products look bad."

I nodded.

"Yeah."

She went back to her desk.

* * *

We spent nearly three hours discussing the offer.

One of the members of the board, a woman named Meredith Cole, had run a nonprofit in Houston for twenty years. She was interested in the question of whether the institute could use the money to accomplish things that were worth accomplishing while declining to participate in some of the other things the company wanted to do.

"Can you separate the work?" she asked me.

"To some extent."

"Well, I'd like to know to what extent."

Peter thought there were fairly good prospects of establishing a collaboration in which the scientific questions were specified in advance and the methods and findings were made publicly available. He didn't want us to dismiss the possibility without exploring it.

Beatrice had a different concern.

"I don't want to be in a position," she said, "where they can decide whether the next thing I want to work on with Peregrine is sufficiently interesting to them."

"We could try to arrange for that not to happen," Meredith said.

"Yes."

She looked at the documents.

"But I don't think the thing we want to do is the thing they're offering to pay for."

There was a discussion about this.

At one point Simon asked whether any member of the board thought that the commercial value of the proposed work depended on the company being able to obtain more information about its customers than its customers would be able to obtain about it.

Nobody answered immediately.

Meredith said, "I'd want them to tell us what they think the commercial value is."

We agreed that we would propose a rather different arrangement from the one Vincent had brought to me in Zurich. There would be no transfer of private research histories for the purpose of improving the company's ability to influence the collaborators. There would be provisions for independent publication and the use of findings by researchers outside the company. And we would want a separate agreement concerning Peregrine, one that did not require Open Question to undertake the other research.

If the company agreed, we would consider the collaboration.

If it didn't, we wouldn't.

The vote was unanimous.

After the meeting, Meredith came over to me.

"You look disappointed," she said.

"No."

"Well, you looked a little disappointed."

"I'd like to be able to do the research."

"I know."

She smiled.

"That's why we hired you."

I had not previously thought of the board as a collection of people who had hired me.

It was a fairly useful thought.

* * *

Vincent called two days later.

He was prepared to accept some of the conditions. He thought others would be difficult to reconcile with the purpose of the proposed work.

"Which ones?" I asked.

We discussed them.

The question of access to the histories was the principal difficulty. I explained that we'd be willing to consider studies involving people who had chosen to participate in them and who understood the nature of the work. We weren't willing to make the existing histories available for a new purpose on the assumption that the people who'd contributed to them would have approved of it.

"I don't think you've understood what we want to do with the material," he said.

"Possibly not."

"Adrian, we're trying to improve the quality of a relationship over time."

"Then I think you ought to have a conversation with the other person in the relationship."

He was quiet for a moment.

"Well, I'll talk to the others."

"Good."

Before he hung up, he asked if I'd been thinking about the document Eva had shown me.

"Yes."

"Has it changed your opinion of the proposal?"

"Probably."

"That's interesting."

"Yeah."

I told him that I would like to know whether he would have made me the same offer if the system had predicted that there was virtually no chance of my accepting it.

He laughed.

"I think that's a hypothetical question I ought to be allowed not to answer."

"Fair enough."

We said goodbye.

* * *

Nadia and I had been trying to decide whom we wanted to invite to the wedding.

There was a piece of paper on the kitchen table with the names of about forty people on it. We had different kinds of relationships with these people. There were some we had known for most of our lives. There were some we'd met within the previous year. There were a few whom I'd never met.

I sat down with a pen.

"Do you think Elliot would come?" I asked.

"You could invite him."

"Well, it's a long way."

"Yeah."

"And I don't know if he'd want to come all that way for a wedding."

"Okay."

I looked at the list.

"Just invite him," she said.

So I wrote his name down.

Then I wrote down Morag's name.

Nadia looked at it.

"That'd be fun."

"Yeah."

"Put Ian down, too."

I put Ian down.

We ended up with considerably more people on the list than we'd intended to have.

Some of them wouldn't come.

I was beginning to look forward to asking them anyway.
