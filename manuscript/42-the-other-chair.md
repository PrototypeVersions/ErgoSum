## Forty-two — The Other Chair

There was a woman at the conference in Paris who wanted to know whether I'd be willing to sit in a different chair.

The chairs on the stage had been arranged in a semicircle. My name was on a little piece of paper on the third chair. The woman wanted me to sit in the fourth chair because Miriam was going to be sitting in the second chair, and there was a possibility that the people operating the cameras wouldn't be able to obtain a good picture of both of us if I was sitting in the third chair.

I sat in the fourth chair.

Then somebody came over to explain that I was sitting in Vincent's chair.

I got up.

It was going to be a fairly long day.

* * *

The conference had been organized by a number of institutions that were interested in the use of artificial intelligence in the provision of public and private services. There were several hundred people in attendance. Some had developed the services. Some had been responsible for decisions concerning whether to use them. A number had been on the receiving end of decisions made with their assistance.

Sabine, whom I'd met in Brussels, was there. She'd arranged for several people who worked with disabled people to take part in the discussions. Gabriel was there, and Emma Lu, the woman who'd appeared on the television program with Vincent and me.

There was also a man named Louis who had been using a service that helped him communicate with people.

Louis had a condition that made speaking difficult. He could type, although typing was slow and tiring for him. The service could generate possible replies during conversations and allow him to select one of them.

He had been using it for a couple of years.

He gave a short demonstration.

Somebody asked him whether he'd been enjoying Paris. Several possible answers appeared on the screen. He selected one.

A pleasant voice said, "Very much. I haven't been here since my sister got married."

There was some applause.

Louis smiled.

Then he began typing something himself.

It took a little while.

"The other answers were rubbish," the voice said.

Everybody laughed.

He went on to explain that the service was very useful to him. It enabled him to take part in conversations that he otherwise found exhausting. There were problems with it, but on the whole he was very glad to have it.

Then he demonstrated a version of the service that somebody had been developing that would enable the system to supply answers to certain questions without waiting for him to select them.

There were various settings he could use. He could specify the circumstances under which he was willing for the system to do this.

He liked the idea.

He didn't like that version of the service.

The reason, he told us, was that sometimes it was difficult to establish which things he'd said and which things he'd allowed the system to say for him. He wanted the distinction to be apparent, particularly when he was dealing with people he didn't know.

Then he said something else.

He said that he would be quite happy for the system to say things for him in certain circumstances even if he didn't know what the things were going to be.

I had a number of questions I wanted to ask him.

I wrote them down.

* * *

When it was my turn to speak, I put up a copy of the first page of the interview transcript.

I'd obtained permission to use it, and the version on the screen contained only information pertaining to me. At the top of the page was a little note explaining that the material was a simulation of a conversation that hadn't taken place.

I told the story of the telephone call from Helen.

I told them about Ray and the guitar. I told them about the meeting at Northline and about some of the things I'd discovered since then.

Then I put up the question I'd found on the second page.

*What would you be doing if you hadn't come here today?*

I explained that when the question was supposedly asked, I'd been doing something else.

There was some laughter.

I told them that I'd initially regarded the transcript as a description of something about me. In some respects it was. But it was also a description of the kind of organization that had produced it. It reflected the questions the people at Northline had been interested in asking, the circumstances under which they had been interested in asking them, and the sorts of difficulties they had expected a candidate might have with those circumstances.

I put up a second slide.

There was a little diagram on it. In the diagram there were two people about whom a service had acquired exactly the same information. There were two different things the service could do. Depending on which person it was dealing with, different things might follow from the thing it did.

I went through the argument.

It wasn't an argument to the effect that people couldn't be understood. It wasn't an argument against the use of predictions in deciding what to do. It was an argument concerning the limitations of a particular kind of information, even in a case in which the information was much better than the information we were ordinarily able to obtain.

I wanted the people in the room to be able to distinguish the success of a service in understanding a person's life within certain arrangements from its success in understanding what the person's life might be like under other arrangements.

And I wanted them to be able to distinguish both of those things from the question of what the service was entitled to do.

I looked at Louis.

He was looking at the diagram.

Then I put the next slide up.

It was a photograph of the building in Dallas in which Nadia and I had got married. It had been taken several days after the flood.

There were chairs standing outside in the sun.

I told them about Renee.

I told them what we had been able to establish about the failure of the coordination service and what we hadn't been able to establish.

I told them that the important thing, in Renee's case, was that she needed somewhere to sleep.

There had been an extremely good reason to use some of the technology that had been developed for the coordination of the relief effort.

There had also been an extremely good reason for Renee to be able to tell somebody that she didn't have somewhere to sleep.

At the end of the talk I put up a slide containing three questions.

The first concerned the accuracy of a prediction.

The second concerned the consequences of using it.

The third concerned the authority to use it in a particular way.

I said I didn't think that an answer to any one of the questions could, in general, be obtained simply by establishing an answer to one of the others.

Then I sat down.

I'd spoken for twenty-six minutes.

For the next hour people asked me questions.

* * *

One of the people who asked me a question was Vincent.

He wanted to know whether I thought the difference between an accurate prediction and a decision made on a person's behalf meant that the person's previous decisions were of no significance in establishing what a system ought to be allowed to do.

"No," I said.

"Well, suppose I have a service that's been making decisions for me for years. I've been happy with the decisions. And suppose there's a circumstance in which I can't be consulted."

"Okay."

"Don't you think there's something to be said for allowing the service to make another decision for me?"

"Yes."

He smiled.

"Good."

I looked at him.

"But there's something I'd like to know about your example."

"What?"

"Did you ask it to make decisions for you?"

"Yes."

"Okay."

"And if I hadn't?"

"Then I think you'd need another argument."

There was a discussion about the kinds of authority a person could give to a system, the kinds of decisions that could be made on that basis, and the circumstances in which the authority could be withdrawn. Sabine pointed out that withdrawing authority was not a particularly useful possibility for people who had no practicable means of doing it.

A man in the audience wanted to know whether I thought a person ought to be able to authorize a system to make decisions that the person subsequently disagreed with.

"Possibly," I said.

"Then what happens when they disagree?"

"I don't know. I think that's one of the things we'd have to work out."

Louis raised his hand.

The person organizing the discussion brought a microphone over to him. He pressed a button on the device in front of him.

"I would like to say something about that," the voice said.

There was a pause.

Then another sentence appeared.

"I sometimes ask my sister to order food for me. She knows me quite well. Occasionally she orders something I don't want."

He began typing.

We waited.

"I am entitled to be annoyed with my sister. She is entitled to be annoyed with me. We don't normally have to decide which one of us is right in order to have dinner."

There was laughter.

Then he typed some more.

"I don't want a machine that makes it unnecessary for me to have that sort of relationship with my sister. I would like one that makes it easier."

There was applause.

I hadn't anticipated that the thing I was most interested in at the conference would be something said by a person who hadn't been invited to give a lecture.

I asked Louis whether he'd mind if I mentioned what he'd said in a paper.

He said that he'd like to see what I wanted to write first.

I told him I'd send it to him.

* * *

That afternoon the working group was trying to arrive at a statement concerning the use of automated systems to act on people's behalf.

There was considerable agreement about some parts of it. There was less agreement about others.

One of the provisions concerned the circumstances under which evidence of a person's likely agreement to something could be treated as authorization.

Vincent didn't think the proposed wording was adequate. He thought that it would make it difficult for institutions to act in a number of circumstances in which they had very good reasons to act.

Sabine asked him to give an example.

He gave an example concerning assistance provided in an emergency.

We discussed it.

Miriam suggested that we distinguish between having a reason to act in an emergency and having the authorization of the person on whose behalf one acted.

A woman at the table said, "But if the action is justified, does it matter which of those things we have?"

Miriam looked at her.

"It will matter later," she said.

"Why?"

"Because later somebody will want to know whether the person agreed to it."

The woman looked down at the document.

I thought of Helen.

We went back through the provision. We found language that preserved room for action under applicable emergency powers without requiring anybody to attribute a decision to a person who hadn't made one. We also agreed on the need to keep records of delegated authority, including the conditions under which it had been granted.

There were still disagreements. The statement was a recommendation, not a law. There would be considerable work involved in turning any of it into useful requirements.

Nonetheless, by the time we stopped for the evening, the document contained something I was very pleased to see.

The provision did not say that an institution should never act without an individual's consent.

It said that an institution's reason for acting should not be recorded as the individual's consent.

I took a photograph of the provision and sent it to Helen.

*That's good,* she wrote.

A minute later she wrote again.

*Now find out who has to do the recording.*

I laughed.

I put my phone away.

* * *

I went out with Vincent afterward.

We walked down a street near the river. Neither of us had a particular place in mind, and eventually we stopped at a restaurant.

He told me he'd been enjoying the work with Celia.

"She's very irritating," he said.

"Yeah."

"She keeps asking me whether I'd be happy with a particular result if it were obtained by the other system."

"She asks me that, too."

"Good."

We ordered dinner.

Then he told me something I hadn't expected.

He'd been thinking about leaving Continuance.

"Why?" I asked.

"Various reasons."

"Are you unhappy there?"

"Not particularly."

"Okay."

He took a drink.

"I think I used to have quite a good idea of what I wanted the company to become."

"Yeah."

"And now there are some other people who have rather good ideas of what they want it to become."

He smiled.

"They're very energetic."

"Are you going to let them?"

"I don't know."

We sat there.

"Do you ever wish you'd accepted the job at Northline?" he asked.

"I did accept the job at Northline."

"No, the other one."

"Oh."

I thought about the directorship Miriam had offered me. There were some very good things I might have done with that research budget.

"Occasionally," I said.

He laughed.

"Well, that's encouraging."

"Why?"

"Because I find it very difficult to believe the people who tell me they never wonder about these things."

We finished dinner.

Outside, he shook my hand.

"I hope the study goes well," he said.

"Me too."

Then I asked him something.

"Which result would you least like to get?"

He thought about it.

"I think I'd least like to find that the two systems are equally good for the people who use them."

"Why?"

"Because then we might have to have a rather difficult conversation about what our customers are paying us for."

He laughed.

"How about you?"

I thought about it.

"I think I'd least like to find that the things I've been most worried about aren't the things the participants have been most worried about."

He looked at me.

"Well," he said, "I suppose we'll find out."

* * *

Back at the hotel I called Nadia.

She was at the library.

The building was almost finished, and there were some people doing the electrical work. She took the phone into the room with the big table.

The table was there now.

She showed it to me.

"How's that?" she asked.

"Beautiful."

"I know."

There were some chairs around it. The windows were open. Through one of them I could see the trees.

"What's going on in Paris?"

I told her.

She listened.

When I'd finished, she turned the phone around.

"Come home," she said. "I want you to see this place."

I told her I would.

After we hung up, I opened my email.

There was a message from Celia.

The independent team conducting the study had identified a possible difficulty with one of the ways we had agreed to measure the discovery of new interests. They wanted to discuss it with the oversight committee before the analysis was completed.

There were no comparative results in the message.

I read it twice.

Then I shut the computer.

For the first time since we'd begun the study, I found myself looking forward to finding out that there was something wrong with a question we'd asked.
