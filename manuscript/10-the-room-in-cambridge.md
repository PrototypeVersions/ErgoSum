## Ten — The Room in Cambridge

Somewhere over the Atlantic I experienced a tremendous feeling of optimism about my life. I attribute this partly to the fact that I was on an airplane going to England and partly to the fact that I had been awake for twenty hours. There are a number of circumstances in which the human mind, deprived of the opportunity to perform its customary functions, will go ahead and give you a rather nice time.

The woman next to me was asleep. The lights were off. On the little screen in front of me there was a picture of an airplane in the middle of a large blue expanse. It appeared to be going extremely slowly, which was a comforting thing to know only if you were able to forget all the other things you knew about airplanes.

I opened my notebook.

I wanted to write something about the distinction between a person who had no intention of doing something and a person who was unable to do it. It seemed to me that the distinction was of enormous importance. I wrote a page and a half, looked at the map again, and fell asleep.

When I woke up, we were over Ireland and I had drawn a small line down the page with my pen.

* * *

Northline had an apartment in a narrow street in Bloomsbury. It was on the third floor of a building whose stairs gave you a great deal of information about the people using the other stairs. I put my bag down, opened the window, and heard a woman on the pavement below saying that she had absolutely no intention of going to Waitrose again.

I was very happy to be in England.

I bought some food, slept for three hours, and went out in the evening. The shops were lit up for Christmas. It was cold in precisely the way that I had hoped it would be cold. I had my new coat on and no particular place to go.

I walked until I came to a little restaurant with steamed-up windows. I had dinner there and read about eleven pages of a book. At the next table there were three people trying to establish the whereabouts of somebody named Tom. There were a number of hypotheses concerning Tom, and I became so interested in their merits that I lost track of the book.

Eventually Tom came in.

Nobody seemed particularly pleased to see him.

On the way home I called Nadia. It was the middle of the afternoon in Dallas. She was on her way to look at some broken drainage pipes.

"What are you doing?" she asked.

"Walking around London."

"How's the coat?"

"Incredible."

"Good. It should be able to get a job there."

I told her about the restaurant.

"Did you find the park?"

"Not yet."

"Well, you've got ten days."

"Yeah."

There was a pause. I could hear the sound of her car, and then the little noise the car made when she turned on the turn signal.

"I miss you," I said.

"Oh," she said.

I wondered briefly whether I had done something terrible.

Then she said, "That's good. I miss you, too."

I walked home through a cold, unfamiliar city feeling almost embarrassingly fortunate.

* * *

The next morning I took a train to Cambridge.

Finch had offered to meet me at the station, but I told him I'd enjoy the walk. I had looked at a map and was able to recognize a considerable number of the places I was supposed to be walking past. After a while I stopped recognizing them. I eventually arrived at his college from a direction I hadn't intended.

He was standing beneath an arch, talking to a man with a bicycle. I knew him from photographs. He was smaller than I'd expected and was wearing a green scarf. When he saw me, he ended the conversation and came over.

"Adrian."

"Professor Finch."

"Oh, for God's sake. Elliot."

We shook hands.

"Did you get lost?"

"Yes."

"Good. I always worry about people who don't get lost the first time they come here."

He took me through the college. We went past a lawn which, judging by the signs, was the most valuable piece of vegetation in England.

"Have you eaten?"

"Not since breakfast."

"Come on, then."

We had lunch in a room with portraits on the walls. The man in the portrait nearest our table looked as if he'd been informed of an error in the meal and had been waiting two hundred years for someone to do something about it.

Finch asked me about Texas. He had been to Houston once and had been impressed by the size of a baked potato.

"Was it an unusually large one?" I asked.

"Well, you'd know better than I would. I only saw the one."

We talked about the trip, then about people we both knew. He was very funny. I had not been expecting him to be funny.

Finally I asked him why he hadn't liked my paper.

"Oh, I liked it."

"Peter gave me a somewhat different impression."

"Well, I thought the argument was wrong. I don't see why that should prevent me from liking the paper."

He cut a piece off his bread.

"The difficulty with that argument was that you seemed to take the existence of a certain kind of subject for granted. And you gave this subject a certain range of possible beliefs, a certain relationship to the information available to him. You derived a contradiction."

"Right."

"Now, I thought you'd established something. I just thought it was something about the conditions you'd specified, rather than something about our ordinary conception of belief."

"That's probably right."

He looked at me.

"Really?"

"Yeah."

"Oh. Well, there goes the afternoon."

I laughed.

He told me about his wife, Ruth. She had been a mathematician, though she had worked for much of her life in a department that didn't have the word mathematics in its name. They had met when he was twenty-six and she was thirty. He said he had been under the impression that she was twenty-four. She had been under the impression that he was twenty-six.

"She was right about a great many things from the outset," he said.

In the last years of her life she had worked on a problem concerning the use of predictions in the administration of social services. He described a simple example.

A local authority needed to estimate the demand for a service. It had data on how many people had made use of it in previous years. It also had data on several thousand people who might be expected to need it in the coming year.

"So far, perfectly ordinary," he said.

"Sure."

"Now, there was a question about whether to continue sending letters informing people of the availability of the service. The letters cost money. There was a proposal that they only be sent to people likely to make use of it."

"Ah."

"Yes. And when somebody who didn't receive a letter failed to make use of the service, that would go into the next year's data."

"Did they do it?"

"I believe so. On a limited basis."

"Did she find out what happened?"

"She tried. That was rather the problem."

We finished lunch and went to his rooms.

* * *

I had once imagined that if I became a professor at a place like Cambridge I would have a room rather like Finch's. There was a fireplace, a big window looking out on a courtyard, and enough books to present a serious engineering problem.

On the mantelpiece there was a photograph of a woman holding up an enormous fish.

"Ruth?" I asked.

"Yes."

"That's quite a fish."

"She didn't catch it. She just wanted a photograph of herself with it."

He took a cardboard box out of a cupboard.

"I haven't gone through all of this," he said. "I thought I'd go through some of it with you."

The box contained notebooks, folders, several old computer disks, and a great many loose sheets of paper. On the top was a photograph of two people standing in a field in Scotland. He put that one aside.

"What are we looking for?" I asked.

"There's a report she wrote in 2006. I have the final version on the computer. But there's another version that has a bit in it which didn't make it into the final one."

We began going through the box.

It was a strange experience. I had come to Cambridge to talk to an eminent philosopher, and here I was sitting on the floor sorting a dead woman's grocery lists from her mathematical notes. I came across a page on which she had written the first few lines of an argument, then a recipe for something involving aubergines, then the rest of the argument.

Finch read the recipe over my shoulder.

"Oh, that was dreadful," he said.

"Did she make it?"

"Repeatedly."

After a while he found the report. He handed it to me.

It was called *Forecasting Participation in Voluntary Programs.*

I read for twenty minutes. The discussion was fairly technical, but the point of the missing section was simple. There were two ways a prediction could cease to be wrong. One could make a better prediction, or one could change the circumstances in which the outcome came about. In the example Ruth had been concerned with, the second possibility was almost entirely under the control of the people evaluating the predictions.

There was a proposal for an experiment.

It involved two groups of eligible people. In both groups the researchers would forecast who would make use of a service. In one group those forecasts would be used to determine who received an invitation. In the other, everyone would receive one. The difference in outcomes would make it possible to estimate some of the effects of withholding invitations.

I looked up.

"This is good."

"Yes."

"Was it ever done?"

"No. They weren't enthusiastic about her proposal. She was offered a smaller project in which she would have evaluated the predictions against the outcomes they already had."

"Oh."

"She said that would be like being paid to count the people who'd walked through a door after somebody else had locked it."

He smiled. Then he looked down at the photograph he'd put aside.

"She was a great deal more difficult than I am."

I finished reading.

"Can I ask you something?"

"Of course."

"How did you get involved with Northline?"

He took his glasses off and put them on the desk.

"After she died, I spent some time going through her work. Not very systematically. There were people she'd corresponded with whom I got in touch with. Miriam was one of them."

"She knew Ruth?"

"Yes. Miriam had been involved in one of the earlier projects. She understood what Ruth was trying to do. Much better than I did, to be honest."

He leaned back.

"When Northline began doing work with individual representations, I thought we might have an extraordinary opportunity to investigate some of these problems. Not by replacing human beings with simulations, but by studying the differences between the two. I wrote a proposal. They funded it."

"The Observatory."

"Yes."

"Did they fund this experiment?"

"No."

"Did you ask?"

"Several times."

"What'd they say?"

"They said they were working on it."

He put his glasses back on.

"Eventually I discovered that my participation in the project had been considerably greater than I'd realized."

"What do you mean?"

He opened his laptop.

"I'm going to show you something. Miriam knows I have it. I'm not sure she knows I've been looking at it."

There was a spreadsheet on the screen. It contained a list of meetings, dates, and project names. Finch pointed at the first few rows.

"These are meetings I attended. I remember them. The minutes are reasonably accurate."

He scrolled down.

"These are meetings I didn't attend. I've been asked whether I can verify some of the minutes."

"Who asked you?"

"A woman at a funding organization. She was doing a review."

"Do the minutes say you were there?"

"Some of them distinguish between the people who were physically present and the people who weren't. Some don't."

He opened a PDF.

There was a heading, a date, and a list of participants. Finch's name was on the list with an asterisk beside it.

I thought of the program for London.

"Remote participation?" I asked.

"That's what I thought."

He found the relevant section.

**EF:** I would recommend that the pilot proceed without an additional consent stage. We have a sufficiently reliable basis for anticipating the responses of the participants.

I read it again.

"Did you ever say that?"

"No."

"Would you?"

"No."

"Are those minutes generated by the Observatory?"

"I don't know. They may simply be a record of something someone said about me. That's one of the things I'm trying to establish."

"Can you get the original recording?"

"I've asked."

He closed the document.

"The reason I'm showing this to you, Adrian, is that there are two rather different possibilities here. One is that there's an administrative muddle. And I'm perfectly prepared to discover that there's an administrative muddle."

"And the other?"

"That somebody has found a way of having a meeting without the people who would make it difficult."

A bell rang somewhere outside.

He got up and went to the window.

"I suppose that might be a very useful application of the technology," he said.

* * *

At half past five I left the college with a copy of Ruth's draft report in my bag and a promise to see Elliot in London.

"You're coming in person?" I asked.

"Of course."

"Good."

He wanted to walk me to the end of the street. We stood there for a little while under a streetlight.

"Adrian," he said, "how did you find Miriam?"

"I like her."

"So do I."

"I don't know what to make of some of the things that have happened."

"Nor do I."

He took my hand.

"Come and see me again sometime when we haven't got something to worry about."

"I will."

He went back through the arch.

I walked to the station. There was a little bookshop on the way, and I stopped and bought two books I had no need of. On the train I took Ruth's report out and read the missing section again.

When I got back to the apartment in London I opened my email.

Helen had sent me a message.

*Adrian, sorry to bother you while you're away. I just wanted you to know that I declined the Denmark position this morning. I did it in writing. I made them acknowledge receipt.*

There was another paragraph.

*They've asked me to come to a meeting on Wednesday. I'll let you know how it goes.*

I sent her a reply and then got out my phone to call Nadia.

Before I did, I looked at the program for the conference.

Finch was still listed as a remote participant.

I thought about emailing the organizers. Instead I forwarded the program to him.

He replied a few minutes later.

*Excellent. Don't ask them to change it just yet.*
