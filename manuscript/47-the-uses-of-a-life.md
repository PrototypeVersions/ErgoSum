## Forty-seven — The Uses of a Life

On the morning of the first lecture, Elliot Finch discovered that I had become famous.

We were standing outside a bakery in Cambridge when a young woman stopped and asked whether I was Adrian Vale.

I said that I was.

She told me she'd been using June's software in connection with her doctoral research. She wanted to know if there was a way of getting in touch with the people who were developing it.

I gave her June's professional email address.

"Thank you very much," she said.

She went away.

Elliot looked at me.

"How does it feel?"

"How does what feel?"

"Being mistaken for somebody who knows how the software works."

We went inside.

He bought a large quantity of bread. He was having some people over to dinner that evening and had been trying to remember how much bread people ate when they came to dinner.

"How many people are coming?" I asked.

"Six."

"How much bread have you got?"

"Enough for about forty."

He looked into the bag.

"Well, we'll see what happens."

* * *

There were three lectures.

The organizers had asked me to give the series a title. I had sent them about eight possibilities, all of which they had disliked sufficiently to ask for another. Eventually we agreed on *The Uses of a Life*.

The first lecture was about knowledge.

I began by describing the sort of thing that happened when somebody was good at anticipating the behavior of another person. I gave some examples of things Nadia knew about me that I hadn't known until she told me about them. Then I described some of the things the people at Northline had managed to establish through the work they'd been doing.

I wanted to distinguish the things one could learn about a person by examining the decisions they'd made from the things one could learn by making it possible for them to make other decisions.

The second lecture was about assistance.

I described several ways in which the provision of assistance could change the nature of the activity in which a person was engaged.

I had an example involving a piano teacher. There were things the teacher could do that would enable a child to play a piece of music. There were things the teacher could do that would result in the piece of music being played while the child was sitting at the piano.

It seemed to me that the difference between those things mattered even if the same notes were played.

And there were plenty of occasions on which the second thing was quite all right. It was one of the ways a child could find out what a piece of music sounded like.

I thought that some of our difficulties concerning the use of artificial intelligence arose from a failure to establish which activity we were engaged in.

There was a discussion afterward.

A man in the audience asked me whether I thought the child would have been better off if the teacher had been unable to play the piano.

"No," I said.

He smiled.

"Well, I think there are people who would say that the danger of the new systems consists in their being too good at certain things."

"Yes."

"Do you disagree?"

"Sometimes. I think some of the danger consists in the ways we arrange things when we discover that they're good at those things."

I told him about Helen.

I told him that it had been possible to develop a system that generated a plausible account of a job interview without developing a practice under which a person was regarded as having attended an interview because the system had generated an account of it.

He nodded.

Then he asked a question about a case in which it seemed quite reasonable to make use of the system's abilities in a manner I hadn't considered.

I wrote it down.

The following morning I changed part of the third lecture.

* * *

At Elliot's dinner there was a woman named Edith, who had known Ruth since the two of them were students.

Edith had been trying for some time to persuade Elliot to go to Italy with her and several friends.

"Tell him he ought to come," she said to me.

"Where?"

"Florence."

I looked at Elliot.

"Sounds good."

"Thank you," Edith said.

Elliot looked at her.

"He hasn't been to Florence."

"Have you?" she asked me.

"No."

She looked back at Elliot.

"Well, you could take him."

We spent some time discussing a trip that neither Elliot nor I had previously intended to undertake.

Later in the evening Edith told me a story about Ruth.

The two of them had gone to France in their early twenties. They'd been staying in a place that had a little garden, and there was a man next door who was making a boat. Ruth became so interested in the boat that she spent several days helping the man with it.

"Did she know anything about boats?" I asked.

"No."

"Did she learn anything about boats?"

"Yes. She was perfectly insufferable about boats for about a year."

Elliot laughed.

I asked him whether he'd heard the story before.

"Oh, yes."

Edith told us another one.

At some point Elliot went to get a photograph. There was Ruth, sitting in the garden in France. There was part of the boat in the corner of the photograph.

She was looking at something outside it.

I asked him whether he knew what she was looking at.

"No," he said.

He smiled.

"Probably Edith."

* * *

The third lecture was the one I was most nervous about giving.

It concerned the possibility of participating in the determination of one's own life.

There had been a temptation, in some of the earlier versions of the lecture, to try to establish that there were things about a person that an artificial intelligence could never know. There were some arguments of this kind that I thought were good arguments. I had written one of them.

But I had come to think that there was a danger in making the importance of a person's participation in the determination of their life depend on the existence of some fact about them that another intelligence couldn't establish.

Suppose the intelligence could establish the fact.

Then what?

I thought of the student who had asked me the question in Edinburgh. Would it bother me if another intelligence could anticipate all my decisions?

I didn't have a general answer to the question. But I didn't think my life would cease to be mine as a consequence of somebody's being able to anticipate a great deal about it.

I described the difference between two ways a person might arrive at a decision.

In the first, somebody else had arranged the circumstances in order to obtain a particular decision from the person. The arrangements were such that the person could not effectively examine or alter them. Some of the other possibilities had been withheld.

In the second, somebody else had also contributed to the person's arriving at the decision. But the person had been able to question the assistance, make use of other information and pursue things the person providing the assistance hadn't anticipated.

It was possible for the two people to make the same decision. It was even possible for them to give much the same account of their reasons for making it.

The difference, I suggested, concerned the sort of participation they'd been able to have in the events from which the decision arose.

There were difficult questions about how to describe that participation. There were circumstances in which a person couldn't participate very much and in which there were very good reasons to make decisions on their behalf. There were other circumstances in which the person's participation consisted in choosing somebody else to make the decision.

I didn't have a theory that provided a satisfactory account of all of them.

What I had was a reason for thinking that it mattered which of these circumstances we were in.

I looked at the people in the room.

Then I put up a photograph of a guitar.

* * *

Ray had sent me the photograph the previous week.

It was a guitar he'd been working on, and I had asked whether he would mind my using a picture of it in the lecture.

I told the audience about the five chords.

I explained that I hadn't known how to play the fifth chord in a manner that I liked. I'd asked Ray what he would play, and he'd played something beautiful. I had gone home and learned to play it.

There was a sense in which the music I'd subsequently played would have been quite different if Ray had done something different.

There were quite a few people at the lecture who played musical instruments. Several of them wanted to know what the chord was.

I told them I wasn't going to tell them.

They laughed.

I said that I didn't think there had been anything particularly valuable about the possibility of my learning to play a chord that nobody else had ever played. There had been something valuable about my learning to play a chord that somebody else had played, and about the things I'd subsequently been able to do with it.

I had been able to use it in music Ray hadn't heard. I had played it for people Ray hadn't met. I had been able to decide that I didn't want to play it.

And Ray had been able to do other things.

I finished the lecture.

The room was quiet for a moment. Then there was applause.

I was pleased.

I was sufficiently pleased that I nearly forgot the piece of paper on which I'd written the questions I wanted to ask the people I'd be having dinner with afterward.

Elliot brought it to me.

"Here," he said.

"Thanks."

He looked at the photograph of the guitar.

"That was rather good."

"Thanks."

"Although I'd quite like to know what the chord was."

I told him.

He made a face.

"Oh," he said. "I thought it was going to be something else."

* * *

When I got back to the flat, I called Nadia.

She was coming the following week.

"How'd it go?" she asked.

"Pretty well."

"Did you finish?"

"Yeah."

"Okay. Now have you got a minute?"

"Sure."

She told me about something that had happened at one of the buildings in Texas. It concerned a group of people who had begun using a room in a way nobody had anticipated. There was going to be a little difficulty with some of the furniture.

I listened.

"What are you going to do?" I asked.

"Well, we're going to move some of the furniture."

"Okay."

She laughed.

"What?"

"Nothing."

We talked for a while.

Afterward I went out for a walk.

It was still light. There were people sitting beside the river. There was a man trying to untangle the lead of a dog that had become interested in a duck.

I walked past them.

I had been spending a substantial portion of the last several months trying to write a book that would establish something about the value of a person's being able to take part in the determination of their own life.

That evening I felt extraordinarily fortunate to have other people taking part in the determination of mine.
