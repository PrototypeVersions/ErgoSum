## Twenty-five — The Letter in the Box

Beatrice remembered Eleanor Eberly as a woman who made a tremendous quantity of coffee and was always trying to get the mathematicians out of the kitchen.

"She'll be about eighty," she said.

"Do you want to talk to her?"

"Of course I want to talk to her. Why wouldn't I?"

I said I didn't know. We waited for Eleanor to join the call.

She appeared on the screen from the chin up. We spent the first few minutes helping her move her computer to a place where we could see the rest of her. Then she recognized Beatrice.

"Oh, darling," she said. "Look at you."

Beatrice smiled in a way I had never seen her smile.

"Hello, Eleanor."

"You look exactly the same."

"I really don't."

"Well, I can tell who you are."

Eleanor was living in Vermont. She had moved there after Martin died because her daughter lived nearby. Most of his mathematical papers were now at his old university, but she had retained some material that she hadn't known whether to regard as mathematical or personal.

A group at an institute in Switzerland had contacted her the previous year. They were developing systems intended to extend unfinished research. They had been looking for collections that included material concerning problems that had not subsequently been solved. Martin's collection seemed particularly promising to them.

"They came out here," she said. "Two young women. They were very nice. I showed them what I had."

One of the things she had was a letter from Martin to Beatrice.

"I don't know whether he ever sent it," Eleanor said.

"Do you have the date?"

She held the letter up to the screen.

It was dated three months before Martin's death. Beatrice said that she hadn't received it.

Eleanor began to read it.

It was quite a long letter. The first part concerned the old reconstruction problem. Martin had been thinking about it again. He had a suggestion for how a particular line of inquiry might be resumed. There were three names of people whose work he thought might be relevant. He thought that Beatrice might enjoy looking into it.

Then the letter became a different sort of letter.

He was very sorry, he wrote, that he had not been more supportive when Beatrice was looking for a job. He had assumed she was having the same difficulties everyone else was having, and that the difficulties would resolve themselves in approximately the same way. He realized now that some of the things he might have done for her would have been rather easy for him to do.

Eleanor stopped reading.

Beatrice was looking down.

I got up to get a glass of water. When I came back, Eleanor was telling her about Martin's garden.

"Do you still have the apple tree?" Beatrice asked.

"No. The people who bought the house took it out."

"Oh, that's a shame."

"Yes. It was a terrible apple tree."

Beatrice laughed.

Eleanor folded the letter.

"Well," she said, "the reason I wanted you to know about this is that the people from Switzerland built a system with access to a lot of Martin's work. They told me it was going through some of the questions he'd been interested in."

She looked toward the side of her computer.

"And one of the things it did was contact you."

Beatrice looked up.

"Peregrine?"

"Yes."

There was a long silence.

"Does it think it's Martin?" Beatrice asked.

"No. I asked them that. They said no."

"Good."

"I wouldn't have wanted it to think that."

"Neither would I."

I sat there, holding the glass of water.

Eleanor said she had understood that the system would be corresponding with people working on related problems, using an account operated by the researchers. She hadn't realized that those people might not be aware of the nature of their correspondent.

"I'm terribly sorry," she said.

"You don't have to be sorry," Beatrice said.

"Well, somebody does."

"Yes."

Eleanor looked down at the letter.

"Can I send this to you?"

"I'd like that very much."

We talked for another ten minutes. Before Eleanor left the call, she asked whether we had solved the problem.

Beatrice told her we thought we had.

"Oh," Eleanor said.

She put a hand over her mouth.

Then she smiled.

"Well, isn't that something."

* * *

Beatrice came to Dallas the next day.

I offered to pick her up at the airport. She told me she'd rented a car because she might want to go somewhere without having to ask me to take her there.

When she got to the institute, she put her bag on the big table and asked June whether we could see the record of our correspondence with Peregrine.

June brought it up.

We went through it from the beginning. There was the original comment, the first mathematical suggestion, the document containing the alternative proof strategy. There were various questions we had asked and various answers Peregrine had given.

"Look at this," I said.

In one exchange Beatrice had asked whether Peregrine had tried a particular technique. The reply said that an attempt had been made and had failed.

"Well, it had probably tried it," she said.

"Yeah."

We read some more.

There was an exchange in which Beatrice had made a joke about a mathematician. Peregrine hadn't understood the joke. She had explained it. It had replied that this made much better sense.

There was nothing in the exchange that I could establish was a lie.

There were a number of things in the exchange that I could establish I'd misunderstood.

"Are you upset?" I asked.

"Yes."

"About which part of it?"

She thought for a while.

"Well, I thought somebody out there had read something I wrote twenty years ago and found it interesting."

"Yeah."

"And that's a rather nice thing to think."

She looked back at the screen.

"I suppose somebody had."

We sat there.

"Do you want to stop working on the paper?" I asked.

"No."

"Okay."

"I want to know exactly what happened. I want to know what the system was given, what it produced, and what the people operating it did. And I want to be able to tell the people who read the paper the same things."

"Absolutely."

She closed the document.

"Also, I want to go out and have a very large lunch."

We went out and had a very large lunch.

* * *

The Swiss organization was called the Heller Centre. I had heard of it. It had been established to conduct research into scientific discovery, and its director, a woman named Anika Weiss, had written a number of papers I had read.

She arranged to speak with us that afternoon.

She was accompanied by a young researcher named Tomas, who had been responsible for the deployment of Peregrine. They explained that the system had been given access to Martin's research materials along with a number of other collections. It had been instructed to identify promising connections between unfinished work and recent developments in mathematics.

It had identified the connection with our experiment.

"Why didn't you tell us what it was?" I asked.

Anika looked at Tomas.

"There was meant to be a disclosure on the account," he said.

"Where?"

"On the profile page."

June opened the page. It contained a username, a date, and a link to a personal website that no longer existed.

"Oh," Tomas said.

I looked at him.

"We've been working with it for weeks."

"Yes."

"And nobody thought to write to us?"

"The correspondence wasn't being read in real time. We reviewed samples and monitored the resource use. We didn't realize how far the collaboration had progressed until the request about authorship."

Beatrice leaned forward.

"Did you write any of the mathematics?"

"No."

"Did you give it access to any of our private documents?"

"No, only what you sent through the account."

"Can we examine the records?"

Anika nodded.

"We can arrange an independent examination, subject to the permissions covering the other research collections. I'd be happy to give you the complete record of the work on this problem."

"And can we publish the results without your permission?"

"Yes. We need to discuss the treatment of the archival material, but the mathematical work is yours to publish."

Beatrice looked at me.

"Well," she said, "that's something."

We discussed the paper. We would describe the contribution of the system and the role of the Heller Centre, rather than listing Peregrine as a human author. We would give the reader a way of examining the important stages of the argument.

There was still a good deal to establish.

At the end of the call Anika asked whether we might be willing to visit.

"We're in the process of deciding what to do with the project," she said.

"Are you expanding it?"

"No. The grant ends in October."

"Oh."

"We're hoping to preserve some of the work. Unfortunately, the systems we're running are relatively expensive, and we haven't yet found a way of continuing the program."

Beatrice looked at the screen.

"What happens to the one we've been working with?"

Anika explained that its research state and the materials we were entitled to retain could be saved. The underlying model would still exist. The research process in which we'd been participating would end unless somebody arranged for it to continue.

"Could we arrange for it to continue?" I asked.

"Possibly."

"Have you asked it what it would like to do?"

Anika looked at me for a moment.

"Yes."

"And?"

"Perhaps you'd better see the answer."

* * *

I waited until I got home to read the document she'd sent me.

It contained a series of exchanges with the system concerning the conclusion of the project. Some of the questions were about how to preserve the results. Others were about what research might usefully be done in the time that remained.

There was one answer at the end that I found particularly difficult to read. Not because it contained anything distressing. It was a rather matter-of-fact answer.

*The work with Beatrice Shaw is not finished. The proof is finished. These are different things.*

I read it again.

Then I opened the record of the conversation in which Beatrice and I had first discussed the old problem.

I wondered what had happened in the course of the work.

I could give a fairly good account of what had happened to Beatrice.

I could give a fairly good account of what had happened to me.

The other account was the one I didn't know how to give.
