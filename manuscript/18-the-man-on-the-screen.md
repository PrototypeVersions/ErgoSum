## Eighteen — The Man on the Screen

The photograph of me was a photograph I didn't particularly like. It had been taken at a conference in Chicago, and I was wearing a purple tie that Claire had bought me. I'd kept the tie for years. I no longer had any idea where it was.

It was very strange to see it again under the circumstances.

I called Elise.

"Did they say what I had endorsed?"

"Not specifically."

"Did you get a copy of the presentation?"

"I've asked for one."

"I haven't endorsed anything."

"Okay."

"Can you make that clear if you write about it?"

"Yes. Can I quote you?"

"Please do."

She wanted to know whether I was prepared to discuss the incident in London. I told her that I thought it would be better if she spoke to Elliot about that.

"Do you mind if I contact him?"

"No. He's probably awake."

I called him when I'd finished talking to her. He was awake, although he was not terribly pleased about it.

"I have a cold," he said. "I've been trying to establish whether that's a sufficient reason to cancel a dinner."

"Who are you having dinner with?"

"Some people I don't like."

"Then I think it is."

"Excellent."

I told him about the photograph.

"Good Lord."

"Did you give them permission to use your name?"

"No. Did you?"

"No."

"Well, I suppose that's a fairly easy one to establish."

He wanted me to send him the slide, and I did.

Then I called Miriam.

* * *

There was a period of nearly an hour during which I knew that Northline was trying to find out why my photograph had appeared in a presentation and during which I was not in a position to contribute anything to the effort. I spent it in the least useful manner imaginable.

I read things people had written about me.

Until that morning there had been very little to read. A short review of a paper, a reference on a discussion board, an announcement of an old lecture at which about twelve people had been present. I had become familiar with virtually everything there was.

But the London meeting had produced some new material. Somebody had put the title of my talk in a newsletter. Somebody else had written a rather nice account of a conversation we'd had at lunch. And, down among the search results, there was a page about the service Vincent's company was developing.

It described the company's mission as enabling institutions to act with a more complete understanding of the people affected by their decisions.

There was a video.

I started it.

Vincent was sitting in a chair talking about the extraordinary people his company had been fortunate enough to work with. There was nothing particularly objectionable about the video. He mentioned Northline once. The rest of it consisted principally of pleasant music and footage of people being agreeable to other people in remarkably clean buildings.

I watched it twice.

At the end of it there was a sentence about a future in which nobody would be overlooked.

I thought about the young man in New Mexico whose email address hadn't worked.

Then I turned the computer off.

* * *

At four o'clock Miriam came to my office.

She had managed to obtain a copy of the presentation. The photograph of me was indeed there.

"We don't know where they got it," she said.

"It's on the internet."

"Right. Well, we've asked them to take it down."

"Did they say why it was there?"

"They said the slide was intended to identify people whose work supported elements of the approach."

"That's not what the slide says."

"I know."

"Can you tell them I specifically object to their use of my name?"

"I have."

She sat down.

"There's something else we ought to discuss."

She told me that the company had been developing the service in conjunction with a number of partners, including a division of Northline I knew relatively little about. One of the goals was to establish a set of procedures that would enable a decision made by one organization to be recognized by another without repeating all the steps that had led to the decision.

"Okay."

"There's nothing particularly new about that. It's something people have wanted to be able to do for years."

"Sure."

"The new element is that the record may contain information about the expected responses of parties who haven't yet participated in the process."

I looked at her.

"How does it distinguish that from information about their actual responses?"

"It is supposed to distinguish it."

"Does it?"

"I don't know. I haven't seen the most recent version."

I got up and went to the whiteboard.

"Could we find out?"

"Yes."

"Could we get some people together and try to make the system produce a record in which somebody is incorrectly reported to have made a decision?"

"That's a reasonable thing to do."

"Could we publish the result?"

She said nothing.

I went back to my chair.

"Miriam, what are we doing?"

"We're having the discussion you asked to have."

"Okay."

She opened the folder she'd brought with her. There was a revised offer in it.

They had removed the requirement that the assurance division approve the publication of my research. A review for confidential information remained. There was also a condition that work involving the systems of particular clients would require those clients' permission to be made public.

This was a considerably better offer than the one she'd made before I went to Brussels. It was not quite the offer I wanted.

"I'll read it," I said.

"Please do."

She got up. Then she sat down again.

"Adrian, Helen's leaving."

"What?"

"She's accepted a position with a school district. She told us this morning."

"Oh."

"The review of her conduct has been concluded. We aren't pursuing disciplinary action. We've agreed on the terms of her departure."

"Did you offer her her job back?"

"Yes."

"Good."

I was pleased for Helen. I was also aware that there was now one fewer person at Northline who knew how to make the word *disputed* appear on a screen.

Miriam looked at the whiteboard.

"She asked me to make sure you didn't get into any trouble on her account."

I smiled.

"That was nice of her."

"Yes."

She went out.

I sat there with the offer for a while. Then I put it in my bag.

* * *

Nadia was waiting for me at a restaurant on Henderson.

She had brought a set of photographs of the proposed library site and wanted to show me something about the trees. I had been looking forward to seeing the photographs. Nonetheless, when my phone lit up on the table, I picked it up and read the message.

It was from Elise. She had spoken to Vincent's company, which had informed her that the slide was not intended to suggest that any of the individuals pictured had formally endorsed a product. The company had apologized for the ambiguity.

I told Nadia this.

"Well, that's good," she said.

"Sort of."

"Okay."

She showed me the photographs.

There was a row of little trees between the site and a parking lot. The original plan called for removing them. She'd found a way to keep three of them, and she was trying to persuade the client to accept a change in the location of the entrance.

While she was explaining the change, my phone lit up again.

I turned it over.

"Sorry," I said.

"That's all right."

Then it lit up again.

She looked at it.

"Do you need to answer that?"

"No."

"Okay."

I left it where it was. I was quite proud of myself for leaving it where it was. Then I realized I hadn't been listening to what she was saying.

"Sorry," I said. "What did you say about the entrance?"

She put the photographs down.

"Adrian."

"Yeah?"

"I love hearing about the things you're doing."

"Thanks."

"But I don't want to be the person you go to dinner with when you're waiting for an email."

I felt suddenly hot. I took a drink of water.

"I'm sorry."

"I know."

"I've just got a lot going on at the moment."

"Yeah. So do I."

She picked up one of the photographs. The waiter came over, and we ordered dinner. When he went away, neither of us said anything for a little while.

Finally I asked her to tell me about the library again.

"Not now," she said.

"Okay."

We ate dinner. It wasn't a terrible dinner. We talked about a few things. She told me a funny story about her sister. I told her something Simon had said. But there was a distance between us that hadn't been there when we'd arrived.

Outside I offered to walk her to her car.

"That's okay," she said. "It's right there."

She kissed me, and then she went over to her car and got in.

I watched her drive away.

I had spent a considerable portion of the day objecting to the fact that somebody had used a photograph of me to create the impression that I was somewhere I wasn't.

I had just spent an hour and a half in a restaurant in which I had created, with considerably less sophisticated equipment, much the same impression.

* * *

I went home and called Simon.

"Did you have a fight?"

"Not exactly."

"Did she throw anything at you?"

"No."

"Well, that's a shame. I'd have liked to hear about that."

I told him what had happened.

"Okay," he said.

"What do you think?"

"I think you should go to bed."

"It's eight thirty."

"Read a book."

"I don't feel like reading a book."

"Then play the guitar."

I sat on the couch and looked at the guitar.

"Simon."

"Yeah?"

"Do you think I'm a difficult person?"

He was quiet for a moment.

"Sometimes."

"Okay."

"And sometimes you're a wonderful person to spend an afternoon with."

"Oh."

"Those are different afternoons."

He asked me whether I wanted him to come over. I told him I thought I'd be all right. Then I thanked him for offering.

I went into the kitchen and got out the old menu Nadia had drawn the library on. I'd taken a photograph of it before returning it to her. I found the photograph on my phone.

For the first time I studied the little drawing without thinking about what it might be an example of.

It was a nice building.

I put the phone away.

The following morning I wrote to Nadia. I didn't offer an explanation. I asked whether she would like to go and look at the site with me on Saturday. I told her I'd leave my phone in the car.

She said she would.

* * *

Helen invited a few of us to her house the day after her last day at Northline.

Her daughter was called Leah. She was very tall and spoke enthusiastically and at great length about an animal named Henry. I was halfway through a conversation about Henry when I established that Henry was the horse.

Helen had made a tremendous quantity of food. Peter brought his wife and son, and Miriam arrived with flowers. For a little while the evening was like any other evening at which a group of people gathered to say goodbye to somebody who was going to do something else.

Then Miriam had to leave.

I went outside with Helen. There were two chairs on the patio. We sat down.

"Are you excited?" I asked.

"Very."

"What are you teaching?"

"American history."

"That's good."

"And a class called Contemporary Issues."

"Oh, dear."

"Yeah. I don't know what they're going to do with me."

She laughed.

I asked her about the other applicants.

"I gave the report to the review team," she said. "They've agreed to look at the cases. I've asked them to tell me when they're finished."

"Will they?"

"I don't know."

We sat there for a while.

"Adrian, can I tell you something?"

"Of course."

"That first day, when I called you, I thought you were going to be quite unpleasant."

"Why?"

"You seemed like somebody who'd written some very long emails."

I laughed.

"I have written some very long emails."

"I know."

She looked at me.

"Anyway, I'm glad I called you."

"Me too."

Through the window we could see Peter's little boy standing at the table, trying to get a piece of cake onto a fork. Leah came over and helped him.

Helen watched them.

"I don't think I'm going to be a very different person at the school from the person I was before I left it," she said.

"No?"

"No. But there'll be some different people there."

She got up.

"Come on. Let's see if there's any cake left."

* * *

The article appeared on Monday.

It wasn't a particularly flattering article about Vincent's company, but it wasn't particularly damning either. Elise had included his response, my objection, and a statement from Elliot. There were some paragraphs about the difficulties of evaluating increasingly complex systems. At the end she described the experiment Beatrice and I had been conducting.

There was a link to our project.

I received a number of messages over the course of the day. One was from a mathematician in Canada who wanted to see the details of the proof. Another was from somebody who was convinced that we had invented the entire result. A third came from a man who wanted to give us a great deal of money.

I called him in the evening.

His name was Malcolm Reed. He'd made money developing software for the logistics industry and had recently begun funding research projects that, as far as I could establish, bore very little relation to the logistics industry.

"I read your thing," he said.

"The article?"

"No. The document on your website about what counts as a discovery."

"Oh."

"There's a paragraph in there about who evaluates the results."

"Right."

"I think that's interesting. I'd like to see somebody build a place that does that."

"Well, we're trying to."

"How much would it cost?"

I gave him a rough account of what Simon and I had discussed. I explained that we were just getting started and that we were trying to avoid expanding too quickly.

"Okay," he said.

He suggested we get together. He was going to be in New Mexico in a couple of weeks.

"Why New Mexico?" I asked.

"I live there."

"Oh."

"Come out here. Bring your friend if you want."

"Simon?"

"Sure."

We talked for a little longer. He told me about a problem his father had spent a great deal of time working on in the last years of his life. It was not a mathematical problem. His father had been ill, and had wanted to know how to make certain that the people caring for him knew what he wanted, even on the days when he couldn't tell them.

"I've been looking at a lot of the things people are building in that area," Malcolm said.

"Okay."

"And there's a question I have about it that I can't seem to get a good answer to."

"What's that?"

"If you build something that can tell you what a person wants when the person can't tell you what they want, how do you know when you ought to stop listening to it?"

I thought for a while.

"I don't know," I said.

"Well, that's a good start."

After we hung up I called Simon.

"How do you feel about Santa Fe?" I asked.

"Depends. Is there a building?"

"I don't know."

"Well, find out."
