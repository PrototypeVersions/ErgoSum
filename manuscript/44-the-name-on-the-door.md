## Forty-four — The Name on the Door

On a Monday in May, Celia received an offer from a university in California.

She told me about it in the kitchen. I was trying to open a package of coffee with a pair of scissors.

"That's wonderful," I said.

"Thanks."

"When would you start?"

"August."

"Oh."

I put the scissors down.

"Are you going to take it?"

"I think so."

We talked about the job. There would be students, and colleagues whose work she'd been interested in for some time. The university had agreed to provide some support for a project she'd been hoping to undertake. She would be able to spend a good deal of time working on questions that hadn't been questions at Open Question until she'd come to work there.

I told her I thought she ought to take it.

She smiled.

"I wasn't actually going to ask you whether I ought to."

"Right."

"I was going to ask whether you'd like to work on the project."

"Oh."

She laughed.

"Well?"

"Yes."

"Good."

She took the package of coffee away from me and opened it.

* * *

A week later we had a meeting about the future of the institute.

The funding situation had improved. June's work with the Canadian group had gone very well, and there were several other organizations interested in using the software. A charitable foundation in Europe had offered to support some of our mathematical research. Asha and Peter had obtained funding for a joint project with researchers in Edinburgh.

There were two rather serious problems.

First, the new arrangements didn't provide for the kind of organization we were currently operating. The money was intended principally for the people undertaking the particular pieces of work. We could use some of it for common expenses, but not enough to maintain a building of our own and all the things associated with it.

Second, I had a fairly strong preference for maintaining a building of our own and all the things associated with it.

Fatima put a document on the table.

She had worked out the cost of continuing in the building for another two years. Then she had worked out the cost of an arrangement under which we would lease a smaller space, work with researchers at other institutions and provide some of the common resources online.

"Well," I said, "where would we have the meetings?"

"In a room."

"Whose room?"

"That depends."

I looked around.

There were several people sitting at the table who had quite a lot of work to do. I had wanted them to have a place where they could do the work. I was now in the peculiar position of having to explain why I wanted them to use some of the money they had acquired for the work in order that I might continue to be able to see them doing it.

June looked at the document.

"I could do most of my stuff anywhere," she said.

Peter nodded.

Beth's job in Austin had worked out very well. He had been spending much of his time there, and there were a number of things about the arrangement that he liked.

Beatrice wanted to continue her work from Albuquerque.

Celia was going to California.

I asked Fatima what she'd like to do.

"Well," she said, "I've had an idea."

She'd been talking to some people about establishing a small organization that would help independent research groups obtain the kinds of administrative and technical support that were ordinarily available only to considerably larger organizations.

There were a number of things she'd discovered how to do during the preceding two years. She thought it would be nice if the next person starting an institute didn't have to discover how to do all of them.

"Would you want to work with us?" I asked.

"Of course. I'd like to work with quite a few people."

I looked at her.

"Adrian," she said, "I think this place might have been a very good place for me to start."

It was the first time she'd described the institute in those terms.

* * *

The difficult conversation was the one I had with Simon.

We went to look at the other Adrian's radio. It had recently developed a problem that Simon regarded as interesting and the other Adrian regarded as annoying.

Afterward we went to get coffee.

I told Simon about Fatima's proposal.

"Sounds good," he said.

"Yeah."

He looked at me.

"What's the matter?"

"I don't know."

"Okay."

He opened a little packet of sugar.

"I suppose I'd quite like it to go on being the place we wanted to build."

"Yeah."

He poured the sugar into his coffee.

"Do you remember that first time we went to look at the building?"

"Yeah."

"There was a guy making lamps."

"Yeah."

"Did you ever wonder what happened to him?"

"Occasionally."

"He's got a place in Carrollton. I went up there a couple of weeks ago."

"Really?"

"Yeah. Bought a lamp."

He showed me a photograph of the lamp.

It was a nice lamp.

"He's got about twice as much space as he had in our place," Simon said.

"Oh."

"Seems pretty happy."

I looked at the photograph again.

Simon put his phone away.

"Look," he said. "I'm going to miss the place, too."

"Yeah?"

"Of course. I spent about a year trying to get that sink to work."

"It wasn't a year."

"Seemed like a year."

He laughed.

Then he asked me what I'd most like to do if I weren't spending a substantial portion of each week trying to obtain money for a building.

I had a fairly good answer to that question.

I told him.

He listened.

"Well," he said, "that's what I thought we were trying to arrange."

* * *

We didn't close Open Question.

We decided to change it.

There was going to be a period of transition, during which we would make arrangements for the projects, the people and the obligations we'd acquired. We would continue to have an independent board. There would still be funding for work that wasn't readily accommodated at other institutions. We would have a much smaller permanent staff. Researchers could participate in the work from places where they already had fairly good reasons to be.

We obtained an agreement with the landlord concerning the end of the lease.

We also had some arrangements to make concerning my own employment.

I had been offered a position at a university in Texas. There would be a certain amount of teaching, a considerable amount of time for research, and the possibility of continuing some of the work we'd been doing through Open Question. The terms were not quite as favorable as the terms of the appointment I'd been offered at Northline several years earlier.

They were considerably more favorable than anything I'd been able to obtain before Helen called me.

I went to the university to talk to some of the people I'd be working with.

There was a woman in the philosophy department named Judith who'd spent much of her life working on questions concerning the nature of explanation. She wanted to know whether I'd be interested in teaching a course with her.

"On what?" I asked.

"I don't know yet."

"Oh."

"I thought we could work that out."

"That'd be wonderful."

She took me to look at an office.

It had a window.

"Would this be all right?" she asked.

"Yeah."

There was a little bookshelf, a desk and two chairs. There was a place on the wall where I could put the photograph from Claire's studio.

I looked out of the window.

I thought I would be able to do some good work there.

* * *

Malcolm came to Dallas at the beginning of June.

I showed him the arrangements we'd made. He read the documents and asked a number of questions. He wanted to know whether we had provided adequately for the people whose employment would be affected by the changes. He wanted to know whether the people who'd contributed money for particular projects had agreed to the relevant arrangements.

I told him what we had done and what remained to be done.

He nodded.

"Well," he said, "that's good."

I hadn't known what he was going to say.

I told him this.

"What did you think I was going to say?" he asked.

"I don't know. I thought you might be disappointed."

"About what?"

"Well, you gave us the money to build a research institute."

"Yeah."

"And it's going to be a rather different research institute."

He thought about that.

"Did you find out anything?"

"Quite a few things."

"Good."

He smiled.

"I didn't give you the money to establish that it was a good idea to give you the money."

I laughed.

We went to get lunch.

He told me that his daughter had been trying to persuade him to go on a trip with her. She wanted to spend some time in a place in which he had no particular interest. He had been thinking about going.

"Do you think you'll go?" I asked.

"Yeah."

"Good."

He looked at me.

"She says I don't have to bring any books."

"Oh."

"I think I'll bring one."

* * *

On the last Friday in the building, we had a party.

There were several boxes on the floor of my office. The plant had been taken home by Fatima. The four lamps had been given to people who wanted them. Simon had finally identified the thing we'd been keeping in the room that subsequently became Celia's office.

It was a part of a machine for making lamps.

He was going to take it to Carrollton.

The table was the last thing we had to find a place for.

Nadia had suggested giving it to the workshop. Curtis had come over to look at it and said it would be useful. He was bringing a truck the following morning.

I sat down at the table with June.

"Are you going to be around next week?" she asked.

"Yeah."

"Good. I want to show you something."

"Can't you show me now?"

"No. It's not ready."

"Okay."

She went to get another drink.

I looked around the room.

There were several people talking to Beatrice about something. Peter was showing somebody a picture of James. Celia had brought her grandmother, who was standing beside the table telling Malcolm a story about a bookstore.

Nadia came over and sat beside me.

"How's it going?" she asked.

"Pretty well."

"Good."

She looked toward the entrance.

"Don't forget the sign."

The little wooden question mark was still on the wall.

I went over to take it down.

It took me a while. One of the screws was difficult to get out, and I had to go and find a screwdriver.

When I finally got it off the wall, there was a little rectangle where the color of the wall was different from the color of the wall around it.

I showed it to Nadia.

"Do you want me to paint that?" I asked.

"Leave it," she said. "They're going to paint the whole place."

I put the sign in my bag.

Then I went back to the party.
