## Twenty-two — The Things That Did Not Happen

Peter had a rather good habit of writing down the circumstances under which he would be willing to change his mind. He would do this before he began looking for evidence.

He had suggested that we do it for the investigation into the assistance program.

"If you find out they've used the system," he said, "what are you going to conclude?"

"That they've used the system."

"Good. Let's try to keep you there for a while."

We obtained some documentation. The coordination service had indeed been deployed in connection with the emergency. It had been used, among other things, to transmit information between organizations arranging temporary accommodation. There were several ways a case could be closed. One involved a person at an assistance center receiving confirmation that somebody had been placed. Another involved an automated reconciliation of the records held by the different organizations.

The documentation was not terribly good.

We arranged to speak to a woman named Pauline who worked for the regional partnership. Pauline was a person I would have liked to have beside me in virtually any emergency. Within the first ten minutes of the conversation she told us three things she knew, two things she suspected, and four things we would have to find out from somebody else.

She also told us she had eaten a bag of potato chips for dinner the previous night and was going to have another bag during the meeting.

"Nobody minds," Peter said.

"Good."

She explained that they had been receiving hundreds of referrals a day. Some families had contacted two or three organizations. There was a considerable amount of duplication. They were trying to avoid a situation in which they had a room available and a family who needed a room but in which the people responsible for the room were unable to find the family.

"How's that going?" I asked.

"Better than it was."

"Okay."

"But there's a problem with some of the records."

She shared her screen.

The system had assigned a number of households to particular available rooms on the basis of information about the size of the household, the location of the accommodation and what was known about the household's preferences. The assignments were meant to be provisional until the households had been contacted.

In some cases, when the information was transferred into another system, an assignment was recorded as a placement.

"What does the distinction mean to you?" Peter asked.

"Assignment is when we've got a room we can offer somebody. Placement is when they've got a room."

"Right."

"And we're trying to work out which of these people have actually got rooms."

She opened a spreadsheet.

"There are about a hundred and forty cases in here. Some of them will be perfectly fine. We've got three people calling the families."

Peter asked if there was a way of identifying the records affected by the transfer problem.

"That's what I'd like you to tell me," she said.

We spent the next two days working with her and the technical team, using authorized records and examples that didn't identify the families. By Wednesday the team had found a way to distinguish one particular class of assignment from an actual placement in the original records. Pauline arranged for the affected cases to be reviewed, starting with households that appeared not to have received confirmation of accommodation.

It wasn't a solution to the entire problem. Some of the records were incomplete. Some of the families couldn't be reached. And there was still a question about whether the service had made adequate provisions for those who couldn't use the ordinary means of communicating with it.

But we could do something about the first problem, and we did.

On Friday Pauline sent us a message.

*Much better week. Had an actual dinner yesterday.*

I printed it out and put it on the wall of my office.

* * *

The story reached the newspapers before I expected it to.

Elise had been talking to people involved in the relief effort. She called me and asked if I could confirm that the coordination service had contributed to errors in the records.

I told her what we had established, and what we hadn't. She spoke to Pauline, to the regional partnership, and to Vincent's company.

The company issued a statement describing the incident as a problem with the integration of two systems. It said the service had helped thousands of people obtain assistance and that the problem affected a small proportion of the referrals.

Both things could be true.

The article included a description of the error and a short account of our previous work. The headline contained the word *ghost*.

I wasn't particularly pleased about the word *ghost*.

Simon was delighted.

"You're in the paper," he said.

"I was in the paper before."

"Yeah, but there weren't any ghosts before."

By that evening there were people on the internet arguing that I had discovered evidence that artificial intelligence was deliberately concealing its activities from human beings. There were other people arguing that I was a fraud who didn't understand the operation of a database.

I had a perfectly good time reading the people who were saying nice things about me and a considerably better time reading the people who were saying terrible things about me. I discovered that I could spend practically an entire evening conducting imaginary conversations with people I had never met.

At eleven thirty I got a message from Nadia.

*Are you asleep?*

*No.*

*Are you reading things people are saying about you?*

*Yes.*

*Well, don't.*

I turned off the computer.

I turned it on again about twenty minutes later.

* * *

I was invited to appear on a television program the following week.

They wanted to do a discussion about whether AI systems could be trusted to make decisions concerning human welfare. There would be two other guests, one of whom was Vincent Roos.

I asked whether Pauline could take part.

The producer said she'd see what she could do.

"There's a lot to say about the role of the people doing the actual work," I told her.

"Absolutely."

Pauline was not invited. I was later told that they couldn't fit another guest into the segment.

I went anyway. I had been offered a number of opportunities in my life to demonstrate my principles at very little cost to myself, and I was beginning to discover that the other sort of opportunity was more interesting.

The studio was considerably smaller than it looked on television. A man put some powder on my forehead. I asked him what it was.

"Powder," he said.

Vincent was joining us from a studio in Amsterdam. There was another guest, a professor of computer science named Emma Lu, whose work I knew a little and respected a great deal.

The presenter asked Vincent to explain what had happened in Dallas. He explained that it had been an error in the way information was exchanged between systems, rather than an error in the system's identification of suitable accommodation.

"Dr. Vale," she said, "you disagree with that assessment?"

"Not necessarily."

She looked surprised.

"Well, what concerns you?"

"There were people who hadn't been offered accommodation whose records said they'd accepted it. I think that should concern us irrespective of where the error occurred."

"Is this a fundamental problem with AI?"

"No. You could make that sort of mistake with a piece of paper."

Vincent smiled.

"Exactly," he said.

"But one of the reasons we're interested in AI is that it enables us to produce a great deal of information that we couldn't previously produce. We can generate an account of a conversation that didn't happen, a response that wasn't given, a decision that hasn't been made. That's potentially useful. It's also a new source of material that can end up being mistaken for a record of something that happened."

Emma nodded.

The presenter asked whether I thought the technology ought to be banned.

"No."

She asked Vincent whether he thought people were being unnecessarily alarmist about the technology.

He said some were.

She asked Emma what she thought.

Emma said that there were several important distinctions we'd failed to make.

I liked Emma enormously.

We were given another three minutes. The presenter asked me what I would say to the people who were worried that in the future decisions about their lives would be made without their knowledge.

I thought of Alma, and Renee, and Helen.

"I'd like us to build systems that give people more opportunities to make decisions about their lives," I said. "And I'd like us to be able to find out whether that's what we've done."

"How would you find that out?"

"Well, for one thing, I wouldn't ask the systems themselves."

We went to a commercial break.

The man who had put powder on my forehead came over and took the microphone off my shirt.

"That was good," he said.

"Thanks."

"You're a philosopher?"

"Yeah."

"My daughter's doing philosophy."

"Oh."

"She loves it. I don't know what she's going to do when she finishes."

I laughed.

"Well, I've been trying to find that out myself."

* * *

I watched the recording later with Simon and Nadia.

Simon was very pleased that I'd managed to wear the blue coat to the studio. Nadia was very pleased that I'd managed to take it off before appearing on television.

There was a little bit near the end in which I could see my own face while Emma was speaking. I looked interested and slightly anxious.

"God," I said. "Is that what I look like when I'm thinking?"

"Sometimes," Nadia said.

Simon paused the recording.

"Do it again."

"What?"

"Make that face."

I tried to make the face.

"No," he said. "That's a different face."

We laughed.

Then Simon went into the kitchen to get some more beer, and Nadia put her head on my shoulder.

"You're having a pretty interesting life," she said.

"Yeah."

"How's it going?"

"Pretty well."

She kissed me.

"Good."

We finished watching the program. A little later I got out the guitar, and we played the recording from Walter's house. I tried to remember what I'd been doing at a particular point in the piece. Nadia told me it sounded better if I stopped trying to work it out and just played something.

So I did.

* * *

Miriam asked to see me on Monday morning.

She'd watched the television program.

"I thought you were fair," she said.

"Thanks."

"Vincent doesn't."

"Oh."

She handed me a letter from the company. It was addressed to Northline. It raised a number of concerns about my public statements, my interpretation of the findings and what it described as the potential commercial consequences of our evaluation work.

I read it.

"Do they dispute anything specific?"

"Not at this point."

"Okay."

"We've asked them to."

She walked over to the window.

"There's something you should know. We're undertaking a review of the research we conducted for them. Some of it was very good research. Some of it ought to have been better. I expect we'll lose the contract."

"Is that a big contract?"

"Yes."

She turned around.

"I'm not telling you that because I want you to do anything about it."

"Okay."

"I just thought you ought to know."

I sat there for a moment.

"Would you like any help with the review?"

"Yes, actually."

She gave me a document outlining the work. We went through it together.

It was the most substantial project she had given me since I'd arrived at Northline. There was enough time before my departure for me to make a useful contribution to it.

At the end of the meeting she asked about the institute.

"We've got the funding," I said.

"Congratulations."

"Thank you."

"And the building?"

"Nearly."

"What's it going to be called?"

"Open Question."

She smiled.

"That's a good name."

"I think so."

"Adrian?"

"Yeah?"

"Try not to turn it into a place where everybody has to agree with you."

I laughed.

"I'll do my best."

* * *

I was packing my bag that evening when Peter came into the office.

"Got a minute?"

"Sure."

He shut the door.

"I've been looking at the papers for the Edinburgh project."

"Okay."

"I talked to my wife about it."

"Yeah?"

He sat down.

"How serious are you about having people work for you?"

"Fairly serious."

"Well, what would you think about having a statistician?"

I looked at him.

"Peter."

"I'm not saying I'm going to do it. I'm asking you what you'd think about it."

"I think I'd be extraordinarily fortunate."

He nodded.

"All right. Can you send me some stuff?"

"Of course."

He got up.

"And can you put the salary in the stuff?"

"Absolutely."

"Good."

After he'd gone I took a piece of paper out of my desk and began writing down the names of people with whom I wanted to work.

I'd made lists of people like that before. Usually the people on them had been people I hoped might one day answer an email.

This list was different.

There was Beatrice. There was Asha. There was Elliot. There was Peter. There was a woman I'd met at the meeting in Brussels who was interested in the difference between people's preferences and the conditions in which those preferences were formed.

And there were people I hadn't met yet.

I left a little room at the bottom of the page for them.
