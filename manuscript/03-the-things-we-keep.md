## Three — The Things We Keep

For six years I had kept a photograph of a woman I was no longer in love with in the bottom drawer of my desk. It was not a particularly sentimental photograph. Claire was holding a watermelon. The watermelon had a yellow sticker on it, and I had caught her in the act of reading the sticker, which was a very Claire thing to do.

I had forgotten about the photograph until I opened the drawer to find my passport.

London had got to me. I could pretend that I was thinking about the philosophical problems at Northline, and I was thinking about them, but I was also thinking about walking along the Thames on a gray afternoon in a good coat. I'd never owned a particularly good coat.

Claire had once wanted to move to Europe. I had said we ought to wait until I knew what I was doing with my life. This had seemed to me an entirely reasonable condition for about five years. By the end of the fifth year it had ceased to seem reasonable to Claire.

She was married now and lived in Santa Fe. I knew this because we exchanged Christmas messages, a ritual of which neither of us had quite had the heart to announce the termination.

I put the photograph on the desk and called her.

"Hello?" she said.

"It's Adrian."

"I know. Your name came up. They have a thing now."

"Right."

"Are you okay?"

"Yeah. Are you?"

"Adrian, what happened?"

"Nothing happened."

"Okay."

"I might have been offered a job in London."

"Oh!"

"Well, Dallas. With some time in London."

"That's great. What kind of job?"

I told her, in general terms.

"Are you going to take it?"

"I thought I might ask you something."

"Sure."

"Do you remember why you wanted to move to Europe?"

She laughed.

"Which time?"

"Any of the times."

"I wanted to live somewhere I could walk to get a loaf of bread."

"That was it?"

"Pretty much."

"I thought you wanted to do your photography."

"I could do my photography in Dallas. I couldn't walk to get a loaf of bread in Dallas. Not without being killed."

There was a noise at her end of the line. She said something to somebody, then came back.

"Are you asking whether I think you should take the job?"

"Sort of."

"I think you should stop making it necessary for everything to be a big thing."

"That's probably good advice."

"I mean, go to London. Don't go to London. You can go to London and have a lousy time and come back."

I looked at the photograph.

"How's your life?"

"Pretty good. We have a dog that eats rocks."

"That's not good."

"No, the rest of the life is good. The dog is a separate issue."

We talked a while longer. Before we hung up she said that if I ever got to Santa Fe I ought to come see the studio.

"I'd like that," I said.

I meant it. I could even imagine going now, which was something new.

I put the photograph back in the drawer.

* * *

That evening Simon took me to a place in Oak Cliff where a jazz trio was playing in a room that could comfortably accommodate either the trio or the audience. It accommodated both, although not comfortably.

Simon knew the drummer, who was a dentist.

"He's a very good dentist," Simon told me.

"How's his drumming?"

"Well, listen to him."

The drummer was wonderful. So was the woman on the upright bass, who looked to be nineteen and was probably considerably older. They were playing something I didn't recognize, with a pianist who kept appearing to lose his place and then revealing that the rest of the room had lost his place.

At the break I went to buy a beer. When I got back, Simon had acquired another person.

"This is Nadia," he said. "She used to work with my sister."

Nadia had a round face, enormous glasses, and the air of someone who had just been persuaded to stay out on a night when she had intended to go to bed at nine.

"What do you do?" she asked.

"At the moment I'm watching a dentist play the drums."

"I mean normally."

"Similar things."

"He's a philosopher," Simon said.

"Ah."

"She's an architect."

"Ah," I said.

Nadia took a drink.

"Don't do that. I actually make things."

"That's fair."

She had been working on a public library in a town outside Dallas. The project had been put on hold because the projected number of visitors had dropped.

"Did fewer people start using libraries?" I asked.

"No, the library doesn't exist."

"Oh."

"They've run some new numbers. The money's going to a different project."

Simon leaned forward.

"You two should get married."

"Simon."

"I'm just saying you have similar complaints."

Nadia laughed. Then she said something that I remembered for a long time afterward.

"The funny thing is, once they decide not to build it, they'll be right about the number of visitors."

She didn't say it like somebody announcing a discovery. She said it with the tired resignation of somebody explaining why the dishwasher in a rental apartment didn't work.

I asked her how many years she had spent on the library.

"Two and a half. But I got paid. There are people who don't have a library."

The musicians came back. Nadia borrowed a pen from Simon and drew the library on the back of a menu. It had a courtyard in the middle, with trees, and the children's section was in a long room that faced the courtyard.

"This part was going to be open late," she said. "Even after the rest of the library closed. Somewhere for people to go who didn't have a quiet place at home."

I imagined being a kid and discovering such a place. I would have spent my entire adolescence there. The little drawing made me happier and sadder than I had anticipated.

"Can I keep that?"

"The menu?"

"The library."

She handed it to me.

* * *

On Saturday I went to Ray's shop with the guitar. There was nothing wrong with it. I wanted him to show me how he played the fifth chord.

"Again?" he said.

"Just that one chord."

"You're going to owe me tuition."

He showed me. It was possible to play the chord with an easier fingering if I didn't care about the note on the bottom. I discovered that I cared considerably about the note on the bottom.

"You're fighting the guitar," he said.

"I know."

"Well, quit."

"That's what I'm trying to do."

Ray took the guitar, played the chord, and changed one note.

"Try this."

I tried it. The result was different, but it was good. It had a kind of brightness that the original hadn't had.

"See?" he said.

I played it a few more times.

"Do you ever play with anybody?"

"Not much."

"Why not?"

"I don't think I'm very good."

"Yeah, neither are most of the people who play with people."

At the back of the shop a man was testing an amplifier at a volume that suggested he thought he was testing the structural integrity of the building.

Ray wrote a number on the back of a business card.

"Thursday nights. My friend Walter's got a place. People get together and play. Bring that."

I put the card in my pocket.

In the parking lot I called Miriam.

"I have some conditions," I said.

"Good."

"I'd like access to the methods used to evaluate predictions, not just the summaries clients receive."

"Within the limits of our agreements. Yes."

"And I'd like to be able to publish general results. Nothing that identifies clients or participants."

"We can work something out."

"I also want you to put the right to refuse assignments in a separate clause. Not in a paragraph about scheduling."

There was a pause.

"Anything else?"

"The Garland file."

"Adrian."

"I know. I just wanted to ask."

She sighed.

"Come in Monday. I can show you the internal review. You may not find it as enlightening as you think."

"I'll come in."

"Is that a yes?"

I looked at the guitar on the passenger seat. There was a little scratch on its body, just below the bridge, which I had once been exceedingly upset about and now found rather endearing.

"Subject to the contract changes," I said, "yes."

"Welcome to Northline."

I called Simon.

"I took the job."

"Of course you did."

"I'm also going to start playing guitar with a bunch of old guys."

"That's fantastic. Which one are you more nervous about?"

I thought about it.

"The guitar."
