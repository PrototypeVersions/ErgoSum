## Five — The Correction

"Well," I said, "that's encouraging. I suppose."

Helen sat down.

"Do you know what a disposition is?"

"Depends who's asking."

"At Northline."

"No."

She explained that when a candidate was recruited, a number of things could happen to the file. The candidate could be referred for further consideration, could withdraw, could accept an appointment. There was a little menu containing all the possible things that could happen to a person in the course of applying for a job.

"There used to be a distinction between a projected disposition and an actual one," she said. "There are still two fields in the database. But what I see in my portal is one field. And the system seems to have developed ideas about which of the two I ought to be seeing."

"Who decided that?"

"I don't know. I put in a ticket."

"How'd that go?"

"They asked me to attach a screenshot."

"Did you?"

"Adrian, it was your interview transcript. I was supposed to close out your file."

I got up and closed the blinds on the glass partition between my office and the hallway. I had never had occasion to close blinds on a glass partition before, and I was a little self-conscious doing it.

"Why didn't you?"

"Because I knew you hadn't been interviewed. I'd been trying to arrange the interview all week."

"But you sent me the first one anyway."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Well, I tried the conventional channels first. And the only other person involved in the interview who seemed to be available to me was you."

I thought about this.

"So you called me to discuss my decision."

"Yes."

"To establish that I hadn't made it."

"Yes."

"You could have just asked."

"I know. It was rather a good transcript, though, wasn't it?"

"It made me sound much cleverer than I am."

"Oh, I wouldn't say that. You spent about a quarter of it refusing to answer quite simple questions."

I laughed.

Then she told me something else.

"You aren't the first person I've done this for."

There had been a physicist in Massachusetts, a computer scientist in Toronto, and a young man in New Mexico who had applied for a fellowship. She had managed to contact the first two. The physicist had actually decided to withdraw, for reasons Helen didn't know. The computer scientist hadn't and was furious.

"And the young man?"

"His email address didn't work."

"Did you call him?"

"There was no phone number."

"What's his name?"

She shook her head.

"I'm already telling you things I'm not supposed to tell you."

"You don't have to give me the name. Can we establish how many there are?"

"I've asked for a report."

"Good."

"Don't be too pleased. I've asked for a number of reports in my life."

She stood up. I opened the blinds again.

"One other thing," she said.

"Yeah?"

"I really would appreciate it if you'd do well here."

For some reason this struck me as a more generous thing to say than the customary expression of confidence in a new colleague's abilities. I told her I'd try.

After she'd left, I sat down and took out the contract. I had signed it that morning, after Miriam agreed to my conditions. I read the clause about publishing. Then I read the clause about refusing work.

They were good clauses. There was something almost comical about the fact that, for the first time in my professional life, I was in possession of a document that went to considerable trouble to establish that I was allowed to think.

* * *

On Tuesday morning Peter and I went through some of the actual and simulated interviews. We chose twenty questions from the pilot study and tried to come up with a defensible way of determining whether an answer to each one was correct.

This was a surprisingly pleasant way to spend a morning. I had forgotten what it was like to be in a room with another person who regarded disagreement as a form of entertainment.

"All right," Peter said. "The actual person says she'd be willing to go up to twenty percent. The simulation says fifteen."

"Fifteen percent of what?"

"Her income."

"Does she say that?"

He read the question again.

"No."

"Then we have a very accurate prediction of an answer to a question we didn't ask."

"You are going to make my life difficult."

"I'm available until five."

At eleven we went down to a little cafeteria where it was possible to buy an apple for three dollars. Peter bought an apple. I bought a second coffee. Outside, a crew of men in yellow vests was stringing Christmas lights around a tree that hadn't yet finished being a fall tree.

Peter told me that he had spent the previous weekend at a children's museum.

"There's this thing where you put a ball in a tube and the air carries it all the way up to the ceiling. James did it for forty minutes."

"Is that your son?"

"Yeah. Forty minutes. The exact same thing."

"Well, maybe he was checking reproducibility."

"That's what I said to my wife."

He smiled at his apple.

"Can I ask you something unrelated?"

"Sure."

"If I generated a transcript of my son at thirty, would you read it?"

"Probably."

"Would you want to read yours?"

"At thirty? I'd rather have a transcript of the next few years."

"I mean if you had a kid."

I thought for a moment.

"No," I said.

"Why not?"

"Because I'd start thinking of him as someone I already knew."

Peter nodded. He put the apple down.

"Yeah," he said. "That's about where I've got to."

We went back upstairs.

* * *

There was a woman named Irene in the first group of volunteers. Her interview was unusual because it contained a number of questions that the interviewer had not written down beforehand. At some point she had grown curious about the experiment and had begun interviewing the interviewer.

We had an audio recording. Peter played it for me.

"Are you going to make one of yourself?" Irene asked.

The interviewer said he thought he might.

"Are you going to tell your wife?"

He laughed.

"Well, I don't know."

"Don't let her find out from one of these things."

Irene had a good laugh. She laughed with an abrupt little intake of breath that seemed to surprise her as much as the people around her.

The simulated interview was interesting in a different way. Some of her answers were very close to the actual ones, others were astonishingly bad. She had mentioned a grandson in the original interview and the simulation had given her three grandchildren.

At the bottom of the final page, beneath the interviewer's last question, was an annotation.

*Participant requested additional interaction.*

Peter explained that Irene was the woman who had wanted to talk to her simulated counterpart.

"Can I listen to that?"

He found another recording.

The voice on the other end was synthetic and didn't sound like Irene. It sounded like a particularly capable receptionist.

At first she asked questions about her own life. It got a few things right and a few things wrong. She corrected its account of a holiday in New Orleans. She wanted to know what it had been told about her brother.

Then, about nine minutes in, she asked it what she ought to do with a piano.

"The upright," she said. "Do you remember the upright?"

The voice said that it did.

She explained that she had been thinking about selling it. She was moving to a smaller apartment, and the piano was something that had belonged to her husband. The voice advised her that the piano mattered to her and that she ought to keep it if she possibly could.

"That's just the trouble," Irene said. "I can't."

The voice offered some practical suggestions. A storage unit, a friend who might have room. Irene had considered those things. She was quiet for a while.

Then she said, very softly, "What do you think Harold would say?"

There was a pause. Peter reached for the mouse.

"Wait," I said.

"I don't particularly like this bit."

"Okay."

He let it play.

The voice said, "I think Harold would tell you that you don't need to keep the piano in order to keep him."

Irene began to cry.

The recording ended about a minute later. Neither of us said anything.

Finally I asked, "What did she say afterward?"

"She said it was the most helpful thing anyone had said to her in months."

"Oh."

"And then she asked us never to make another one of her."

We were quiet again.

"I don't know how to score that," Peter said.

"Me neither."

I asked if he knew whether she'd sold the piano.

He didn't.

For the rest of the afternoon I kept returning to an idea that was unpleasantly resistant to the form I was trying to give it. We had been speaking as if the simulation were an instrument for discovering a person's preferences. But here was a woman who had gone into a room with one set of preferences and come out, perhaps, with a different set.

Maybe she had changed her mind because of something that had happened to her.

In fact I couldn't think of a single occasion on which I had changed my mind that hadn't involved something happening to me.

I crossed out a line in my notebook.

At home that night I went into the bottom drawer of my desk and got out the photograph of Claire holding the watermelon. I put it in an envelope with some other old photographs and put the envelope on a shelf.

I still wanted to keep it.

I just didn't think I needed to keep it there.
