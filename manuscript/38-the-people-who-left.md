## Thirty-eight — The People Who Left

By October, Celia had acquired an office.

It was a room in which we had previously stored some boxes, an old printer and a thing that none of us could identify but that Simon thought we shouldn't throw away.

Celia had put a desk in the room. Above the desk she'd put up a photograph of a woman standing beside an enormous pile of books.

"Who's that?" I asked.

"My grandmother."

"Did she read all those?"

"No. She was moving a bookstore."

I looked at the photograph.

"That's a lot of books."

"Yeah."

She sat down at the desk.

"Have you got a minute?"

"Sure."

"Come and look at something."

* * *

It had taken us most of the summer to arrive at an agreement concerning the study.

The work would be conducted by a group of researchers from several institutions. The participants would be adults who wanted assistance in exploring educational and professional possibilities. Continuance would supply one of the systems. Open Question would supply another. There would also be a comparison group that received access to the same collection of opportunities through a relatively simple search interface.

We had spent a great deal of time establishing what the systems would be permitted to do, what information would be collected and who would have access to it.

The company could examine the findings after the independent analysis had been completed, but it couldn't prevent their publication.

Vincent had agreed to this.

I was impressed that he'd agreed to it.

He had also insisted that we include a measure of the extent to which the participants accomplished the things they had said they wanted to accomplish at the beginning of the study.

Celia had agreed to this.

The two of them had become quite fond of arguing with each other.

At one meeting Vincent asked whether we had been able to obtain the views of people who didn't use recommendation systems.

"Yes," Celia said.

"How did you find them?"

"We didn't use a recommendation system."

Vincent laughed.

It was the first time I'd seen him laugh at something without subsequently attempting to establish whether he agreed with it.

* * *

In the first stage of the study, a relatively small group of volunteers used the three interfaces for several weeks. We wanted to find out whether our procedures worked before asking a much larger group of people to undertake them.

There had been some interesting differences between the systems. There had also been some things I hadn't anticipated.

The system supplied by Continuance was extremely good at certain kinds of tasks.

A person who wanted to find an affordable course in a particular subject and had several restrictions concerning when and where they could take it was likely to have a better experience using Vincent's system than using ours. The company had spent years developing some of the relevant capabilities. We'd spent most of a year discussing the conditions under which the capabilities ought to be used.

I had occasionally forgotten the difference.

Peter showed me one of the preliminary summaries.

"We need to be careful," he said. "It's a pilot. The numbers aren't going to tell us very much yet."

"I know."

"But this is something we'd better understand."

He pointed to a figure.

A number of participants using our system had spent quite a lot of time exploring the material without making appreciable progress toward their original goals.

"Did they like it?" I asked.

"Some did. Some didn't."

"Okay."

He showed me an anonymous response from one of the volunteers.

*I already know what I want to do. I would appreciate it if the computer would stop being interested in whether I might want to do something else.*

I laughed.

Peter looked at me.

"It's quite a good criticism, actually."

"Yeah."

We sent the response to June.

She came over to the table a little later.

"Well," she said, "we can change that."

"Should we?" I asked.

"Adrian, if somebody tells you they don't want to keep getting suggestions about something, what do you normally do?"

I thought about it.

"Apparently I ask Peter whether they really mean it."

He laughed.

We went back to work.

* * *

The thing Celia wanted to show me that morning was a different sort of problem.

She had been examining the records of the volunteers who hadn't completed the pilot study.

People could stop participating at any point. They didn't have to give a reason. There were also a number of people who had continued using their assigned interface for a while but hadn't responded to the follow-up questionnaires.

We had arranged for an independent research team to offer those people an opportunity to take part in a brief interview, with no requirement that they return to the study.

Some of them had agreed.

Celia played me a short extract from a recording for which we had permission.

The volunteer was a man who had been considering retraining for a different occupation. He had been assigned to the Continuance system.

He'd had a very good experience with it. He described some of the things it had helped him do. It had identified a training program, helped him work through the information about the program, and assisted him in preparing some questions to ask the people who ran it.

Then, in the course of the investigation, he'd spoken to his brother.

His brother had told him that a man they both knew was retiring and that there might be an opportunity for him to learn a particular kind of work.

He'd gone to see the man.

He was going to begin working with him the following month.

"Why'd he leave the study?" I asked.

"Keep listening."

The interviewer asked him whether he'd found anything particularly frustrating about the system.

He said no.

Then he explained that he'd told the system about the possibility of working with the man. It had supplied some information about the risks associated with that kind of employment and had suggested that he continue investigating the training program until he'd made a decision.

He thought this was good advice.

He hadn't followed it.

He'd made his decision. He no longer needed the system.

The recording ended.

"Well," I said, "that's interesting."

"Yeah."

"Was he happy with what he'd decided to do?"

"So far."

"Good."

Celia opened another document.

It contained some of the responses from people who had stopped using our system.

There was a woman who'd discovered that she was eligible for a program she'd assumed she couldn't apply to. She'd gone to the website for the program and made the necessary arrangements. She hadn't come back to tell us what she'd done.

There was a man who didn't like our interface.

There was a woman who had found the entire experience rather overwhelming and had decided to talk to somebody at the community college instead.

And there was another volunteer who had discovered, through the simple search interface, a course that wasn't particularly well suited to anything she'd said about herself when she joined the study.

She was taking the course.

"What's the problem?" I asked.

"Well, we designed a study in which we're trying to establish how well a system works for somebody. And some of the ways in which a system might work for somebody involve their not using it anymore."

I looked at the document.

"Oh."

"And some of the ways in which it might not work for somebody involve their not using it anymore."

"Yeah."

She looked at me.

"So we have to be quite careful about what we do with all the records of the people who are left."

* * *

That afternoon we talked to Vincent.

He wasn't surprised that the systems were useful to people who subsequently stopped using them. His company had encountered the phenomenon in several of its products. He was concerned about the difficulty of obtaining reliable information about what happened to the people who stopped.

"Well, we share that concern," Asha said.

There were going to be limitations to the information we could obtain. We couldn't make people tell us about their lives. We wouldn't be able to determine whether the people who declined the interview had had the same kinds of experiences as the people who agreed to it.

Peter suggested several ways of making the uncertainty explicit in the eventual analysis.

Vincent asked whether there was anything we could do to improve the follow-up procedure.

Celia thought there was.

They spent the next twenty minutes discussing it.

At the end of the meeting Vincent asked me what I thought.

"I think it might be interesting to distinguish the question of whether the service has been successful from the question of whether it's got a good reason to continue being involved in a person's life."

He smiled.

"That's a rather awkward distinction for a company to make."

"I know."

"Do you think it's a distinction your institute ought to make?"

"Yes."

"Well, you might want to think about what would happen if the people who funded your institute came to the conclusion that you'd been successful and that there was no longer any reason for you to exist."

I laughed.

"Yeah."

"I mean it."

"I know."

When the meeting ended, I sat looking at the screen.

It was a good question.

I wished he'd asked me a different one.

* * *

That evening I went to a little restaurant with Nadia.

She was going to El Paso the following week, and there were some things we needed to discuss about the arrangements for the house. Her work on the border project had begun, and she was going to be spending rather more time away than either of us had anticipated.

We made a plan.

Then she showed me some photographs.

There was a little town in which the group of architects had been talking to people about a building they might be able to put up on a piece of publicly owned land.

"What do they want?" I asked.

"Different things."

"What sorts of things?"

"Well, one of the women wants a place where people can do their tax returns."

"Oh."

"And a man wants a place where he can teach people to dance."

"Okay."

"And there's a kid who wants a swimming pool."

"Are you going to build a swimming pool?"

"Probably not."

She put her phone down.

"But I want to find out a little more about what the kid wants to do."

We ordered dinner.

I told her about the people who'd left the study.

"Did you ever find out what happened to the people who stopped playing music at Walter's house?" she asked.

"No."

"Do people stop going?"

"Occasionally."

"Why?"

"Different reasons, I suppose."

"Do you think Walter worries about it?"

I thought about Walter.

He had been playing music with people for nearly fifty years. There were a number of people he'd played music with for a considerable portion of his life and didn't play music with anymore.

I thought he probably did worry about some of them.

I didn't think he'd have wanted to make it harder for them to leave.

* * *

When we got home, there was a message from June.

She had finished constructing the first version of a new research system using some of the material from the collaboration with Peregrine. It wasn't running yet. She wanted Beatrice and me to look at the arrangements before she began the experiment.

She'd given the system a temporary name.

*P2.*

Underneath the name was a description of the research state it would receive, the things it would be able to do, and the things we were going to ask it to investigate.

There was also a description of a control system that wouldn't receive the record of the earlier collaboration.

At the bottom of the message June had written:

*I don't think we should tell either system which one we hope will do better.*

I wrote back that I agreed.

Then I went into the music room.

I had a little recording from the wedding on my computer. Somebody had made it on a phone. The quality wasn't very good, but in the middle of the recording there was a short passage in which Elliot was playing the clarinet and Morag was playing the fiddle.

I played it several times.

At one point I could hear myself laughing.

I didn't remember why I'd laughed.

I went to get Nadia.

"Listen to this," I said.

She came into the room.

We listened to the recording together.

"That's you," she said.

"Yeah."

"Do you remember what happened?"

"No."

She laughed.

"I do."

And she told me.
