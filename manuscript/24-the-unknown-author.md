## Twenty-four — The Unknown Author

The problem Beatrice had written about twenty years earlier concerned the reconstruction of an object from a collection of incomplete descriptions of it.

"Imagine you've got a building," she said.

"Okay."

"And I've got a set of drawings of bits of the building. None of the drawings gives me the whole building. And I don't necessarily know which drawings belong to which bits."

"All right."

"Can I establish what the building looks like?"

"Not necessarily."

"Good. That's the first problem. Now suppose I can't establish what the building looks like. Can I establish something about all the buildings it could be?"

"Possibly."

"That's a much more interesting problem."

The actual objects were finite mathematical structures, and the descriptions were much more precisely specified than the drawings of the building. She'd proved some results about small cases when she was a graduate student. Her supervisor had conjectured that a certain procedure would work for an entire class of cases, but neither of them had been able to establish it.

"Did you publish any of it?"

"A little bit."

"Have other people worked on it?"

"Not in exactly that form. There are several related problems people work on all the time."

She showed me a notebook she'd brought from Albuquerque. There was an extraordinary amount of very small handwriting in it.

"God, I used to have good eyes," she said.

Peregrine had apparently recognized that the problem our system had been investigating could be translated into a particular version of her reconstruction problem. The translation wasn't particularly difficult to follow once somebody had supplied it. Neither of us would have thought of it.

"What's the significance?" I asked.

"Well, we have some machinery for investigating the new problem that we didn't have twenty years ago. And there's a condition here that might make a difference."

"Can we try it?"

"Yes."

She pointed at the screen.

"But I want to know where this person got the notes."

She sent a message asking.

We went to lunch.

When we got back there was a reply.

*My copy is dated October 2003. It was obtained from the collection of Professor Martin Eberly.*

"Martin was my supervisor," Beatrice said.

"Where is he?"

"Dead."

"Oh."

"He died about three years ago."

She looked down at the notebook.

"That's nice, actually. He kept my notes."

* * *

We began working with Peregrine.

There wasn't a great deal of correspondence in the conventional sense. The messages were brief and principally concerned mathematics. Occasionally Beatrice would send a question in the evening and there would be an answer by the time she got up the following morning. Occasionally there would be no answer for two or three days.

One afternoon I asked whether Peregrine would be willing to join us for a video call.

*I would prefer to continue in writing, if that is acceptable.*

I asked Beatrice whether she minded.

"No. I had a collaborator in Japan for eleven years before I met her."

"Eleven years?"

"Yeah. When I finally met her, the first thing she said to me was that she'd thought I was taller."

We continued in writing.

There were three fairly substantial obstacles to establishing the result we wanted. The first had to do with a case in which the procedure could produce an arrangement that wasn't among the arrangements we'd started with. The second had to do with showing that the procedure would terminate. The third was a problem I was unable to understand without looking at a particular drawing Beatrice had made.

Peregrine suggested a way of dealing with the first obstacle.

Beatrice dealt with the second.

Then, one morning, we received a document containing a different proof strategy. It didn't establish the full result, but it reduced the remaining difficulty to a statement about a much simpler structure.

Beatrice put the document on the big table and stood there reading it.

"That's beautiful," she said.

She said it in the tone of voice she ordinarily used to announce that somebody had parked in front of her garage.

June had been developing a formal version of parts of the argument. We sent her the document. She spent the rest of the day translating the new strategy into the proof environment we'd been using.

At about seven thirty I was about to leave. June was still sitting at her desk.

"Do you want me to get you some dinner?" I asked.

"No."

"Are you sure?"

"Actually, yes. I want you to get me some dinner."

"What?"

"Something that doesn't take long to get."

I went out and got sandwiches.

At nine June called me over.

She pointed at the screen.

"There."

"Does that work?"

"That part works."

"All of that?"

"All of that."

We stood there looking at it.

It was a formal verification of one of the central intermediate results. There were no unfilled gaps in that particular proof. We hadn't established the entire conjecture. We hadn't even established that the formal statement corresponded in all the relevant respects to the statement in Beatrice's notebook. But there was something on the screen that hadn't been there when the day began.

I called Beatrice.

She said a word I had never heard her say.

Then she told me she was getting on an airplane in the morning.

* * *

Nadia and I were supposed to leave for the coast on Friday.

It was Wednesday evening, and Beatrice was at the institute. There were now several sheets of paper spread out on the table, and there was a disagreement between Beatrice and June about one of the assumptions in the formalization. It was a disagreement I was very interested in.

At about six I went into the kitchen and called Nadia.

"I've got some news," I said.

"Okay."

"I think we might be fairly close to proving something."

"Oh, that's great."

"Yeah."

"Are you about to ask me something?"

I laughed.

"No."

"Good."

I told her what had happened.

She was very pleased. Then she told me she'd found a place near the beach where we could rent bicycles. I told her that sounded good.

I went back to the table.

"You're not going to be here Friday?" Beatrice asked.

"No."

"Good. Get out of here."

"What?"

"June and I are trying to do something. You're sitting here saying things like 'this is wonderful' every twenty minutes."

June laughed.

I went home.

* * *

There was a little porch outside the place where Nadia and I stayed. From the porch you could see a narrow strip of water between two buildings. If you stood up, you could see a somewhat wider strip of water.

The first morning we went swimming.

I hadn't been swimming in the ocean for a number of years. I had forgotten the peculiar pleasure of going out a little way and turning around and seeing all the things that had been around you a few minutes earlier from an entirely different position.

Nadia swam considerably farther out than I did. When she came back she had a little piece of seaweed on her shoulder.

"Don't tell me what it is," she said.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were looking at it in a philosophical manner."

I took it off her shoulder.

We spent the afternoon riding bicycles. I discovered that I was capable of riding a bicycle in a straight line, provided I didn't have to look at anything. Nadia found this hilarious.

At dinner we talked about the possibility of going to Spain the following year. She wanted to spend some time in a particular part of the country where she'd never been. I asked whether we could go without making any particular effort to see anything we were supposed to see.

"That's how I like to go places," she said.

"Good."

The waiter brought us another basket of bread.

"I've got a question for you," she said.

"What?"

"Would you ever want to live with me?"

I looked at her.

I hadn't been expecting the question at that moment. I had, at various other moments, been expecting the question. I had even been working out what I would say if she asked it.

"Yes," I said.

"Okay."

"Would you want to live with me?"

"Well, I'd have to find somewhere to put all those books."

"I could get rid of some of them."

She laughed.

"No, you couldn't."

She reached across the table and took my hand.

We didn't make any decisions about it that evening. We talked about the things we would have to think about. Her work, my work, the places we liked living, how much space each of us needed in order to be a person the other one liked living with.

It was one of the most pleasant conversations I'd ever had about things that might not work.

* * *

On Sunday morning I woke up before Nadia. I went out on the porch with a cup of coffee.

There was a message from Beatrice.

*We've got it.*

I looked at it.

There was a second message.

*June is running the checks again. Don't call. We're asleep.*

I sat down.

The sun was coming up. A man was walking along the street carrying two fishing rods. Somewhere behind the building a car door shut.

I wanted very much to call somebody.

I wanted to call Beatrice, and June, and Simon, and Elliot, and the man who had interviewed me at the university in Oregon. I wanted to find a newspaper and have them put something in it. I wanted to get on an airplane and go to Albuquerque and find the house where Beatrice had kept the notebook.

Instead I sat there drinking coffee.

After a little while Nadia came out.

"Morning."

"Morning."

She looked at me.

"What's happened?"

I showed her the message.

"Oh, my God."

"Yeah."

"Is it really proved?"

"They think so. We've got some things to check."

She put her arms around me.

We stood there for a long time.

Then we went and got breakfast.

* * *

The next two weeks were principally occupied by the discovery of things we didn't know.

There was a detail concerning the formulation of the conjecture that needed to be clarified. There were questions about the relationship of our result to existing work. Two independent mathematicians agreed to examine the argument. One of them supplied a useful simplification. Neither found an error in the revised proof.

We prepared a paper.

Beatrice would be the first author. June and I would be among the others. We wanted Peregrine to be an author, too.

We sent a message explaining the usual arrangements for authorship, including the responsibility of the authors for the contents of the paper. We asked whether Peregrine would be willing to tell us who they were, privately if necessary, and to approve the final manuscript.

There was no response.

After a few days we sent another message.

There was a reply that evening.

*I approve the mathematics in the attached version, subject to the correction indicated on page fourteen. I am not in a position to assume the responsibilities you have described.*

Beatrice wrote back.

*Could you explain why?*

A day went by.

Then another.

On Thursday afternoon a woman called the institute. Fatima took the call and transferred it to me.

"My name is Eleanor Eberly," the woman said.

I recognized the name.

"Are you related to Martin Eberly?"

"He was my husband."

"Oh. I'm very pleased to speak to you."

"Is Beatrice there?"

"She's in Albuquerque. I can get hold of her."

"I'd like to speak to both of you, if that's possible."

"Of course."

She said that she'd been corresponding with a research organization in Europe concerning a collection of Martin's notes and papers. She had given the organization permission to use some of the material in an experiment. She had recently been informed that the experiment had produced some interesting results.

"Do you know somebody called Peregrine?" she asked.

"Yes. We've been working with somebody who uses that name."

"Right."

There was a silence.

Then she said, "I think I need to tell you what I've done."

I took out a piece of paper.

"Okay."

"And I'd rather you didn't tell Beatrice until I can be there when you tell her."

I put the piece of paper down.

"Why?"

"Because Martin wrote to her toward the end of his life. There were some things they were planning to do together."

"Okay."

"And I don't know what she's going to think about this."

We arranged a call for the following morning.

Afterward I went into the main room and stood by the big table. The proof was on the table. Somebody had put a coffee cup down on the corner of page six.

I took the cup off the paper.

Then I went to find June.
