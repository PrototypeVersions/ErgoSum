## Fifty — Ergo Sum

Ray was selling the guitar shop.

I found this out from Walter, who told me about it one evening while we were trying to establish why the amplifier Judy was using had begun making a noise that wasn't the noise Judy was making.

"Did you know Ray's selling the place?" Walter asked.

"No."

"Yeah."

"Is he all right?"

"Far as I know. Wants to go fishing."

I put the cable I'd been holding on the floor.

"Where?"

"I don't know, Adrian. Somewhere where there are fish."

The following afternoon I drove down Greenville Avenue.

The shop looked much as it had looked the first time I'd been in it. There were a number of guitars in the window, several of which had been in the window for a remarkably long time. There was a little stool by the counter. A man was standing by the stool, trying to get his son to put a guitar back on its stand.

Ray was at the workbench.

"Hey," he said.

"Hey."

"What'd you break?"

"Nothing."

"Well, that's nice."

I told him what Walter had said.

"Yeah," he said.

"You're selling it?"

"Well, if I can find somebody who's dumb enough to buy it."

He showed me a little thing he'd been working on. It was part of the mechanism of a guitar that had been made in the sixties. He wanted me to hold something for him while he got a screw out.

I held it.

"Are you going to keep working on guitars?" I asked.

"Probably."

"Good."

He got the screw out.

"Want to know something?"

"Sure."

"Never been particularly fond of fishing."

I laughed.

He put the screw in a little dish.

"Got a friend out near the lake. He's got a place where we can work on things. I thought it'd be nice to spend some time over there."

"That sounds great."

"Yeah."

He went on with what he'd been doing.

I looked around the shop. There was a guitar on the wall that I'd wanted for several years. It was a beautiful guitar. I could have bought it if I'd wanted to.

I asked Ray if I could play it.

"Sure."

He took it down.

I sat on the stool and played it for a while.

Then I handed it back to him.

"Nice guitar," I said.

"Yeah."

He put it on its stand.

"How's the one you've got?"

"Pretty good."

"Good."

* * *

There was a boy named Samuel who came into the shop while I was there.

He was about fifteen. He had a guitar in a bag that was considerably too large for it. Ray knew him. He'd been coming in for several months to look at the guitars and occasionally to ask Ray a question.

He had a question that afternoon.

He wanted to be able to play something he'd heard on a recording. He took out his phone and played the recording for Ray.

Ray listened.

Then he picked up a guitar and played something.

"Like that?"

"Yeah."

Samuel tried to play it.

He didn't quite get it.

Ray showed him again.

Samuel tried it again.

There was something Samuel was doing with his first finger that made the thing he was trying to do with his third finger quite difficult. Ray showed him a different way of doing it.

He got it.

He looked at Ray.

Ray nodded.

Samuel played it several times.

I had been intending to go home. Instead I asked Ray whether he'd mind my taking one of the guitars off the wall.

"Go ahead."

I took it down and sat beside Samuel.

"Play that thing again," I said.

He played it.

I played something over it.

He stopped.

"What was that?" he asked.

I showed him.

He tried it.

Ray went back to the workbench.

For a little while Samuel and I played variations on a thing neither of us had been able to play when we came into the shop.

Eventually the man who'd been trying to get his son to put the guitar back on the stand came over and asked what we were playing.

We didn't have a particularly good answer.

Samuel looked at me.

"Do you play in a band?" he asked.

"Sort of."

"What are you called?"

"We don't have a name."

"Oh."

He looked down at his guitar.

I told him about the people at Walter's house. Ray said that he'd be happy to talk to Walter if Samuel and his parents wanted to find out about it.

Samuel said that sounded good.

Then he played the thing Ray had shown him once more.

It sounded very good.

* * *

On the way home I stopped at the old apartment building.

I hadn't been there for several years.

There was somebody sitting on the steps at the entrance. She had a little dog with her, and when I got out of the car the dog got up.

"Hi," I said.

"Hi."

"Is the dog friendly?"

"Very."

I went over and made the acquaintance of the dog.

The woman lived in an apartment on the first floor. She'd been there for about six months.

I told her that I used to live upstairs.

"Did you like it?" she asked.

"Very much."

"Yeah?"

"Yeah. Although the windows didn't quite close."

She laughed.

"They still don't."

I looked up at the window where the pecan tree came close to the building. There was a little lamp on the other side of the glass.

"Do you know who lives up there?" I asked.

"Couple. They just moved in."

"Oh."

The dog got interested in something in the grass.

"Well," I said, "nice meeting you."

"You, too."

I went back to the car.

I had been trying to remember what it had felt like to be the person who lived in the apartment. I could remember a number of things about him. I could remember being very pleased about a piece of work one evening and being extremely unhappy about the same piece of work the following morning. I could remember a smell that came in through the window at certain times of the year. I could remember Simon sitting by the window eating a hamburger.

There were a number of things I would have liked to tell the person who lived there.

Most of them were things I wouldn't have been able to tell him in a manner that would have enabled him to appreciate them.

I don't think he would have been especially interested in my telling him, for instance, that there would be a day on which a woman would ask him to help her move some furniture in a library.

He'd have wanted to know whether something was going to happen to his career.

And there was a rather nice thing I could have told him about his career.

But I would have liked to tell him about the furniture.

* * *

That evening I finished a draft of this book.

I had been working on it for a little over two years. There were various things about it that I was quite pleased with and various things I thought I might be able to do better. I had read parts of it to some of the people who were in it. Some of them had told me things I hadn't known. Some had told me that they didn't think things had happened in precisely the way I'd described them.

I'd changed some of it.

There were also things I'd put in the book that I couldn't remember anybody actually saying. I could remember what we'd been doing. I could remember what we'd been talking about. I could remember the pleasure I'd had in talking about it.

I had tried to write the conversations in a way that would enable somebody who hadn't been there to have some idea of that pleasure.

I hadn't attempted to produce a transcript.

There was one thing I had written about which I'd been particularly uncertain. I had written that I wanted very much for the people at Northline to discover that their predictions about me were wrong.

I didn't think that was quite right.

I wanted Helen to call me.

I wanted the people at Northline to make me an offer.

I wanted to be able to find out what it would be like to accept the offer, and I wanted to be able to turn it down.

And there were things I hadn't wanted at all, because at the time I hadn't known there were such things to want.

I sat at the desk for a little while.

At the top of the document was the title.

*Ergo Sum.*

I had liked those words since I was a student. I'd spent quite a lot of time considering what went before them.

Descartes wanted to establish that there was something that he couldn't be mistaken about. He found something of extraordinary interest to him in the circumstance that he was thinking.

It had been a rather different circumstance that Helen had been interested in. She had found my name on a piece of paper. There were a number of things on the paper that she didn't know to be true.

She had put it down and called me.

She was interested in finding out whether I was somewhere where the piece of paper said I'd been.

I wasn't.

I was in a guitar shop.

I don't suppose there's a great philosophical discovery to be made about a man being in a guitar shop. There were, however, quite a lot of things that the man in the guitar shop was going to discover.

And he had been very lucky to get the opportunity to discover them.

I shut the document.

* * *

Nadia came home at six.

She had two bags of groceries and a little package somebody had given her at the library.

"What's that?" I asked.

"I don't know."

She opened it.

It contained a small wooden house. It was painted blue, and there were about seven windows on the front of it, one of which was upside down.

A child had made it for her.

She took it into the living room and put it on a shelf.

Then she came back into the kitchen.

"Did you get anything done today?" she asked.

"Yeah."

"Good."

I told her about Ray.

"Oh," she said. "Well, we'll have to do something nice for him."

"Yeah."

I helped her put the groceries away.

She wanted to know whether I was going to Walter's that evening.

"I thought I might."

"Good."

"Do you want to come?"

She thought for a moment.

"Yeah."

We ate dinner. Then she went to get her little wooden instrument, which Morag had sent her a rather better version of the previous Christmas.

I got the guitar.

As we were walking out to the car, a man came down the street.

It was Simon.

He was carrying something in a brown paper bag.

"What's that?" I asked.

"Something for Adrian."

"Which one?"

"The other one."

He looked at the guitar case.

"Going to Walter's?"

"Yeah."

"All right. I'll be over in a minute."

"Are you coming?"

"Thought I might."

He went down the street.

Nadia and I got into the car.

* * *

There were some people at Walter's that evening whom I hadn't seen for a while.

Judy was at the keyboard. Sophie was there, and she'd brought a friend. There was a man who played the drums who'd been introduced to me by Cynthia. Ray was sitting in a chair with a guitar on his knee.

I put my case down.

"Hey," he said.

"Hey."

"How's the guitar?"

"Pretty good."

He smiled.

Walter asked if there was anything in particular I wanted to play.

I thought about it.

Then I heard Judy play a little bit of something on the keyboard.

"What's that?" I asked.

"Nothing," she said. "Just something I was doing."

"Do it again."

She did.

There were a few notes I could play with it. I played them. Ray played something else. Walter found the bass notes.

Judy changed what she was playing.

We followed her.

At some point Simon came in. Nadia waved to him. I looked over at her and she raised the little instrument she'd brought from the house.

I didn't know where she was going to come in.

Then she came in.

It sounded beautiful.

I played a chord I knew, and then another one. I thought I knew what Ray was going to play next, but he played something different.

I listened to him.

Then I played something of my own.

It was getting late. I had a class the following morning. There were some things I needed to do when I got home, and there was a letter on my desk concerning a question that Beatrice wanted me to think about.

I was looking forward to all of those things.

But there was another little bit of music Judy was playing now, and I hadn't heard it before.

I moved my hand up the neck of the guitar.

And I joined in.
