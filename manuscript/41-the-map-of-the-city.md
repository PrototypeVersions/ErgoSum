## Forty-one — The Map of the City

Miriam wanted to show me a map.

We met for lunch at a little restaurant near the building where Northline had its offices. She had brought the map in a cardboard folder, and as soon as the waiter went away she took it out and spread it across the table.

It was a map of a city in the Midwest.

There were blue dots of various sizes on it and a series of red lines, some of which appeared to go through places where there were no blue dots.

"What's this?" I asked.

"The project I was working on before I started Northline."

She had been employed by a consulting firm. The city was trying to establish where to provide certain public services, and the firm had been hired to estimate the demand for them.

One of the things Miriam had done was spend several days driving around with a woman who worked for the city.

"What did you do?" I asked.

"We went to places."

She pointed to one of the blue dots.

At this location there was a building in which people could get assistance with a number of things. The woman from the city knew the people who worked in the building. She knew a great deal about the things they'd been doing and considerably more than Miriam did about the things they would have liked to be doing.

There had been a problem getting people to use one of the services. Miriam had expected to find that the people who lived near the building weren't particularly interested in it.

The woman from the city took her to meet a man who lived near the building.

"He couldn't get in," Miriam said.

"Why not?"

"Steps."

She looked at the map.

"He could get into a building two miles away. His sister took him there on Saturdays."

"Oh."

"So the model was anticipating fairly substantial demand for the service at the building two miles away."

"Right."

"And not much where he lived."

She folded part of the map back.

"I was twenty-eight. I'd been very good at school. I'd been very good at university. I'd been at the firm for about four years. And suddenly I had this wonderful feeling that there was something I could do that I hadn't known I could do."

"What?"

"Find out why the dots were where they were."

She smiled.

"Well, it was more interesting than deciding how big they were."

We moved the map when the food came.

She told me about the people she'd wanted to work with, and about the first few years of Northline. There had been a mathematician from California and a sociologist from Chicago and a woman who'd been working on public transportation for most of her life. They had been very good at several things and not very good at the same things, which had been one of the pleasures of working with them.

Then they'd got a number of large contracts.

There were some projects that she would have preferred not to undertake. There were other projects she wanted very much to undertake that nobody was interested in paying for. There were people working at Northline whose salaries depended on her finding a way of paying for them.

"Eventually," she said, "there was a department whose job it was to decide which questions we were going to be paid to answer."

"Yeah."

"And I spent a lot of time with that department."

I looked at her.

"Did you like it?"

"Sometimes. It was quite interesting."

She took a drink.

"Then there were things going on in the other departments that I didn't know about."

* * *

I asked her about Helen.

"Did you think she'd done something wrong?"

"At the time I thought she'd given you material that she wasn't authorized to give you."

"Yeah."

"And I thought there was a fairly good chance that the material would be published in a newspaper and that the people who worked for me would spend the next several months trying to explain what had happened."

I nodded.

"Did you think she ought to have called me?"

Miriam looked down at her plate.

"Yes," she said.

She thought for a moment.

"Actually, no. I thought somebody should have called you. I didn't think it should have been necessary for Helen to decide whether she ought to be the person who did it."

We sat there.

I asked her whether the public report would describe the circumstances in which Helen had been put on leave.

"Yes."

"Okay."

"And I've asked her whether she would like to contribute something to it."

"Has she?"

"She's thinking about it."

Miriam smiled.

"She's a very good teacher, you know."

"I know."

She put the map back in the folder.

Then she asked me how things were going at Open Question.

I told her.

She listened to the account of the financial arrangements, and to the things we were considering doing over the following year. When I'd finished, she asked me whether I'd thought of making an arrangement with a university under which some of the institute's work could be continued independently of the institute itself.

"Yes," I said.

"Good."

She looked at me.

"There are things you can preserve without preserving the organization."

I nodded.

She didn't say anything for a moment.

Then she picked up the folder.

"Do you want this?"

"The map?"

"Yeah. I've got another one at home."

I took the folder.

"Thanks."

* * *

In January the new stage of our study began.

We had made changes to the systems in light of the pilot, completed the necessary reviews and published the plan for the analysis. People were being recruited in several places. The independent research team would have control of the data. We were to receive reports about the conduct of the study but not the comparative results while it was underway.

I had agreed that this was a good way of doing it.

About three weeks later I was finding it very difficult to agree that it was a good way of doing it.

"Do you know anything about how we're doing?" I asked Peter.

"No."

"Okay."

"And if I did know anything, I wouldn't tell you."

"Good."

I went back to my office.

I was trying to finish a lecture for Paris. I'd decided that I would discuss the original interview transcript, some of the things we'd learned at Northline, and the argument in our new paper. There were several fairly good examples in the lecture. I had also included a short description of the study.

On Wednesday a man in London wrote to me asking whether I could comment on something he'd read.

He sent me a link.

It was a story about the use of AI in educational and professional guidance. The story described the study we were conducting and included a quotation from me.

I read the quotation.

I'd never said it.

There was another quotation from me a little farther down the page. I hadn't said that one either.

I sent the story to Fatima.

She called me a few minutes later.

"Have you seen the source?"

"No."

"Look at the link at the bottom."

I did.

The quotations came from a document on our own website.

* * *

June came in.

She looked at the document.

"That's mine," she said.

"What?"

"That's one of the examples."

The document was part of the demonstration material for the provenance software. It described an invented public statement, generated as an example of a system's ability to produce a plausible account of a person's views. A prominent label indicated that the material was synthetic. June had used my name, with my permission, in a number of the examples.

One of the things we'd intended the demonstration to illustrate was the difficulty of preserving the status of a statement when it was taken out of the environment in which it was produced.

Somebody had taken it out of that environment.

"Well," she said, "that's annoying."

I read the quotation again.

It said that the study would demonstrate the superiority of systems designed to expand a person's opportunities rather than influence their decisions.

"That's a terrible thing to say," I said.

"Yeah."

"I haven't got any idea what the study's going to demonstrate."

"I know."

I got up.

"Can we take the document down?"

"We can. But there's nothing wrong with the document."

I looked at her.

"Okay."

She opened the page.

"We could make the status more apparent. We could change how the examples are distributed. But we probably can't make it impossible for someone to copy a sentence and leave something out."

I nodded.

We added a note to the page explaining what had happened, and Fatima contacted the publication. The writer apologized and corrected the story.

By then the quotation had been reproduced elsewhere.

A professor in Germany had written that he was disappointed by my willingness to announce the results of a study before it had been conducted. I wrote to him and explained what had happened.

He thanked me for the clarification.

Then he asked whether I thought the institute ought to have published the example in the first place.

I got up and went to the kitchen.

I came back.

I read his question again.

Then I asked June what she thought.

* * *

The public report on Northline appeared the following week.

I read it at home.

There were accounts of several failures that had occurred in the use of the research. There were descriptions of changes that Northline had made, and of changes it had recommended to the people using its systems. There was a section concerning the recruitment project.

There was also a statement from Helen.

She'd written it herself.

It was very short. She described the circumstances in which she had first contacted me. She explained that she'd had no way of determining whether the decision associated with the record reflected a decision I'd made. She described the steps she'd taken to obtain the information.

At the end she said that she was glad the organization had changed its procedures. She also said she thought there ought to be a way for somebody to raise a concern about a record without having to know in advance that the record was wrong.

I read the statement several times.

Then I sent Miriam a link to the correction on our own website.

I told her what had happened.

She wrote back that she was sorry.

A few minutes later I got another message from her.

*Would you mind if I used the example in a talk I'm giving?*

I laughed.

*Of course not.*

*Can I quote you on that?*

*Yes.*

There was something very nice about the fact that she had asked.
