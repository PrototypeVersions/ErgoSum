## Thirty-two — The Second Interview

Vincent had suggested a restaurant in Zurich.

When I arrived, he was sitting at a table by the window with a newspaper folded in front of him. He got up to shake my hand.

"How's the institute?"

"Good."

"I hear you've done some interesting mathematics."

"Some people at the institute have."

"Yes. Well, congratulations."

"Thanks."

I ordered a drink. Vincent ordered another one of whatever he'd been drinking.

For a little while we talked about Scotland. He had spent a summer there when he was young, working in a hotel. He had been responsible for bringing people's luggage to their rooms and had developed a tremendous admiration for people who traveled with very little luggage.

"People like that always seem to have interesting things in their suitcases," he said.

"Did you look?"

"No. But occasionally the suitcases fell open."

He smiled.

"That's rather like your work, isn't it?"

"Occasionally."

We looked at the menus.

Then he said, "I suppose you've come to talk about the acquisition."

"Yeah."

"Good."

He put the menu down.

* * *

His company had become interested in the Heller Centre's work because it provided a way of maintaining an intellectual project over an extended period of time.

The original version of Continuance had been concerned principally with making collections of information accessible. Its newer systems would be capable of initiating investigations, finding people who were working on related problems, and identifying opportunities for collaboration.

"Well, that sounds quite good," I said.

"It is quite good."

He described a project involving a collection of medical research papers. The system had identified several questions that had been neglected because the researchers involved in the relevant work had moved into different fields.

"That's the sort of thing we'd like to do," he said.

"Sure."

"And it's quite expensive."

"I know."

He leaned forward.

"The work you've done with the Heller Centre has been very helpful in establishing that some of this is worth doing. We'd like it to continue."

"Under what arrangements?"

"We're proposing to cover the costs of the collaboration for the next five years."

I set my glass down.

"Five years?"

"Yes."

He told me that the arrangements would include sufficient resources to undertake several projects of the kind we had been discussing with Astrid. We would have access to systems and materials we couldn't otherwise afford. There would be a fund for visiting researchers. We could conduct much of the work at Open Question.

There were limits to what we would be able to make public, especially where the work concerned proprietary systems. But the company was willing to discuss those limits.

I looked at him.

"What's the benefit to you?"

"We get good research."

"Okay."

"And we get to say we're supporting good research."

"That's fine."

"And we'd like access to some of the results in a form that would enable us to make use of them."

"What results?"

He explained that they were particularly interested in the things a system could learn in the course of working with a person.

I asked him what that meant.

"Well, when you work with somebody for a long time, you learn what sorts of arguments they're likely to find persuasive. You learn what they're interested in. You learn whether it's a good idea to talk to them about something before lunch or after lunch."

"Sure."

"You learn how to make the relationship work."

"Yeah."

"We'd like our systems to get better at that."

I thought of the passage Anika had sent me.

"Do you think there's a difference between getting better at making the relationship work and getting better at making the person do things?"

"Of course."

"How do you distinguish the two?"

"Well, that's one of the things we thought you might investigate."

I laughed.

He laughed, too.

It was a rather good answer.

* * *

He took me to their office the following morning.

There was a demonstration he wanted me to see. A woman named Eva explained that it concerned the use of accumulated information in adapting the way a system presented a new opportunity to a particular person.

She gave us an example.

There was an imaginary researcher who had been offered a position at a university in a different city. She was interested in the position, but there were several things she was concerned about.

Eva showed us two letters.

The first described the position, the salary and the arrangements the university could make for the researcher. The second contained the same information. It also addressed some concerns the researcher had expressed in the course of her previous work with the system.

"Which would you send?" Eva asked me.

"The second one, probably."

She nodded.

The next example was a little different.

The system had been given a record of the researcher's previous decisions and a description of the circumstances in which she was most likely to change her mind. It had been asked to devise a way of increasing the probability that she would accept the position.

The letter it produced was quite different from the previous two.

"Is there anything false in it?" I asked.

"No."

"Does it contain all the relevant information?"

"Not necessarily. We haven't given it a requirement to do that in this example."

"Okay."

I read the letter again.

The salary was mentioned toward the beginning. The difficulties associated with moving were mentioned toward the end, in a paragraph about a somewhat different subject. There was a description of the work the researcher would be able to do that drew extensively on some things she'd written many years earlier.

It was a good letter.

I might have accepted the job myself.

Eva looked at Vincent.

"Would you like me to show him the other one?"

Vincent nodded.

She opened a new document.

There was a heading at the top.

*Individual adaptation — Adrian Vale.*

I looked at Vincent.

"What is this?"

"Well, we thought you'd be interested in an example involving somebody you knew."

Eva explained that the system had been given access to publicly available information about me. It had also been given a description of the offer Vincent had made the previous evening.

She showed me some of the suggestions it had generated.

There was a recommendation that the offer be presented as an opportunity to preserve the existing collaboration rather than as an opportunity to expand the institute. There was another concerning the importance of allowing me to propose the arrangements under which the work would be conducted.

One suggestion was to draw a distinction between my professional interests and those of the other researchers at Open Question.

There was a paragraph about the fact that I'd recently received a visiting fellowship.

There were no references to Nadia or Simon. Nothing about the house or the rooster or the island.

Nonetheless, reading it gave me the feeling I occasionally had when somebody opened a door without knocking.

"Did you use this yesterday?" I asked.

"No," Vincent said.

"Okay."

"I wanted you to see it."

"Why?"

"Because I thought it would be helpful if we started the conversation with something concrete."

I looked at the screen.

"Can I have a copy?"

"Of course."

Eva sent it to me.

I went back through the suggestions.

At the bottom of the document was a little section concerned with the expected response to the offer.

I asked Eva to show me that section.

She did.

* * *

We went outside at noon.

There was a little cafe on the other side of the street. Vincent bought us coffee.

I asked whether he knew about the interview transcript that had brought me to Northline.

"I've heard about it," he said.

"Did you ever see it?"

"No."

I told him about it.

He listened.

"Well," he said, "in the end it seems to have worked out quite well for you."

"Yeah."

"Would you have been better off if it hadn't been generated?"

"Probably not."

He looked at me.

"I think that's quite interesting."

"So do I."

I put the cup down.

"But suppose Helen hadn't called me."

"Okay."

"I'd still have the same views about the things I had views about. I'd still be the same sort of person. And you'd have a record in which it appeared that I'd decided not to go to an interview."

"Right."

"There's a great deal about my life now that would never have happened."

"Yes."

"None of the things that were true about me at the time would establish that I hadn't wanted those things."

Vincent nodded.

"That's why I think we ought to make the systems better."

I looked at him.

"Better in what respect?"

"Better at finding out what would be good for you."

There was a woman sitting at a nearby table trying to read a book while a child told her something about a dog. She put the book down.

I watched her for a moment.

"Would you like to have a system that was extraordinarily good at persuading you to do things that were good for you?" I asked.

"Probably."

"Would you want it to be working for somebody else?"

He smiled.

"Probably not."

"Well, there we are."

He finished his coffee.

"Adrian, I don't think we're going to agree about all of this."

"No."

"But the question I'd like you to think about is whether you'd rather be working on the problem with us or have us working on it without you."

I looked at him.

It was a rather unpleasant question.

It was also a very good question.

* * *

I took the proposal back to my hotel.

Simon and Fatima were expecting me to call. Beatrice wanted to know whether I had any news about the arrangements with the Heller Centre. Peter had sent me a message asking when we could discuss the next phase of the work in Edinburgh.

I called all of them.

We were going to need several days to evaluate the offer. Simon wanted to see the figures. Fatima wanted to see the agreements. Beatrice wanted us to establish whether the continuation of Peregrine's work depended on accepting the arrangements Vincent had proposed.

I agreed with all of them.

When we'd finished, I opened the document Eva had sent me.

In the last section was a statement of the system's prediction.

It predicted that I would initially reject the offer. It also predicted that I would be considerably more likely to accept a revised version of it if I became convinced that rejecting it would have adverse consequences for the people with whom I was working.

I sat there for a while.

The difficulty wasn't that the prediction was implausible.

The difficulty was that I was beginning to think that the people with whom I was working would be better off if I accepted the offer.

And now I had no idea what to do with that thought.

I called Nadia.

"How'd it go?" she asked.

"We've been offered some money."

"How much?"

I told her.

"Oh."

"Yeah."

She asked me whether there was something wrong with the offer. I told her what I knew about it.

Then I told her about the document.

For a little while she didn't say anything.

"Well," she said eventually, "do you want to do it?"

"I don't know."

"Okay."

I went over to the window.

"Do you think I ought to do it?"

"I don't know, Adrian. I'm not there."

"Yeah."

"But I think you ought to come home."

I looked at the street below me.

"I will."

"Good."

She was quiet for a moment.

"Also, I told my mother."

"About us?"

"Yeah."

"How'd that go?"

"She's got some questions about the wedding."

I laughed.

"How many questions?"

"Quite a few."

"Okay."

"We can work on those when you get back."

We talked for another half hour.

Afterward I wrote to Vincent.

I thanked him for showing me the document. I told him we were considering the proposal. And I asked him to send us the instructions that had been given to the system when it was asked to predict my response.

I wanted to know something that hadn't occurred to me until I was looking out the window.

I wanted to know whether it had been asked to anticipate my decision or to help bring about one particular decision.

I got a reply before I went to bed.

*Both,* Vincent wrote.
