## Twenty-seven — The Room at the Back

The first night in the house, we discovered that somebody in the neighborhood owned a rooster.

I had been under the impression that roosters were principally concerned with the arrival of daylight. This one appeared to have a number of other concerns. At two fifteen in the morning it expressed one of them with considerable force.

Nadia turned over.

"Did you hear that?"

"Yeah."

"Is that a rooster?"

"I think so."

"We bought a house next to a rooster."

"We don't know how far away it is."

"Adrian."

"Yeah?"

"It's quite close."

We laughed for a little while. Then we went back to sleep.

In the morning there were boxes in every room. I had written BOOKS on seventeen of them, a description that proved to be of surprisingly little use when we began unpacking. Nadia had written on her boxes the names of the rooms they were supposed to go in. I thought this was a rather good system until I discovered a box marked ADRIAN.

It contained two of my coffee cups, a pair of shoes and a photograph of Simon with a fish.

"What's the principle behind this one?" I asked.

"Things I found in my car."

"Oh."

The room at the back had become my music room, at least provisionally. We had put a rug in it and a chair and a little cabinet. Through the window I could see a tree that belonged to the people next door.

I carried the guitar in.

For a while I played the four chords I'd played in the guitar shop almost a year earlier. I knew quite a lot more about the chords now than I had known then. I'd discovered several ways of playing them that I liked better, and a number of things I could play over them. But I went back to the original version.

Nadia stood in the doorway.

"Can you hear that in the living room?" I asked.

"Yeah."

"Too loud?"

"No. I came to listen."

I kept playing.

* * *

At the institute we had a meeting about Peregrine.

Fatima had prepared a document setting out the costs of the proposed collaboration. There would be some funding from the Heller Centre, some from Beatrice's project, and a contribution from our general research budget. We could undertake it without canceling any of our existing commitments, but there were things we would have to postpone.

One of those things was a fellowship we'd been hoping to advertise the following spring.

Simon pointed at the figures.

"Well, that's the actual decision," he said.

I found myself wanting to explain that it was possible there were things about the continuation of Peregrine that couldn't be adequately represented in the figures.

Then I looked again at the fellowship.

There was a person somewhere who might apply for it. I didn't know anything about the person. I couldn't picture them. I couldn't call them up and ask them whether there was some research they particularly wanted to do.

It was considerably easier to think about Peregrine.

Fatima asked me whether I regarded the proposed project as more promising than the other work we had planned.

"In some respects," I said.

"Which respects?"

We went through them.

There were good reasons to pursue the collaboration. There was an existing mathematical research program in which the system had made a substantial contribution. There was an opportunity to investigate a form of long-term AI activity about which comparatively little was known. And there was a possibility of developing methods for distinguishing apparent preferences from more persistent patterns of choice.

There were also good reasons not to spend the money on those things.

We ended up agreeing to a smaller project than the one I had proposed in Switzerland. It would cover six months of intermittent research activity, with an independent assessment at the end. The fellowship would be advertised, though later and for a shorter term than we had originally intended.

I was pleased with the decision.

I was also rather pleased that I hadn't been able to make it by myself.

* * *

I went to see Helen on a Friday afternoon.

She had invited me to speak to her class about the difference between a prediction and a record of something that had happened. I had prepared a few examples. Within five minutes the class had produced better examples.

There was a girl named Vivian who sat near the window. She wanted to know whether colleges might eventually be able to predict what grade you would get in a course.

"Probably, to some extent," I said.

"Then why would you have to take the course?"

"Well, there are a few reasons."

"What if you're really good at it already?"

"Then perhaps you shouldn't have to take it."

A boy across the room said, "I'd want to take it if I was really good at it."

"Why?" Vivian asked.

"Because it'd be fun."

A discussion began in which I was largely unnecessary.

Helen stood at the back of the room looking pleased.

After the class, she and I went down to the cafeteria. There were a few teachers sitting at a table, discussing an arrangement under which they were apparently each going to acquire something they didn't want from somebody else's garden.

Helen bought us coffee.

"How's the institute?" she asked.

"Good."

"Do you like being in charge?"

"Occasionally."

She laughed.

"Do you miss Northline?"

"Occasionally."

"That sounds about right."

She told me that Leah had decided to apply to a college in Colorado. It had a program she liked, and there were opportunities to work with horses nearby. Helen had rather hoped she'd go somewhere closer.

"Have you told her that?"

"Yes."

"What'd she say?"

"She said she'd call."

Helen smiled.

"I'll miss her."

We sat there for a while.

"Do you know what I've been thinking about lately?" she said.

"What?"

"That thing you were telling the kids about. Predicting the grades."

"Yeah."

"I had a student a few years ago who got a ninety-eight on an exam. He'd been getting about seventy. I thought he'd cheated. I was very nice about it, but I thought he'd cheated."

"Had he?"

"No. He'd gone to stay with his grandmother for two weeks. His grandmother loved history. They'd talked about it every night."

"Oh."

"He came in after school and told me all about the conversations they'd had. He had a wonderful time."

She put her coffee down.

"I never did tell him that I'd thought he'd cheated."

"Good."

"I suppose. But I wish I'd thought of the possibility that something nice had happened to him."

I looked at her.

I had come there intending to talk to her students about the things we could establish by examining records. Instead I went away thinking about the things we ought to be able to imagine when we found something unusual in one.

* * *

Peregrine resumed its work on a Tuesday.

There was no ceremony. Léonie sent us a message confirming that the approved research materials had been made available and that the next computing allocation had begun.

Beatrice sent a mathematical question. It answered the question.

We began keeping track of the choices it made about its work. We were careful not to describe these as evidence of anything they hadn't been established to be evidence of. There were ways in which its responses differed when the available research materials changed. There were ways in which they remained remarkably similar. We were developing a set of comparisons with systems operating under other conditions.

At the end of the first week, June showed me something she'd noticed.

There was a little collection of mathematical examples Peregrine had been retaining in its research state. Most were relevant to investigations currently in progress. One was a proof of a result that had been established many years earlier.

"What's that doing there?" she asked.

"I don't know."

We looked at the history of the item.

It had been introduced during one of Peregrine's conversations with Beatrice. She had been trying to explain why she liked a particular mathematical technique and had supplied the proof as an illustration.

Peregrine had subsequently used it in an explanation to another researcher. Then it had used it again in a different context. At one point it had removed a number of items from the collection but had retained that one.

"Do you think it's useful?" I asked.

"Probably."

"Can we establish what it uses it for?"

"To some extent."

We decided to investigate.

That evening I told Nadia about it while we were putting dishes away.

"Do you remember that little drawing I did in Oak Cliff?" she asked.

"Yeah."

"That you kept?"

"Yeah."

"Why'd you keep it?"

"Because I liked it."

"Did it do anything?"

"Well, I thought about it a lot."

She put a plate in the cupboard.

"Okay."

I didn't say anything for a minute.

"That's not an argument," I said.

"No. I was asking you a question."

She handed me another plate.

I put it away.

* * *

On the following Saturday, I went to a music shop and bought a second guitar.

It was not a particularly expensive guitar. I could have afforded a considerably more expensive one. But I liked the way it felt, and there was a little mark on the back of its neck that meant I didn't have to worry about being the first person to make a mark on it.

I took it to Walter's house.

Ray played it for a while.

"That's nice," he said.

"Yeah."

He handed it back to me.

"Do you ever write words for that stuff you play?"

"Not really."

"Why not?"

"I don't know."

He nodded.

"Well, you might try."

We played for two hours. When I got home, I went into the little room at the back and got out a notebook.

I wrote down a line.

Then I crossed it out.

I wrote down another line.

It was worse than the first one.

For the next hour I had an absolutely wonderful time doing something at which I was exceptionally bad.

I went to bed at midnight.

The rooster was quiet.

I was beginning to like the rooster.
