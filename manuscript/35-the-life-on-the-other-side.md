## Thirty-five — The Life on the Other Side

The other Adrian was a man of about sixty who had spent thirty years repairing elevators. He had a considerably better understanding of my name than I had of his occupation, and for the first few minutes of our conversation I had the peculiar experience of feeling that I was impersonating him.

He showed me the rooster.

"That's Raymond," he said.

"Does he ever sleep?"

"Sure."

"When?"

"I don't know. I'm asleep."

There was a little shed at the end of the yard. He had a collection of old radios in it. He was trying to get one of them to work, and he wanted to know whether I understood anything about electricity.

I told him I knew a guy.

"Bring him over," he said.

I went home and called Simon.

For the next several weeks Simon would occasionally disappear for a few hours and return smelling of solder and knowing something about the year 1947.

* * *

Elliot came to Dallas in May.

He had decided to spend a week at the institute. There were some people he wanted to meet and a number of things we had been discussing in writing that he thought we might be able to discuss more satisfactorily if we were in the same room.

He arrived with a rather small suitcase.

"Is that all you've brought?" I asked.

"Yes."

"Where are your books?"

"Well, I thought you might have some."

I took him to the house.

Nadia was making dinner. Elliot went into the kitchen to ask whether he could help her and remained there for approximately forty-five minutes. When he came out, he knew a great deal more about the arrangements for the wedding than I did.

"You're going to have a nice wedding," he said.

"Yeah?"

"Apparently."

He went into the music room.

I played him the little piece I'd been working on. He listened to it without speaking. Then he asked if he could hear it again.

When I'd finished he told me that Ruth used to play the piano. She had been particularly fond of a piece that was quite easy to play and didn't sound particularly easy to play.

"She was very pleased about that," he said.

"Did you play anything?"

"The clarinet. Extremely badly."

"Have you still got it?"

"Yes."

"Well, bring it next time."

He smiled.

"I might."

* * *

On Monday we took the argument Beatrice and I had been working on to the big table.

Celia had developed a way of describing some of the possible sequences of events in our recommendation experiment. Peter had put together a number of examples of systems that would produce the same records despite operating in substantially different circumstances.

I showed Elliot one of them.

There was a person who had been using a service for several years. The service provided her with opportunities to do certain things. We supposed that it had made accurate predictions of every choice she had made during those years. We even supposed that it knew how she would respond to any combination of the opportunities it had been making available.

There was another opportunity that it had never made available to her.

We wanted to know whether the service could determine, from what it knew, whether her life would go better if that opportunity became available.

"Obviously not," Elliot said.

"Well, hang on."

"All right."

I explained that we weren't supposing the service knew nothing about the person. On the contrary, we were allowing it a quite extraordinary amount of knowledge. We were allowing it to know, for example, how her interests would develop in response to every sequence of experiences it was currently capable of making available to her.

"Yes," he said. "But not the other one."

"Right."

"So why would you expect it to know?"

"I wouldn't."

"Well, that's good."

Beatrice laughed.

"Adrian, show him the next page."

I showed him the next page.

We had constructed two possible descriptions of the person. They agreed about all her choices under the existing arrangements, including choices that she hadn't actually made but that the arrangements would have allowed her to make. In one description, an opportunity outside those arrangements would enable her to develop an interest of great value to her. In the other description, it wouldn't.

There was nothing in the information available to the service that would enable it to distinguish those descriptions.

Peter had written a little result about this. If the two descriptions required different decisions, a rule that received exactly the same information in each case could not guarantee that it would make the better decision in both. Randomizing the rule wouldn't give it such a guarantee either.

It was a fairly elementary result.

Elliot looked at the page.

"Yes," he said. "That's fine."

I sat down.

"What concerns me," I said, "is that there are a number of people who would regard the success of that service over the previous several years as evidence that it's got sufficiently good at understanding this person to decide which opportunities it ought to continue making available to her."

"Ah."

"And if they let it make that decision, the new information it receives will be information about her under the arrangements it chose."

Elliot went back to the first page.

"So you're interested in the circumstances under which a system can keep itself from finding out that it's mistaken."

"Among other things."

He looked at Beatrice.

"That's rather nice."

"Yeah," she said. "We've been telling him that."

"Well, I suppose he wanted to hear it in my accent."

I laughed.

We spent the afternoon looking at the assumptions in the argument. Elliot wanted to know what justified the supposition that the two descriptions of the person were possible. Celia wanted to know whether there were conditions under which a system could find out enough about other people to establish something useful about this person.

Peter wanted us to make quite clear that we weren't establishing that the introduction of an unfamiliar opportunity was always a good idea.

We weren't.

Some opportunities were dangerous. Some were expensive. Some were things a person had already said they didn't want. We couldn't give anybody all the opportunities there were.

The result told us something about what couldn't be established by a particular kind of evidence. It didn't tell us what to do in every case in which we lacked that evidence.

It seemed important to get that right.

* * *

The following day Elliot gave me a copy of a page from Ruth's notebook. It was dated the year before she'd died.

There were two little tables on the page. One concerned an authority's estimates of demand for a service. The other concerned the things the authority had decided to do on the basis of those estimates.

At the bottom she had written:

*The missing observation may be missing as a consequence of the use to which the other observations have been put.*

I took the page over to Peter.

He read it.

"That's good," he said.

"Yeah."

We made a place for Ruth's work in the paper we were writing, including the report Elliot had shown me in Cambridge. We also read a considerable amount of other work on learning under restricted conditions, on preferences that develop through experience, and on the value of opportunities. There were people who had been thinking about various aspects of the problem for a long time.

I was pleased by this, although I had been sufficiently pleased with myself before doing the reading that it took me a few days to become as pleased by it as I ought to have been.

By Friday there were fourteen pages of the paper.

I had wanted it to be called *The Impossibility of a Complete Account*.

Beatrice thought this was ridiculous.

"That's the paper you wrote ten years ago."

"Well, it's a different impossibility."

"Then give it a different title."

We called it *Learning Within a Life*.

Elliot liked the title.

"Sounds like a book my wife would have bought," he said.

"Really?"

"Yes. She wouldn't have read it."

* * *

On Saturday Nadia took us to the library site.

Construction was underway. There were men working on the roof, and a substantial part of the building was beginning to look like the building in the drawings she'd shown me.

She walked us through it.

"That's the room with the table," she said.

I looked inside.

There wasn't a table yet. There wasn't much of anything yet. But there was a window, and from the window you could see the three trees she'd wanted to keep.

Elliot went over to the window.

"That's lovely," he said.

"Thanks."

He stood there for a while.

Then he asked Nadia what people were going to do in the room.

"I don't know," she said.

He smiled.

She showed us the rest of the building.

On the way home I told her that we'd made some progress on the paper. I explained the example of the two possible descriptions of a person and why the available information couldn't distinguish them.

She listened.

When I'd finished, she asked whether we'd put in anything about the cost of providing the opportunities.

"Yeah."

"Good."

"And something about the difference between an opportunity somebody knows about and an opportunity they can actually use."

"Good."

We stopped at a traffic light.

"Do you think the paper's any good?" she asked.

"Yeah."

"Are you pleased with it?"

"Very."

"Good."

She turned the radio on.

There was a song playing that she'd liked when she was in high school. I had never heard it before. She turned it up and sang some of the words.

I thought it was a terrible song.

I was very happy to be in the car.

* * *

On Sunday evening I sent Vincent a copy of the draft.

I'd been expecting a response to the conditions the board had proposed. We had received some further correspondence concerning particular provisions but no decision on the proposal as a whole.

I told him I thought the paper concerned one of the difficulties we'd been discussing in Zurich. I invited him to tell me what he thought we'd got wrong.

I didn't hear from him for several days.

Then I received a message.

He liked the paper. He thought the result was correct. He also thought the significance of the result depended on some fairly strong assumptions concerning the information a system could obtain.

He had a suggestion.

His company was preparing to conduct an extensive study of a new service. They would be interested in a comparison with a system designed on the basis of the principles we had been developing at Open Question.

He wanted to know whether we would consider taking part.

He had attached a description of the study.

I opened it.

There were going to be several thousand participants. The service would assist them in discovering educational and professional opportunities. The company intended to evaluate it using a measure of the participants' satisfaction with the opportunities they subsequently pursued.

I read the description twice.

Then I forwarded it to Celia.

She called me within five minutes.

"Adrian," she said, "they've got the wrong outcome measure."

"I know."

"Do you think they'd let us change it?"

"I don't know."

There was a silence.

"Well," she said, "can we ask?"

I looked at the paper on my desk.

"Yes," I said.

And this time I knew what I wanted them to agree to.
