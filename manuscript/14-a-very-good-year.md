## Fourteen — A Very Good Year

On the twenty-ninth of December, Nadia and I got in her car and drove west.

I had proposed taking my guitar. She had said that she didn't think anybody in her family would mind if I took my guitar, which was a sufficiently unenthusiastic endorsement of the proposal that I left the guitar at home.

We stopped for lunch in Abilene. At the next table two men were having an argument about a washing machine. I had difficulty following the argument because each of them seemed to be talking about a different washing machine, and I began to wonder if they knew it.

Nadia had ordered a very large piece of pie.

"You want some?" she asked.

"No, thanks."

"Well, I'm not giving you any later."

"Okay."

I watched her eat about half of it.

"Could I have a little?"

She pushed the plate toward me.

"You're incredibly predictable."

"Don't say that."

We got back in the car.

There were parts of the drive where you could see practically all the way to the end of the world. Every so often a little building would come into view. It was impossible to know, from looking at some of the buildings, whether anybody had been in them for fifty years or whether somebody was sitting in them at that moment eating a sandwich.

I liked to think there was somebody sitting in them eating a sandwich.

Nadia told me stories about her family. Her sister, Teresa, had three children, two dogs, and a husband who'd been planning to build a shed since the second Obama administration. Her father had died several years earlier. Her mother lived in a little house a few streets from Teresa and was known throughout the neighborhood for giving people plants they hadn't expressed an interest in having.

"What did you tell them about me?" I asked.

"That you were coming."

"That's all?"

"I told my mom you were a professor."

"I'm not a professor."

"I know. I tried explaining it to her. You're welcome to try explaining it to her."

We drove for a while without talking.

"Hey," Nadia said.

"Yeah?"

"I'd like you to have a good time."

"I think I will."

"I don't want you to spend the whole time trying to figure out whether they're having a good time with you there."

I looked at her.

"Okay."

She reached over and squeezed my hand.

* * *

Teresa opened the door of her house with a telephone in one hand and a potato in the other.

"You're late," she said.

Nadia hugged her.

"We're not late. I told you we'd be here around seven."

"It's seven twenty."

"Well, that strikes me as being around seven."

Teresa looked at me.

"You're the professor."

"Hi."

"Come on in. You can tell me why my son won't go to college."

Her son was seventeen and had a part-time job at a place that repaired cars. I met him a few minutes later. His name was Anthony. He had been informed that I taught philosophy, and wanted to know what a philosopher actually did.

I told him about the research I'd been doing.

"Okay," he said. "So you study how people make decisions?"

"Among other things."

"Do you know why people buy trucks they can't afford?"

"No."

"I see it all the time."

"Why do you think they do it?"

He thought about it.

"Because they really want a truck."

"That's probably it."

He nodded, satisfied that we had made some progress.

We went out to the garage to look at a motorcycle he was working on. It didn't run. He explained to me what he thought the problem was, and I understood approximately half of the nouns he used.

"How'd you learn all that?" I asked.

"My friend showed me. Then I watched some videos."

He showed me a part he'd taken out and cleaned. He was very pleased with it.

Later that evening Teresa asked me to talk to him about college.

"I think he's a pretty bright kid," I said.

"Of course he's a bright kid. That's why I want him to go to college."

"Well, he seems to have found something he's interested in."

"Yes, and when he finds another thing he's interested in, I want him to have some choices."

I hadn't considered this. I told her I thought it made sense.

She handed me a bowl of green beans.

"Here. Put that on the table."

I put the green beans on the table.

It occurred to me that I was becoming rather good at being a guest in this house.

* * *

On New Year's Eve, Nadia took me up into the mountains. It was cold and windy. We parked the car and walked along a trail from which we could see the city spread out below us, and the cities and the mountains beyond it. There was a tremendous amount of country out there.

"This is where I used to come when I was a teenager," she said.

"By yourself?"

"Sometimes. Sometimes with my friends."

We sat on a rock.

"What did you want to do when you were a teenager?" she asked.

"Lots of things."

"Like what?"

"I wanted to write a book. I wanted to discover something. I wanted to be one of those people whose lives you read about and think, my God, what an interesting life."

She took a drink of water.

"What sort of things were they doing in those lives?"

"Traveling. Coming up with ideas. Knowing interesting people. Having love affairs."

"Good Lord."

"I know."

She laughed.

"I wanted to design a house my dad wouldn't know how to build."

"Did you?"

"Several."

"Was he impressed?"

"Eventually."

She looked out over the city.

"Do you know what the interesting thing about my dad was?"

"What?"

"He wasn't particularly ambitious. He didn't want to build the biggest house or be the best builder in Texas. But he was always interested in what people were doing. We'd go somewhere and he'd disappear for an hour, and you'd find him talking to a guy who was fixing an ice machine."

I smiled.

"I'd like to have met him."

"He'd have liked you. He'd have asked you a million questions, and then he'd have tried to get you to help him put in a screen door."

We sat there until it got too cold.

On the way down I slipped on some loose stones. Nadia caught my arm. I didn't fall, but I made a rather extraordinary noise in the process of not falling.

"What was that?" she said.

"I don't know."

She imitated it.

I laughed so hard that I had to stop walking.

* * *

That night there were fourteen people in Teresa's house. There was music, a good deal of food, a baby who was being passed around like something everybody was waiting for a turn to read, and a dog who had discovered that I was an exceptionally good person to sit beside if you were interested in obtaining pieces of cheese.

At about ten, Anthony got out a guitar.

It was an acoustic guitar with one of the tuning pegs missing its little plastic handle. I asked if I could play it.

"Sure."

I played the four chords I'd been playing for months.

He sat down beside me.

"What's that?"

"Something I've been working on."

"Can you show me?"

I showed him the first two chords. He had a little difficulty with the second. We found another way to play it. Then he got up, went into his room, and came back with a smaller guitar.

"Do it again," he said.

We played for a while. His little sister came in and asked if we knew a particular song. Neither of us did. She sang some of it for us. Anthony figured out two of the chords, and we got her to sing it again.

At some point Teresa appeared in the doorway.

She stood there listening to us.

"What?" Anthony said.

"Nothing."

She went back into the kitchen.

A few minutes before midnight Nadia and I went outside. Some people in the neighborhood had begun setting off fireworks. The sky over the roofs lit up in little patches, then went dark again.

"What are you going to do next year?" she asked.

"I don't know."

"Really?"

"Well, I've got a few ideas."

"Of course."

We watched the fireworks.

"I'd like to build something," I said.

"What?"

"Some kind of research group. Something where I could work on the things I want to work on, with people who want to work on them."

"Isn't that what you've got now?"

"Sort of. But I don't know if the place where I'm doing it actually wants to do those things."

"Would you have to quit?"

"Probably. Eventually."

She took my hand.

"Well, don't quit before you've got a plan."

"That's very sound advice."

"I know. I give it to people all the time. They don't take it."

We heard a lot of shouting from inside the house. It was midnight, or sufficiently close to midnight for the people in the house.

I kissed Nadia.

"Happy New Year," I said.

"Happy New Year."

We went inside.

* * *

On the second of January, while Nadia was helping her mother move a large number of plants from one part of the yard to another, I received an email from Gabriel Lemaire.

He wanted to know whether I could come to Brussels in February.

There was a private meeting being organized among researchers and people involved in drafting a new framework for the use of automated systems in public administration. He had attached a short document describing some of the proposed provisions.

I read the document in Teresa's kitchen.

One provision concerned the use of individualized forecasts as evidence of a person's likely decision. The language was careful. It made a distinction between evidence of likely assent and actual assent. I was pleased to see the distinction.

Then I came to a paragraph about exceptional circumstances.

In those circumstances, where it was deemed impractical to obtain an individual's response, sufficiently reliable evidence of anticipated assent could be considered in determining whether an administrative decision was consistent with that individual's preferences.

I read the paragraph again.

There was a reference to a report.

I opened the report. The authors included several people I hadn't heard of and one person I had.

Elliot Finch.

I searched for his name in the document.

There were two quotations attributed to him. One came from a published paper I'd read. The other was attributed to a meeting of an advisory committee.

I recognized it.

It was the recommendation he'd shown me in Cambridge.

I sat there for a while.

Anthony came in carrying a piece of toast.

"What's wrong?"

"Nothing with you," I said.

"Okay."

He took another bite of toast.

"Did you want to play guitar again?"

I closed the laptop.

"Yeah," I said. "I'd love to."

We went into the living room.

I played with him for nearly an hour. Then I went outside and helped Nadia and her mother with the plants.

It was late afternoon by the time I went back to the email. I sent Gabriel a reply asking him to put me in touch with the people responsible for the report.

I also wrote to Elliot.

Then I opened a blank document and wrote down a name for the thing I had been telling Nadia about the previous night.

I didn't particularly like the name, so I wrote down another one.

That was worse.

I saved the document anyway.
