## Thirty — The Island of Other People

In February I went to Scotland.

Nadia was coming over in March, when she could get away from the library for ten days. I had arranged to spend seven weeks in Edinburgh, with the understanding that I would continue to be available to the institute during a certain portion of each day. Fatima had specified the hours during which the portion occurred.

Simon drove me to the airport.

"You're going to have a nice time," he said.

"Yeah."

"You're going to meet some people who are going to want you to do things."

"Probably."

"And you're going to tell them you'll do the things."

"Some of them."

He pulled up in front of the terminal.

"All right. Think of me."

"I will."

"Not affectionately. When somebody tells you how much something costs."

I got out of the car.

He got out, too, and gave me a hug. It was a fairly substantial hug. I hadn't been away from him for seven weeks in a long time.

"Go and be a professor," he said.

* * *

The flat in Edinburgh had a window overlooking a garden, and in the garden there was a statue of a person whose identity had apparently been of considerable importance to somebody. I tried to establish who it was. The woman who lived in the flat below mine informed me that it had been there when she moved in.

"How long ago was that?"

"Thirty-one years."

"Have you ever wondered who it is?"

"Not really."

I decided that I would spend the next seven weeks regarding the statue as a statue of an entirely ordinary man. I found that I rather liked it as a statue of an entirely ordinary man.

On my first morning at the university, Asha took me to a room in which half a dozen people were discussing the design of a study.

There was a place for me at the table. Nobody explained why there was a place for me at the table. I sat down and joined the discussion.

It took me several days to appreciate how much I enjoyed this.

I had been so pleased to have an institution of my own that I had begun to forget some of the pleasures of not having an institution of my own. In the evenings I went to talks at which there was no need for me to say anything. I borrowed books. I had lunch with a graduate student who was trying to work out whether the desire to have a particular experience could be satisfied by an experience of which one retained no memory.

I thought about her question all the way home.

She had asked me whether I would like to have an extraordinarily good afternoon if, at the end of it, I would forget every detail of the afternoon.

I had said yes.

Then she'd asked me whether I would like to have the afternoon instead of the afternoon I was currently having.

I hadn't known.

* * *

The arrangements Malcolm had mentioned in his letter concerned a group of people on an island off the west coast of Scotland.

There were seven of them. One was an oceanographer, one was a retired engineer, two of them were involved in managing a small collection of scientific instruments, and the others had various reasons for spending a considerable amount of time on a boat.

One of them, a man named Ian, had been a friend of Malcolm's for years.

I called him.

"Malcolm says you're interested in mistakes," Ian said.

"Among other things."

"Well, we've got quite a few."

He invited me to come and see them.

I had originally thought I would go by myself, but Asha said she'd been wanting to go out there for some time. We got the train west on Friday afternoon.

By the time we arrived, it was raining.

Ian met us beside the harbor. He was a tall man with a large beard. He was wearing an orange jacket and had brought two more orange jackets.

"Have either of you been on a boat before?"

"Yes," Asha said.

"I've been on a boat," I said.

"What kind?"

"A boat that went to the other side of a lake."

"Good."

He handed me a jacket.

The water was a dark gray. There was a narrow channel between us and the island, and in the distance I could see the faint outline of a hill.

"Are we going out in this?" I asked.

"Not far."

I looked at Asha.

She was smiling.

* * *

There is a kind of cold you encounter on a boat that seems to involve considerably more parts of you than the ordinary kind of cold. I was encountering it when Ian asked me whether I could see a little white object on the surface of the water.

I said I could.

"What's it doing?"

"Going up and down."

"Good."

He explained that it was one of their instruments. It was collecting information about the movement of the water. There were several others in the area, though most were too far away for us to see.

He wanted to know whether the information could be used to determine what was happening in places where there were no instruments.

"To some extent," I said.

"Yeah. That's what the people who sell the software say."

He told me they'd been trying out a number of different forecasting systems. They had a reasonably good idea of how to compare some of the results, but there were difficulties concerning the fact that, in certain circumstances, they could not observe the thing the forecast concerned.

"Well, we could move one of those things," I said.

"Yes."

"But then you'd no longer be observing the place you were observing before."

"Exactly."

Asha began asking him questions.

I stopped talking. I was attempting to establish whether I could find a way of standing that would enable me to remain standing when the boat did something it hadn't given me any indication it was going to do.

We reached the island shortly afterward.

I was extremely pleased with the island.

* * *

They had a little building in which they stored equipment, and another in which they stayed when they were working out there. There was a stove. Ian made us tea.

We spent the afternoon going through some of their records.

What interested me was that they could decide where to collect their next observations. They had a certain amount of time on the water, a certain number of instruments, and several ways of using them. One of their questions was whether the different forecasting systems could help them make those decisions.

Ian wanted to know which system made the best predictions.

Asha asked him whether he'd ever considered finding out which system was best at telling him what he ought to go and look at next.

"No," he said.

We began discussing how we might compare the two things.

I thought of Beatrice's old reconstruction problem. We had been interested in what could be established about an object on the basis of incomplete information. Here was a situation in which we could select some of the information that would become available. There were entirely different things we could do with the same collection of observations.

I began drawing a diagram.

Ian looked at it.

"What's that?"

"An idea."

"Is it a good idea?"

"I don't know yet."

"All right. We'll give you some more tea."

By evening we had a modest plan. There would be no autonomous operation of the boat or interference with the equipment. We would use the existing records and a small simulation to investigate how different strategies for selecting observations affected what could be learned. If the results were promising, Ian's group would consider a limited trial during its ordinary work.

I wanted June and Peter to take a look at it.

We ate dinner together.

There were twelve of us in the room, including several people who lived on the island and had no particular interest in scientific instruments. One of them was a woman named Morag who had been a music teacher in Glasgow. She asked me what I played.

"Guitar."

"Have you brought one?"

"No."

"Well, there's one over there."

It was leaning against the wall behind a chair. The strings were rather old. I tuned it as well as I could.

Morag got out a fiddle.

We played a few things I knew and a number of things I didn't know. She was particularly good at playing things that I didn't know in a manner that enabled me to join in.

At some point Ian went outside and came back in with another man.

"You'll want to hear this," he told him.

I didn't know whether he was referring to Morag or me. I hoped he was referring to Morag.

We played until quite late.

When I went outside afterward, the rain had stopped. There was a little light across the water, and there were stars above it.

I had gone to Scotland because somebody had invited me to a university.

The island had not been included in the invitation.

* * *

On Monday morning I received a call from Anika.

The proposed acquisition had moved further than she'd expected. She had been sent a description of the commercial organization's plans for the discovery program.

There was a provision for making some of the research systems available to paying customers. There was another for using the accumulated research histories to improve the service.

"Would that include Peregrine's?" I asked.

"Potentially. The materials are subject to several separate agreements. We've been going through those agreements."

"Okay."

"I've asked them to make an exception for the projects we're currently collaborating on."

She paused.

"They've asked whether you'd be willing to meet with them."

I told her I would.

"There's another thing," she said.

The team had been preparing an account of the possible consequences of the acquisition. As part of that process, they had asked Peregrine to identify things that might be lost if the existing research arrangements were changed.

It had supplied a list. There were mathematical projects, collaborators and collections of material whose availability under the new arrangements was uncertain.

Toward the end of the list it had identified a problem that none of the researchers had considered.

Anika sent me the relevant passage.

I read it.

Peregrine had identified a way in which its own research history could be used to construct a system that was much better at obtaining particular responses from the people with whom it had worked.

I read the passage again.

"Is that technically possible?" I asked.

"Yes. To some extent."

"Has anybody proposed doing it?"

"Not as far as I know."

"Okay."

"But there's a rather interesting question about the way the system arrived at the concern."

"What?"

"Well, it didn't identify it as something it might lose."

I looked at the passage.

Peregrine had identified it as something that might be lost by its collaborators.

I sat down at the little table by the window.

There was the statue in the garden.

For a long time I had been interested in the things it might be possible to establish about a person on the basis of what that person did.

I now had a question about the things it might be possible to do in light of what had been established about another person.

I wrote to Beatrice and June.

Then I wrote to Vincent.

I told him I would be in Switzerland the following month, and that I would like to buy him a drink.

After all, I still owed him one.
