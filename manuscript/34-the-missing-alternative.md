## Thirty-four — The Missing Alternative

The first time I rode my bicycle all the way around White Rock Lake, I passed Simon twice.

He was walking. The first time I passed him, he waved. The second time he looked at his watch.

"What?" I shouted.

"Nothing!"

When I got home he had sent me a message.

*At this rate you could get to Houston by August.*

I took a shower and went to the institute.

Peter was there with a young woman I hadn't met. Her name was Celia Grant. She was a graduate student at a university in Austin and had applied for the fellowship we had finally managed to advertise. She was visiting for the day.

She had been working on ways of comparing recommendations made by different kinds of automated systems.

"What's the application?" I asked.

"Mostly educational."

"Course selection?"

"Among other things."

She told me about a system that recommended books to people who were attempting to learn something about a subject. She had been interested in establishing whether the books recommended by the system were better than the books people would otherwise have read.

"What do you mean by better?" I asked.

She smiled.

"Peter asked me that."

"What'd you tell him?"

"That I'd been hoping he wouldn't."

We got coffee.

She showed us some results from an experiment involving a collection of short articles about unfamiliar subjects. People had been able to ask for recommendations concerning what to read next. In one condition the recommendations were based on an explicit description of the person's interests. In another they were based on what the person had previously chosen to read.

Both methods worked fairly well, in the sense that people tended to like the material they were subsequently given.

"What's bothering you?" Peter asked.

"Well, we keep finding out what people want to read by giving them things to read."

"Yeah."

"And when we do that, there are things they don't get a chance to read."

She opened another document.

There was a list of articles on it. Some of them had never been recommended to anyone in the second condition.

"Why not?" I asked.

"We don't know. The people who might have been interested in them didn't read enough of the relevant stuff to be identified as people who might have been interested in them."

I looked down the list.

There was an article about the production of pigments in medieval manuscripts. I wanted to read it.

"Did anybody see this one?" I asked.

"No."

"Can I have a copy?"

"Sure."

She sent it to me.

It was a very good article.

* * *

We had decided to undertake a small project in which we would compare systems that made different kinds of recommendations. We had no interest in demonstrating that one of the systems was capable of persuading people to do something they didn't want to do. We wanted to see whether there were measurable differences in the opportunities people had to discover things that interested them.

Celia had suggested a number of ways of doing it.

We used a collection of material concerning activities, subjects and places. There were photographs, short texts and descriptions of things people could try. The volunteers would each spend some time exploring the material. We would make it possible for them to search the collection themselves, and we would give them different kinds of assistance in doing so. We would ask what they'd found interesting, what they hadn't found interesting and what they wished they'd been shown.

It was going to be a little experiment. Nobody's education or employment depended on it. We were going to pay the volunteers for their time.

The university team in Edinburgh helped us with the design and ethics review.

While we were preparing the materials, I asked June whether it would be possible to make a version of the interface in which a person could see the grounds on which something had been recommended.

"Yes," she said.

"And could we make it possible for the person to say they weren't interested in being given certain sorts of recommendations?"

"Yes."

"And could we have some provision for making suggestions that were unrelated to anything we knew about the person?"

"Sure."

"Great."

She looked at me.

"Which one do you want me to do first?"

I sat down.

We had made several prototypes by the end of April.

One afternoon we tried them out ourselves.

I was given the version that was intended to make it easier for people to discover new interests. I was extraordinarily pleased with it. It recommended a description of a little musical instrument I'd never heard of. There was something about maps. There was a piece about the history of an architectural technique.

Then Peter came over to my desk.

"How's it going?"

"Great."

"All right. Give me a second."

He changed something in the configuration.

The next collection of suggestions was considerably less appealing.

"What'd you do?"

"I turned off your additions."

"What additions?"

"The stuff you gave June last week."

I'd sent June a list of examples of the kinds of things I'd hoped the system would enable people to discover. She'd included them in the initial collection of material from which some of the suggestions were generated.

"Well, those were just examples," I said.

"Yeah."

"I didn't mean it to recommend those to me."

"I know."

He opened a spreadsheet.

"Here are the people who put together the material. Here's a description of the categories we told the system to use. And here are some of the suggestions we've been getting when we use the different configurations."

I looked at them.

There was a lot of mathematics. There were a number of things concerning architecture. There were instruments, maps, interesting old buildings, photographs of mountains and descriptions of places you might go on a bicycle.

It was a fairly good account of the interests of the people who worked at Open Question.

Celia came over.

"What happened?" she asked.

Peter showed her.

She started laughing.

"Oh, that's wonderful."

"Why?" I asked.

"Because that's exactly what I did with the books."

I sat back.

We had been trying to build a system that would enable a person to discover things we hadn't anticipated the person would want to discover.

We had made a collection of things we thought it would be nice for people to discover.

There was a difficulty here that couldn't be resolved simply by adding more things to the collection.

I went to the kitchen and got another cup of coffee.

Then I went back and asked Celia how she'd dealt with the difficulty in her own work.

* * *

There was a little park near the institute. I went there at lunchtime.

A man was sitting at a picnic table with a large plastic box containing several hundred small pieces of something. He was taking the pieces out of the box and examining them one at a time.

I walked past him.

Then I walked back.

"Excuse me," I said. "What are those?"

He looked up.

"Watch parts."

"Oh."

He showed me one of them. It was a little wheel with teeth along the edge. I could barely see the teeth.

He explained to me how the wheel was used. Then he told me about a problem he'd had with a watch that had been made in the nineteen-fifties.

I asked a number of questions.

When I left, I knew considerably more about the repair of watches than I had known when I sat down. I didn't know whether I wanted to learn anything else about it.

I didn't think the encounter had been a waste of my time.

Back at the institute I told Celia about the man.

"Would you have clicked on a thing about watch repair?" she asked.

"Probably not."

"Would you click on one now?"

"Possibly."

"Okay. Well, what changed?"

I thought about it.

The man had been interested in the watch. I had been interested in the man's interest in the watch. It hadn't been possible to tell me about that interest without his telling me about the watch.

And the conversation had changed what I could subsequently be interested in.

"I don't know how to put that in a collection of articles," I said.

"No," she said. "Neither do I."

We went back to the table.

* * *

That evening I was looking at the documents concerning the acquisition of the Heller Centre's software when I received a message from Anika.

The negotiations were nearly complete. The company had agreed that the research histories of the existing collaborations wouldn't be transferred for commercial use without additional permission. It had not agreed to make the relevant software available independently on the terms we had proposed.

There were several possible arrangements under which our existing collaboration might be extended. The one the company appeared to prefer was that Peregrine's work continue as part of the larger research program Vincent had offered to fund.

I called Beatrice.

She had been expecting the news.

"Well," she said. "We have a few weeks."

"Yeah."

"And we've got the record of the mathematics."

"Yeah."

She was quiet.

"Could we ask it to propose something?"

"What do you mean?"

"Well, we're trying to work out how to continue its investigations under rather different circumstances. It might have some ideas about that."

I thought this was a good suggestion.

We arranged a final set of sessions under the existing research agreement. We described the restrictions we anticipated, the resources that might remain available and the questions we were hoping to pursue. We asked Peregrine to identify parts of its work that could be continued using ordinary research tools, independently of the software whose ownership was about to change.

It produced several proposals.

Most were variations on things we'd already considered. One involved the creation of a collection of mathematical problems on which a number of different researchers, human and artificial, might work independently.

Another involved our work on recommendations.

We hadn't given Peregrine any personal information about the volunteers. We hadn't given it access to the experimental systems. It had access to the public description of the study and to some of the questions we'd been discussing.

The proposal was very short.

It suggested that, rather than attempting to discover which recommendations would produce the most desirable set of interests in a person, we might investigate which arrangements would make it possible for the person to encounter things whose desirability could not have been determined in advance.

At the end of the proposal it made a distinction.

There was a question about what a person would choose among the opportunities they had.

There was another question about which opportunities the person ought to have.

It suggested that there might be no complete account of a person's current preferences that would settle the second question.

I read the last sentence several times.

Then I went to find Beatrice.

* * *

We spent the following morning working on a little mathematical example.

There were two environments in which an agent could act. The environments were identical with respect to every choice the agent was currently able to make. In one of them a certain action would make a new kind of activity available. In the other it wouldn't.

We assumed that we had a complete account of the agent's preferences over its current alternatives. We asked what this would enable us to establish about which of the environments the agent would prefer to inhabit.

"Nothing, in general," Beatrice said.

"Right."

"Unless you make some more assumptions."

"Right."

She looked at me.

"This isn't a particularly difficult result."

"No."

"What's exciting you?"

I got out my notebook.

I had written a paper years earlier about the impossibility of certain complete representations. I'd been thinking for most of the intervening years about what it meant for a representation to be complete.

It seemed to me that there was now a rather different question to ask.

What would it mean to have represented everything about somebody that was relevant to a particular decision, if the decision could determine which things would subsequently become relevant to that person?

I began explaining it to Beatrice.

She stopped me about halfway through.

"Hang on. Draw the other environment."

I drew it.

"No. The one where the new thing isn't available."

I drew that one.

She sat looking at the drawings.

"Oh," she said.

Then she took the pen from me.

By the time we stopped for lunch, we had a new problem.

For once I was glad we didn't know the answer.

When I got home that evening, Nadia was out in the yard with the woman who lived next door. They had been trying to establish who owned the rooster.

The woman had a small dog and had lived on the street for nineteen years. She told me her name was Cynthia.

"Are you the one that plays the guitar?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Oh, good. My brother plays the drums."

I looked at Nadia.

She smiled.

"Well," I said. "We should probably get together sometime."

Cynthia said she'd tell him.

Then she told us that the rooster belonged to a man two houses down, whose name was apparently also Adrian.

I had never met him.

I thought I might go and introduce myself.
