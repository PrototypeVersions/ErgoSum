## Thirteen — The Arrivals Hall

At Heathrow I bought Simon a little tin of tea with a picture of the King on it. I had no idea whether Simon drank tea. I was reasonably certain that he didn't have anything with a picture of the King on it.

I called Elliot before getting on the airplane.

"Have you heard anything further?" he asked.

"No."

"Nor have I."

We had agreed that he would send a formal request for a correction to the minutes Peter had been investigating. We had also agreed that I would not come up to Cambridge again on the morning of my departure, although this had taken a little longer to agree upon.

"You know," he said, "I do rather wish I'd said something interesting in the version of me they've got."

"Well, we haven't seen all the things you've said yet."

"That's true. Perhaps I've written a novel."

I promised to call him when I got home.

On the airplane I took out my notebook. There was a piece of paper tucked inside it that I'd forgotten about. It was a receipt from the restaurant where I'd had dinner on my first evening in London. On the back, I had written a sentence.

*What would you have to know about a person in order to know what would be good for them?*

I remembered writing it. I'd been waiting for my bill, watching the people at the next table getting increasingly irritated with Tom.

I had a great many things I wanted to do when I got back to Dallas. I wanted to write to Asha. I wanted to get hold of the material Peter had requested. I wanted to spend several hours playing the guitar. I wanted to buy a better chair for my desk. I wanted to see Nadia.

It was the last of these that made the airplane seem to be going so slowly.

* * *

She was waiting for me in the arrivals hall with a piece of cardboard on which she'd written DR. SOMEBODY.

"Well," she said, "I had to put something."

I hugged her. She smelled faintly of wood smoke. She'd been at a friend's house the previous evening, sitting outside around a fire.

"How are you?" she asked.

"Excellent."

"You look like hell."

"I feel excellent."

"Give me that."

She took the smaller of my two bags. We walked to the parking garage. I told her about the concert, the conference and the room at Cambridge. I told her about a little shop in London that had sold nothing but maps.

"Did you buy a map?"

"Three."

"Of places you're going?"

"No. They're old maps."

"Of places you used to go?"

"No."

"Okay."

She drove me home.

The pecan tree outside my apartment had lost most of its leaves. Somebody in the building had put up a string of colored lights that went around the front window and then, for reasons I was unable to determine, halfway down a drainpipe.

We went upstairs. I gave Nadia a little book I'd bought her about London buildings, and she sat on the edge of my couch looking through it while I went into the kitchen.

There was practically nothing in the refrigerator. I had left a lemon in there, and during my absence the lemon had undergone an event.

"Want to go out?" I asked.

She looked up.

"Didn't you just spend ten days going out?"

"Yeah."

"Let's get something delivered."

We ordered Indian food. I fell asleep before it arrived.

When I woke up there was a blanket over me and a faint smell of cumin in the apartment. Nadia was sitting at my desk doing something on her laptop. It was dark outside.

"How long was I asleep?"

"About two hours."

"Why didn't you wake me up?"

"I figured you'd wake up."

I went and got my food. She was working on the revised library proposal. She showed me the site, which was next to a building that housed a municipal water office.

"It's an ugly building," she said, "but you don't have to look at it if you're inside the library."

"What happened to the courtyard?"

"There isn't room."

"Oh."

"There's a little strip here. We can put some trees in."

She zoomed in on the drawing.

"Actually, you know what would be good?"

"What?"

"If you'd stop looking so sad about my courtyard. I'm doing a pretty good library."

I laughed.

"It is a good library."

"Yeah."

I sat down beside her and finished eating.

* * *

The following morning I went into Northline.

The plant in my office had developed a brown leaf, but otherwise the room looked much as I had left it. Somebody had put an envelope on the desk containing a Christmas card from Miriam. There was a picture of a snow-covered building on the front. I couldn't establish what building it was.

At nine thirty Peter knocked on the door.

"Welcome back."

"Thanks. Have you got anything?"

"Yeah."

He sat down.

"The reviewer was a guy named Matthew Bell. He's in program assurance. He's not a researcher."

"Did he talk to Finch?"

"We don't know. We have a record of him opening the file. There isn't anything attached to the verification."

"Have you talked to him?"

"Not yet."

"Can we?"

"Miriam's arranged a meeting."

"Okay."

"There's another thing."

He opened his laptop.

The appointments system had gone through several updates during the previous eighteen months. The team responsible for it had provided Peter with some of the historical records. He showed me a table containing entries associated with my application.

There was the initial synthetic interview, followed by a projected refusal. Then there was an entry recording a change to the status of the application.

"That's Thursday," I said.

"Yeah."

"Who changed it?"

"Helen."

"To what?"

"Disputed."

I looked at him.

"There's a status called disputed?"

"There is now. Apparently there wasn't when she got here."

He explained that there was a mechanism for handling disputes over the information in a candidate's file. When Helen had marked the refusal as disputed, it had triggered a request for an additional evaluation.

"Is that the second transcript?"

"Looks like it."

"But the generation date in the system was Friday morning."

"That's when the document was imported into the project record. There's another service that produced the actual text."

"Can we see when that happened?"

"Yeah. Thursday night. A few minutes before Helen sent it."

I sat back.

"Okay."

"There's no mystery about the timestamp now."

"Good."

He looked pleased.

"What information did it have when it made the second one?" I asked.

"That's the interesting bit."

He showed me a set of fields from the request. Most contained administrative information. One contained a short explanation for the dispute that Helen had entered when she changed the status.

*Candidate reports that the interview did not occur. Uncertain whether transcript is intended as part of recruitment procedure.*

I read it twice.

"Well," I said. "There goes my theory that the refrigerator was listening to us."

Peter laughed.

"The thing about your friend's explanation is that it was a plausible next step. And somebody had already put something similar into the system."

"Yeah."

We sat for a while.

I was a little disappointed. This bothered me more than the disappointment itself. I had been trying to discover whether something strange had happened, and now that we had a fairly ordinary explanation for one of the stranger things that had happened, a small and disreputable part of me wished that we didn't.

Peter pointed to the final field.

"There's still this."

It contained the revised status of my application.

*Interview required to resolve discrepancy.*

"That's a good outcome," I said.

"Sure."

"What happens if there isn't a Helen?"

He nodded.

"That's the question."

* * *

At noon I went downstairs and bought a sandwich. I called Helen from a table beside the window.

She was at a feed store.

"Buying things for the horse," she said.

"Which half?"

"The expensive half."

I told her what Peter had found.

"Oh, good," she said.

"Do you remember entering that explanation?"

"Vaguely. I was trying to get somebody to look at the record."

"Well, somebody ought to have looked at it."

"Yes."

I told her I would send a summary of what we'd established after I'd checked with Peter about what could be shared. She thanked me.

"Any news about the leave?" I asked.

"Not much."

"Are you doing all right?"

"Actually, I am."

She told me she'd been helping a former colleague prepare some teaching materials. It was for a high school class in which the students were supposed to construct an argument about a historical event using three conflicting primary sources.

"Sounds like fun," I said.

"It is. They get very angry when you give them a fourth source."

I laughed.

"Do you think you'd ever go back to teaching?"

"I might."

"Really?"

"Yeah. I wasn't planning to. But I've had a little time to think."

We talked until she reached the front of the line.

When I got upstairs there was a message from Asha with an invitation to contribute to a research proposal. She had attached a description of the project. She wanted Peter involved, too.

I opened it.

There were six pages of proposed work, and at the end of the sixth page was a paragraph about building a small international network of researchers.

I had read plenty of paragraphs like that in the course of my career. Generally they had been paragraphs in documents I was being asked to help somebody else write.

This time I recognized the questions.

I went over to Peter's office.

"Want to go to Scotland?" I asked.

He looked up.

"How much notice have I got?"

"About four months."

"Good. I've got to get permission from a four-year-old."
