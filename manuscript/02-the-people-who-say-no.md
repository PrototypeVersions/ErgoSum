## Two — The People Who Say No

I arrived downtown early enough to buy a coffee and late enough to regret buying it.

A woman was standing on the sidewalk outside the Northline building. She wore an orange coat and was holding a white cardboard box against her hip. She stopped me just as I was trying to work out which of the building's two doors I was meant to use.

"Do you work in there?"

"No."

"Are you going to?"

"That's an excellent question."

"Would you hold this for a second?"

I took the box. It was heavier than it looked. She rummaged through her purse, came up with a phone, and photographed the entrance. Then she photographed the sign beside it.

"Thanks."

"What's in the box?"

"Birthday cake."

"Oh."

"Documents," she said. "Sorry. I get bored answering the same questions."

She took the box back. There was a small cut on the knuckle of her index finger, and she had covered it with a bandage meant for a child. It had a yellow dinosaur on it.

"Are they expecting you?" she asked.

"I think so."

"That's nice. They never expect me."

She went toward the revolving door. I went with her.

The security guard knew her.

"Morning, Ms. Ortiz."

"Morning, Eddie."

"He's not here."

"You don't know who I'm looking for."

"Well, he's not here either."

"Eddie, someday you're going to get another job and we're going to be friends."

"I'll look forward to it."

She put the box on his desk and handed him an envelope.

"For Mr. Voss. Please give it to him this time."

"I gave him the last one."

"And did he eat it?"

The guard looked at me.

"Can I help you, sir?"

"Adrian Vale."

He checked his computer, gave me a visitor's badge, and pointed toward the elevators.

The woman in the orange coat picked up her box.

"Congratulations," she said to me. "You exist."

She left through the revolving door.

It was eight fifty-three.

* * *

Miriam Sloane was in her fifties, had short brown hair and wore a black suit that looked as though it had been designed by somebody who disliked the entire idea of suits. There were two other people in the conference room, a young man named Peter and Helen Park, who stood when I came in.

"Good to meet you," Helen said.

"I was beginning to wonder if we had."

"Coffee?"

I held up mine.

Miriam looked at the cup.

"There's a better place on the other side of the street."

"Everyone keeps telling me that."

"Sit wherever you like."

There were six chairs. I chose the one nearest the door, then felt foolish for doing it.

Miriam asked me if I had read the transcript.

"Twice."

"What did you think of it?"

"I thought the salary was a nice touch."

She nodded.

"It's accurate."

"The salary?"

"Yes."

"Is the rest of it accurate?"

"Some of it. That's why we'd like to talk to you."

Peter turned his laptop around. On the screen was a photograph of the conference room, taken from near the ceiling. It showed the four of us at the table.

"Are you recording this?" I asked.

"Not that," Peter said. "Look at the picture."

In the picture I was sitting on the opposite side of the table. Helen was wearing a dark green sweater. In reality she was wearing a gray blouse. A glass of water stood in front of me in the picture. In reality I had my coffee.

"Okay," I said.

"We generated this last night."

"Why?"

"To illustrate the output."

"Well, it's a nice illustration of me sitting in a chair I'm not sitting in."

"Exactly."

I looked at Miriam.

"Is this a demonstration of how well you can generate photographs, or a demonstration of how badly you can predict what I'm going to do?"

Helen smiled. Peter did not.

Miriam reached for a folder.

"Imagine you wanted to know how a meeting would go. You could take people with some familiarity with the participants and ask them to make a prediction. Or you could construct a statistical model. Or you could try to build a representation of the situation in sufficient detail that you could have something resembling the meeting."

"A simulation."

"Right."

"That's been around for a while."

"It has."

"Then I'm not sure what I'm doing here."

She slid the folder across the table.

Inside were two sheets of paper.

The first contained the answers to a questionnaire filled out by a volunteer in a research study. The second contained answers generated by a computer. The questions were ordinary: whether the volunteer would prefer to live in a large city or a small town, whether she thought children should have access to social media, whether she would take a guaranteed sum of money or gamble for a larger one.

There were some differences. On a question about whether she would accept a job in another state, the woman had written, *It would depend on where my daughter was going to school.*

The computer's answer was no.

Miriam said, "What would you score that?"

"As a prediction?"

"Yes."

"I'd say you don't know yet."

"Exactly."

I turned the pages over.

"What about it?"

"The client scored it as correct."

"Why?"

"She didn't accept a job in another state."

"Was she offered one?"

"No."

I looked at her.

For the first time since I'd arrived, she seemed to be enjoying herself.

"We have a considerable amount of work of that nature," she said. "And considerable disagreements about how to evaluate it. We'd like you to help."

"Who are your clients?"

"Among others, municipal agencies, insurers, financial institutions."

"What do they use it for?"

"Planning."

"Planning what?"

"Different things."

I looked at Helen. She was writing something in a small notebook. It had occurred to me by then that she was the only person in the room who hadn't touched her coffee.

"Suppose," I said, "your system predicts I wouldn't accept a job, and a company decides not to offer me the job on that basis. Are you telling me the company gets to count that as a correct prediction?"

"Some have," Miriam said.

"That doesn't seem like a particularly hard philosophical problem."

"No," she said. "It doesn't."

"Then why do you need me?"

"Because the easy ones have turned out to be quite hard to persuade people about."

Peter closed his laptop.

Miriam took out another sheet of paper. This one was a contract. The money was the money in the transcript.

I thought about my apartment, and about the university in Oregon, and about how many hours I had spent making things nobody had asked me to make.

"Can I take it home?"

"Of course."

"Is there an expectation that I'll do work for particular clients?"

"There's a provision for that. You're free to refuse an assignment."

"All right."

"And we have a small apartment in London that researchers can use. If that's of interest."

It was of interest.

I asked a few more questions. They gave me reasonable answers to most of them and very long answers to the others.

At some point I noticed that it was nine fifteen.

I had decided, in the car the previous night, that at nine seventeen I would do something entirely gratuitous. Perhaps stand up and ask whether anybody wanted a banana. It had seemed a good idea at the time.

At nine sixteen, Helen put down her notebook.

"Miriam, before we finish, I think we should show him the Garland file."

Peter looked at her.

"No," Miriam said.

"It's going to come up."

"Not today."

I looked at the clock.

"What is the Garland file?"

Helen looked at Miriam. Miriam looked at Peter. Peter suddenly developed a great interest in the hinge of his laptop.

"An objection to some work we did for a housing partnership," Miriam said.

"From whom?"

"An attorney."

"The woman downstairs?"

Nobody answered.

It was nine seventeen.

"Can I see it?" I asked.

Miriam leaned back.

"No."

There was something almost wonderfully ordinary about her saying it. No performance, no smile, no argument about confidentiality. Just no.

I had read contracts before. I had signed a great many things without thinking particularly hard about what would happen afterward. I had once signed a form permitting a university to make use of my photograph in promotional material and subsequently spent two years appearing on a webpage about a subject I knew nothing about.

I put the contract in my bag.

"I'll be in touch."

* * *

The woman in the orange coat was sitting outside the coffee shop across the street. There was no sign of the cardboard box.

I bought another coffee, which turned out to be better than the first, and went outside.

"Ms. Ortiz?"

She looked up.

"You got in."

"I did."

"What are you?"

"A philosopher."

"Oh, God."

"I get that a lot."

She moved her bag off the other chair.

I sat down.

"What's the Garland file?"

She stopped smiling.

"Who told you about that?"

"Nobody, really."

"Did they show you?"

"No."

She looked past me at the Northline building. A city bus went between us and the building, then the building came back.

"There's a woman named Alma Reyes," she said. "She's seventy-one. She rents a little house in Garland. Or she used to. She received an offer from the company that bought the property. Four thousand dollars to move out early. She didn't take it."

"Okay."

"That ought to be the end of a very short story. But it isn't."

She unlocked her phone and opened a photograph of a letter.

A relocation payment was recorded as pending. Beneath the payment, in smaller type, was a line that read:

*Resident agreement anticipated; review waived.*

"Anticipated," I said.

"Right."

"What does that mean?"

"That's what I went to law school to ask."

She told me that the partnership had submitted its relocation plan to a city office. The plan counted Alma among the residents expected to accept an offer. When Alma objected, the company told her that the plan had already been approved. There was no record of her ever accepting anything. There was, however, a record of a system concluding that she almost certainly would.

"Did Northline make the prediction?"

"That's what I'm trying to find out. Their name turns up in the procurement documents."

"Does she still live there?"

"With her sister now. Different neighborhood. She's got two bus transfers to get to the pharmacy she's been going to for twenty years."

We sat without speaking for a minute.

"How did the interview go?" she asked.

"I think I got the job."

"Good."

"You sound surprised."

"No, I mean good. Somebody should be in there asking questions."

She put her phone away.

"What's your first name?"

"Adrian."

"Lena."

We shook hands. I had a sudden and unpleasant awareness of the expensive contract in my bag.

"Did you ever get the chance," I said, "to ask Alma what she would have done if she'd been given a real choice?"

Lena looked at me for a long time.

"She was given a choice, Adrian. She said no."

I thought about what had been written at the bottom of my imaginary interview.

It wasn't that I didn't know what happened to the people who said no.

It was that I was beginning to know.
