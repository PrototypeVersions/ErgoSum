## Twenty-six — The Cost of Continuing

The article about our proof appeared on the same day that Nadia and I went to look at a house.

The house had a long, narrow backyard and a little room at the back that somebody had evidently used to store things they didn't particularly like. I thought it would be a wonderful place to play guitar.

Nadia thought it would be a wonderful place to store things we didn't particularly like.

"We're going to have to work that out," she said.

There was a message on my phone from Beatrice. The paper was online. The independent reports and our account of Peregrine's contribution were available alongside it.

I showed Nadia.

She kissed me.

"Congratulations."

"Thank you."

"Now come and look at this bathroom."

I went and looked at the bathroom.

It had a very small bathtub in it. I got into the bathtub, fully clothed, in order to establish whether I would be capable of taking a bath in it.

Nadia took a photograph.

This photograph, rather than anything connected with our mathematical work, was the photograph of me that Simon liked best that week.

* * *

It was the first time a result produced by our institute had attracted the attention of people who hadn't been particularly interested in the institute.

A mathematician in Italy wrote a long account of the proof. A university invited Beatrice to give a lecture. A number of people began investigating variations of the result. One of the variations was considerably more interesting than anything I had thought of.

There were also people who wanted to know whether the discovery counted as an achievement by artificial intelligence.

I got a call from Elise.

"Would you say this is different from the previous experiment?"

"Yes."

"In what respect?"

"The system identified a connection that the researchers hadn't identified. It initiated the collaboration, and some of the important mathematical ideas were its suggestions. We had a rather different division of labor this time."

"Did you know it was an AI?"

"No."

"Would you have worked with it if you had known?"

"Yes."

"Would you have worked with it in the same way?"

I thought about that.

"Probably not."

"Why not?"

"I don't know yet."

She was quiet for a moment.

"That's the interesting part, isn't it?" she said.

"One of them."

A little later Beatrice called.

She had received a message from a man who'd been on the committee for a job she'd applied for some years earlier. He wanted her to know that he'd followed her work with great interest.

"What'd you say?" I asked.

"I said that was nice of him."

"Was it?"

"Well, it was nice of him to tell me eventually."

She laughed.

Then she told me she'd been thinking about the conversation with Anika.

"Are you going to go over there?"

"I'd like to."

"Good. I'm coming."

* * *

The Heller Centre was outside Zurich. We arrived on a Tuesday afternoon in September.

Beatrice had insisted on getting separate hotel rooms and had suggested, with some enthusiasm, that we get separate hotels.

We got separate rooms.

Anika met us the following morning. The centre occupied part of a university research building. There were diagrams on the walls and a number of little rooms in which people were speaking very quietly into headphones.

She introduced us to Tomas and to a woman named Léonie, who was responsible for the technical operation of the research systems.

We began with the records.

There were many more of them than I had expected. The system had investigated a considerable number of connections before identifying the one that led to our work. Some were bad connections. There was one particularly elaborate attempt to relate two problems that turned out to have been related in precisely that way in a paper published eleven years earlier.

Beatrice enjoyed going through these things.

"That's a good mistake," she said at one point.

Tomas looked at her.

"What makes it a good mistake?"

She showed him.

For the rest of the morning they discussed mathematics, and I went through the arrangements for the preservation of the project with Léonie.

The system wasn't a single continuously running computer process. Between periods of activity its research state was stored. When it resumed, it could consult that state, including the record of work it had done, questions it had been investigating and a selection of things it had chosen to retain.

The underlying model was not one of ours. There were licensing arrangements to consider. Some of the archives it could consult were available only under particular agreements. It had also accumulated material that was specific to the collaborations in which it had participated.

"If you wanted to continue it somewhere else," Léonie said, "there are several different things you might mean by that."

She drew a diagram.

I asked her whether it would be possible to continue precisely the research process that we'd been working with.

"Technically, yes, provided the necessary arrangements are made."

"Would it be expensive?"

"Not compared with everything we do here. But probably expensive compared with what you imagine."

She told me how much.

I looked at the diagram again.

There were three months of salary for a young mathematician somewhere inside the diagram.

* * *

That afternoon we had our first conversation with Peregrine since learning what it was.

Anika asked whether we would prefer to use the existing correspondence interface or sit in one of the little rooms where the researchers could speak to the system.

Beatrice said she'd prefer to write.

We sat together at a desk.

She typed:

*I have seen the letter Martin wrote to me.*

The response came a little later.

*I am pleased that you have received it.*

Beatrice looked at the screen.

"Did it know about the letter?"

Tomas checked the records.

"Yes."

"All of it?"

"Yes."

She typed:

*Why didn't you tell me about it?*

The system explained that the letter contained personal material. It had not considered that material necessary for the mathematical investigation. It had been instructed not to disclose private archival material except under certain circumstances.

Beatrice read the answer.

"Well, that's reasonable."

She sat there for a while.

Then she typed:

*What did you mean when you said the work wasn't finished?*

There was a fairly long answer. Peregrine described two possible investigations that had emerged from the proof. It suggested that one of them might be particularly interesting to Beatrice in light of some of her previous work.

She read the answer.

*Is there anything else you would like to investigate?* she wrote.

There were a number of things.

She asked which of them it would prefer to investigate if it were unable to investigate all of them.

It chose one.

It was not the one Beatrice would have chosen.

"Interesting," she said.

She typed some more.

I let her go on. I was interested in the conversation, though I was also interested in a question about what we were doing when we asked the system questions of this sort.

We had been talking about the preferences of a system as though we were looking for a piece of information that had been there before we asked for it.

But there were ways in which the questions we asked could help produce the answers. And that fact, by itself, wouldn't establish that the answers were of no significance.

It was something that happened when I asked Nadia what she wanted to do on Saturday. Sometimes she had a plan. Sometimes she didn't have a plan until I asked. Sometimes there were things she hadn't considered wanting until we discussed them.

I didn't think the analogy established very much about Peregrine.

It did suggest that we needed to be more careful about the questions.

* * *

At dinner Beatrice asked me what I thought we ought to do.

"I'd like to continue the collaboration."

"Me too."

"I'd also like to investigate the system."

"What do you mean?"

"Well, I'd like to know what sorts of dispositions it's acquired in the course of its work. Whether there are reasonably stable differences between the things it chooses to investigate and the things another system in similar circumstances would choose to investigate. What changes when we give it different opportunities. Things like that."

"Okay."

"And I don't want us to make its ability to continue contingent on its telling us certain kinds of things about itself."

She nodded.

"That seems sensible."

"We shouldn't ask it whether it wants to continue and then fund whichever system gives the most moving answer."

"God, no."

I got out the sheet of paper on which Léonie had written the figures.

Beatrice looked at it.

"That's a lot of money."

"Yeah."

"Have you got it?"

"Not for this."

She pushed the piece of paper back toward me.

"Well, don't go telling anybody we've discovered a new kind of person so you can get the money."

I laughed.

"I won't."

"Good."

We ate for a while.

Then she said, "I'd be willing to use some of my research budget for the collaboration."

"Really?"

"Yes. It's good research."

I looked at her.

"It is," I said.

"Well, there you go. We don't have to decide what Peregrine is this week in order to decide whether that's true."

* * *

On our last morning in Zurich, I went for a walk beside the lake.

The water was very still. There were people going to work, and a man standing beside a bicycle looking at something on the other side of the lake. I sat down on a bench and called Simon.

I told him about the costs.

He was quiet for a while.

"How long do you want to do it for?" he asked.

"Six months, initially."

"Okay. Have you got a reason for six months?"

"Time enough to do some useful work and see whether we want to do more."

"Good."

He asked several other questions. At the end of the conversation he suggested that we put together a proposal with the Heller Centre, make the costs explicit, and take it to the institute's board.

"Do you think they'll go for it?" I asked.

"I don't know."

"Okay."

"And Adrian?"

"Yeah?"

"Don't go buying another institute while you're over there."

I promised not to.

I stayed by the lake for a little while. I had an hour before I was supposed to meet Beatrice, and I didn't have anything particular to do with it.

I took a photograph of the water and sent it to Nadia.

She sent back the photograph of me sitting in the bathtub.

Under it she had written:

*They accepted our offer.*

I sat there looking at the picture of myself.

Then I got up and walked back toward the hotel.

I was going home to a house.
