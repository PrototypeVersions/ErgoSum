## Forty-eight — The Road to the Sea

Nadia had been in England for two days when we received the news that the grant application from Oslo had been unsuccessful.

I read the message on a train.

It was a very polite message. The reviewers thought that the proposed work was interesting, but there were concerns about the relationship between the proposed experiments and the conclusions we hoped to be able to draw from them. Astrid and I had been working on the application for most of the previous year.

I sent her a note.

She sent me one back almost immediately.

*Well, that was expensive in terms of evenings.*

I laughed.

Nadia asked me what had happened.

I told her.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"Yeah."

She took my hand.

We looked out of the window for a little while. There were sheep in a field, and a house with a remarkable number of chimneys.

"Do you think you'll try again?" she asked.

"Probably."

"Okay."

We went back to our respective books.

About twenty minutes later I realized that I was enjoying the train ride. It was a curious thing to realize. For many years, on the days on which I had received messages of that kind, I had regarded the possibility of enjoying the remaining portion of the day as a fairly remote one.

I read a little more of my book.

* * *

We had decided to go to Spain.

There was a building in Granada that Nadia had been interested in since she was seventeen. There were several other buildings that she had been interested in since various other ages. I had never been to Spain, and the principal thing I wanted to do there was be in Spain.

For the first week we went to the places she'd wanted to go to.

At the Alhambra she became interested in a particular transition between two rooms. We went through the rooms several times. Eventually she asked me whether I could see why the transition was interesting.

I said that I could.

"Why?" she asked.

"Well, it's very beautiful."

She laughed.

"You're not going to make much of an architect."

"No."

She took my arm and brought me back to the first room. She showed me where the light came in, and how the things one was able to see changed as one moved.

I went through the rooms again.

This time I could see some of what she had been talking about.

It wasn't that I had previously thought they were less beautiful. It was that there were now more things in the rooms.

In the afternoon we sat outside a cafe. Nadia ordered something for me that I wouldn't have known how to order. I liked it.

A man at the next table was arguing with somebody on his telephone. I couldn't understand what the argument concerned.

Nadia could.

"What's he saying?" I asked.

"He's trying to find his brother."

"Where's his brother?"

"Well, that's the argument."

We watched the man get up and walk to the end of the street. A few minutes later he returned with another man who looked very much like him.

They sat down together.

Apparently the argument was over.

* * *

We rented a car on Thursday.

The plan was to drive toward the coast, stopping in a town Nadia had read about. I was responsible for the navigation. This turned out to be a position of considerably more responsibility than I had anticipated.

There was a point at which the directions indicated that we ought to turn left. I told Nadia to turn left.

There was a point at which the directions indicated that we ought to make a fairly complicated maneuver involving two roads, only one of which appeared to exist.

I told Nadia to go straight.

"Are you sure?" she asked.

"No."

She went straight.

We came to a village.

There was a church, a little square and a number of white houses. An elderly man was standing beside a fountain.

Nadia parked the car.

We went over to him.

She spoke to him for a few minutes. Then she came back.

"We're not where we're supposed to be."

"Okay."

"But there's somewhere we can get lunch."

We got lunch.

There were three tables outside the restaurant. A woman was sitting at one of them repairing the strap of a sandal. At the other was a young man with a guitar.

I looked at the guitar.

Nadia looked at me.

"Go on," she said.

The young man spoke a little English. He had grown up in the village and was studying in Málaga. His name was Daniel. He'd come home for a few days because his father needed help with something at the house.

I told him I played guitar.

He handed me his.

It was a nylon-string guitar, and it felt quite different from the guitars I usually played. I played a few chords.

Daniel took the guitar back and played something.

There was a little passage at the end of it that I particularly liked. I asked him if he'd play it again.

He did.

Then he showed me how to play it.

I wasn't very good at it.

We spent about twenty minutes working on it, during which Nadia and the woman repairing the sandal had become quite well acquainted.

When we left, Daniel told me there was a place in Málaga where he sometimes played with friends. He wrote the name of the place on a piece of paper.

I put it in my pocket.

* * *

Two days later we found the place.

It was not a particularly impressive place from the outside. Inside, however, there were a number of people who knew Daniel. He was delighted to see us.

He introduced us to his friends.

There was a woman named Lucia who had been playing guitar for nearly twenty years. She'd learned from her father, who'd learned from his uncle. She had strong opinions about the importance of the right hand.

She demonstrated something.

I attempted to reproduce it.

She laughed.

I attempted to reproduce it again.

She took my hand and moved one of my fingers.

Then I got it.

Not very well, but well enough to produce the sound I'd been trying to produce.

We played for a while. At some point somebody began singing. There was a little girl asleep in a chair in the corner of the room. I was trying to play a thing I had learned two days earlier in a village in which we hadn't intended to stop.

When I looked up, Nadia was dancing.

She saw me looking at her.

She smiled.

I kept playing.

* * *

The next morning I woke up with a pain in my right hand.

"What's the matter?" Nadia asked.

"Apparently the right hand is very important."

She laughed.

We went down to the water.

I'd been carrying my telephone around with me for most of the trip, although I hadn't been looking at it very much. There were now some messages on it that I needed to deal with.

I sat on the beach and went through them.

One was from Astrid. She'd been reading the reviewers' comments again. She thought there was something to be said for conducting a much smaller study before attempting to conduct the one we'd proposed.

Another was from Celia. She wanted to know whether I'd be willing to supervise a student for part of the following year.

There was a message from Vincent.

He had left Continuance.

He hadn't decided what he wanted to do next. He'd been spending some time with his brother in France. He asked whether I'd be interested in having a conversation about a problem concerning the use of automated systems in scientific research.

At the bottom of the message he'd written:

*I haven't got anything to sell you.*

I laughed.

I wrote back that I'd be delighted.

Then I opened a message from June.

She'd been contacted by a group of researchers who wanted to use her software in a study concerning the ways that AI systems contributed to the production of scientific results. They'd asked whether it would be possible to make an account of the contributions that could be examined by the people who'd made them.

She wanted to know what I thought.

I wrote that I thought it was a good idea.

Then I added that I thought it would be useful if the people could do something other than examine the account.

I thought they ought to be able to add something to it.

June replied a few minutes later.

*Already on the list.*

I put the telephone down.

* * *

We had one day left.

Nadia asked me what I'd like to do.

I thought for a little while.

"Do you want to go swimming?"

"Yeah."

We went swimming.

Later we got lunch. We walked along the water. We bought a present for Teresa and something for Simon that we didn't think he'd be able to identify without assistance.

In the evening we sat outside a restaurant.

I had brought the notebook in which I'd been writing the lectures. There were some things in it I wanted to discuss with Nadia.

I told her that I'd been thinking about the possibility of writing another book.

"Besides the book you're writing?"

"Yeah."

"About what?"

I looked at the notebook.

"Well, partly about the things that have happened to us."

"All right."

"And partly about some of the things that might have happened."

She took a drink.

"Am I in it?"

"Quite a bit."

"Okay."

She considered this.

"Make me funny."

I laughed.

"You are funny."

"I know. But you tend to make me say something sensible."

"Occasionally you say something sensible."

"Occasionally."

She smiled.

Then she asked me a question about what I thought the book would be for.

I didn't know how to answer it.

There was a man playing a guitar a little way down the street. We couldn't see him, but we could hear him. I thought I recognized something he was playing.

I listened.

It wasn't the thing I'd thought it was.

Nadia took my hand.

I shut the notebook.

There would be plenty of time to work out what the book was for.
