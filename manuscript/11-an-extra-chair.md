## Eleven — An Extra Chair

There were little notebooks at every place at the conference table, each with the Northline logo on the cover. I took two. There had been a time when the prospect of receiving free stationery had been one of my principal reasons for attending academic conferences. I was pleased to discover that my new financial circumstances hadn't ruined this for me.

The meeting was being held in a building near the Strand. It was a lovely room, although there was a large television on one wall that looked as if somebody had put it there during an argument with the architect.

Miriam had arrived the previous evening. She introduced me to a woman named Asha Mehta, who worked on public health statistics, and to a man named Gabriel Lemaire, who was in the process of trying to get a number of European governments to agree on something.

"What sort of thing?" I asked.

"Definitions," he said.

"Oh."

"Yes. It isn't going well."

I liked him immediately.

Peter came in carrying a bag of pastries. There was a little white card in front of each chair with a name on it. He looked for his name, found it, and put the pastries in the middle of the table.

"Any news from Finch?" he asked me.

"I think he'll be here."

"On the screen?"

"In the room."

Peter looked at the screen.

"Well, that'll be good."

There was no chair for Finch. There were two other people who were supposed to be joining us remotely, but their names appeared beside their faces when they came up on the screen. Finch's name appeared beside a small gray circle.

A woman from Northline's London office stood at the front of the room and welcomed everybody. Her name was Louise. She explained that the proceedings would be recorded for internal use and that we'd each receive a copy of the meeting record.

"You'll also have an opportunity to make any necessary corrections," she said.

I raised my hand.

"Will we get to see the record before it's used for anything?"

"Of course."

She said it with the kindness of a person who had been asked whether the building had toilets.

"And if we don't have any corrections?"

"Then we take it that you're happy with it."

"All right."

Peter gave me a pastry.

For the first hour we discussed the kinds of data used in constructing simulated individuals. It was considerably more interesting than it sounds. Asha told us about an experiment in which a conventional questionnaire had consistently failed to establish that people in a particular district needed transportation to medical appointments. It turned out that the question they'd been asking was whether people had access to a car. A great many of the people who answered yes had access to a car in precisely the sense in which I had access to Simon's car: there was somebody they knew who owned one.

"We could have saved ourselves six months," she said, "if we'd asked them who took them to the hospital."

During the break I went over to talk to her. We found that we had both spent several years studying problems that turned out, in the end, to be problems about questions. She invited me to visit her group in Edinburgh.

I said I'd love to.

It was the first of several nice things that happened to me that morning, and for a while I entirely forgot about Elliot Finch.

* * *

At eleven twenty, Louise began the next session.

She introduced a document on the proposed use of simulated interviews in a new evaluation program. The document contained comments from several of the people in the room, and some from people who had been unable to attend.

On the television there was a column of names beside a diagram of the program.

"Professor Finch has recommended proceeding to the second phase subject to the usual qualifications," she said.

Miriam looked up.

"Which qualifications?"

Louise scrolled down.

There was a paragraph on the screen. I read it twice. The qualifications concerned the accuracy of the representations, the supervision of the interview process, and the regular review of the results. There was no mention of obtaining separate consent from the people whose representations would be used.

"Could we hold off on that recommendation for a minute?" I asked.

Louise looked at Miriam, then back at me.

"Do you have a concern?"

"Well, I'm not sure Professor Finch has made it."

"I can bring up the comment."

She clicked on his name. A box opened.

The text was attributed to Elliot Finch. Above it was a little green mark.

"What does the green mark mean?" I asked.

"That the contribution has been incorporated."

"Did he send it to you?"

She looked at the screen.

"I think it came through the participant workspace."

"Could you check?"

There was a knock at the door.

Louise went over and opened it.

Elliot Finch was standing in the hallway with a damp umbrella and a paper bag.

"Sorry I'm late," he said. "I bought some pears."

Miriam stared at him.

"Elliot."

"Hello, Miriam."

He came in and put the pears on the table. Peter got up to find him a chair.

"I thought you were joining us remotely," Louise said.

"Yes, I saw that. How's it been going?"

She glanced at the television.

"We were just discussing your recommendation."

"Splendid. What is it?"

Nobody laughed. It was a pity, because I thought it was rather funny.

Louise read the paragraph aloud.

Elliot listened attentively.

"No," he said when she'd finished. "I wouldn't recommend that."

"Which part?"

"The recommendation."

"All right."

He sat down on the chair Peter had brought.

"Where did it come from?"

Louise looked genuinely distressed.

"I'll have to look into it."

"Please do."

Miriam asked everybody to take an early lunch.

Elliot picked up one of his pears and examined it.

"Would anybody like a pear?"

Asha took one.

* * *

Miriam asked Peter, Elliot, Louise and me to stay behind. Louise opened another part of the conference software on her laptop.

There were several kinds of material that could be associated with a participant. There were documents the participant had uploaded, notes supplied by the organizers, and something called a prepared contribution.

"What is a prepared contribution?" Miriam asked.

"It's a summary of the points they're expected to make," Louise said. "Some of the organizers use it to make sure the material gets covered if someone's unable to attend."

"Prepared by whom?"

"Usually the program team."

She clicked through to another page. In this case the prepared contribution had been generated from a document set that included two of Elliot's papers and an old research proposal.

"Was that my proposal for the Observatory?" he asked.

"Yes, I think so."

"There's a section in that proposal on why we shouldn't do this."

Louise scrolled again.

She found a record of how the prepared contribution had been imported into the meeting documents. The record contained a source identifier, an import date and a review status. It did not contain any evidence that Elliot had seen or approved it.

"Is this how the comment got there?" Miriam asked.

"I think so."

"Why is it green?"

Louise began explaining what she understood the colors to mean.

Miriam interrupted her.

"No, I mean why does it look like something he's said?"

Nobody had a good answer.

It turned out that the recommendations weren't binding. That seemed to me important. It also seemed to me important that the first thing everyone said after we established how Finch's comment had arrived was that the recommendations weren't binding.

Elliot took out his phone and found the document he had shown me in Cambridge.

"Can we establish whether something similar happened with this?"

Louise looked at it.

"I don't have access to that project."

"Who does?"

She told him.

Miriam asked her to send the record of what had happened that morning to everyone in the room. Then she asked Peter to preserve the source material and the import records.

"Don't change anything," she said. "I want to know what we had before we decided it was a problem."

Peter nodded.

I looked at Miriam. She looked furious.

For the first time since I'd known her, I thought she and I were furious about exactly the same thing.

* * *

Elliot and I went to a pub for lunch.

"Well," he said, "I'm very glad I bought the pears."

"Did you have any idea that's what would happen?"

"Not really. I'd been getting some peculiar correspondence about the meeting. I wanted to see what it was like to arrive unexpectedly at something I was expected to attend."

We ordered sandwiches.

"I thought you handled it well," I said.

"Oh, I had a perfectly good little speech prepared. Quite a fiery one. Then I got there and the woman looked so miserable I couldn't give it."

"She seemed to have no idea."

"Yes."

We ate for a while.

"Do you remember," Elliot said, "when I wrote to you that the situation in your paper couldn't arise?"

"Vividly."

"I still think that was correct, you know."

"I know."

"But I rather underestimated the lengths to which people might go to make something that looks like such a situation arise."

He took a drink.

"There's a perfectly respectable research program here. I'd hate for us to lose it because somebody has managed to make the results look much more interesting than they are."

I agreed with him. I thought about Irene and the piano. I wanted to know whether there was a way of giving Irene what she had found helpful without obliging her to live with a representation of herself that she hadn't wanted to exist anymore.

"Do you ever worry," I asked, "that you're going to spend the rest of your life telling people why they can't do things?"

"Constantly."

"What do you do about it?"

"I try to make friends with people who build things."

I told him about Nadia.

He listened with considerable interest.

Then, quite unexpectedly, he asked me if I'd mind telling him about the guitar.

We spent the remainder of lunch discussing the guitar.
