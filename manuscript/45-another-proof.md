## Forty-five — Another Proof

June had arranged to meet me at the library.

She had never been there before. I found her in the room with the table, sitting beside a man who was eating a sandwich of which the library probably wouldn't have approved.

"Hey," she said.

"Hey."

She moved her bag.

I sat down. She turned her laptop around.

On the screen was a diagram of the kind Beatrice had been drawing when we first began working on the reconstruction problem. There were a number of little shapes connected by lines. June clicked on one of the lines.

A sentence appeared.

She clicked on the sentence.

A mathematical argument appeared.

"Okay," I said.

She clicked on a link in the argument, and there was a page from Beatrice's paper.

"That's the old stuff."

"Yeah."

She opened another window.

"This is the new stuff."

There were three versions of a proof on the screen. The first had been produced by P2, using some of the material we'd preserved from the original collaboration. The second had been produced by a group of researchers who had encountered the problem through our public collection. The third was the result of an attempt by Beatrice to establish which parts of the first two arguments were actually necessary.

June went through the differences between them.

I could follow about half of what she was saying. The half I could follow was quite exciting.

The result concerned a class of objects that were much more general than the objects in the original reconstruction problem. Several things that we'd had to establish separately in the original proof could now be obtained as consequences of one underlying result.

"Is that proved?" I asked.

"That version is."

"And the other version?"

"We found a mistake."

"Oh."

She showed me the mistake. Then she showed me the history of the argument in which it occurred.

There were two things that interested me.

One was that the people who'd identified the mistake hadn't been involved in our original research. The other was that the system that had produced the argument containing the mistake had subsequently produced a reasonably good explanation of why it was a mistake.

"Did you give it the answer?" I asked.

"We gave it the objection."

She took a drink of coffee.

"Do you want to see where the interesting bit came from?"

"Yeah."

She showed me.

It came from an argument that didn't work.

* * *

The unsuccessful argument was one that Peregrine had produced in the course of its work with Beatrice. It concerned a difficulty that we'd eventually got around in a different way.

Peregrine had suggested a particular construction. Beatrice had found an example in which the construction failed.

For the purposes of the investigation we'd been conducting at the time, that was the end of the construction.

But the researchers who were working with the more general class of objects had encountered a somewhat different problem. They didn't need the construction to work in all the cases in which we'd needed it to work. They needed to establish something about the cases in which it failed.

Beatrice had gone back through the old argument with them.

They'd found what they needed.

I went through the record again.

"That's pretty remarkable," I said.

"Yeah."

"Does Beatrice know you've done this?"

"Adrian, Beatrice did this."

"No. I mean the thing with the links."

"Oh. Yeah."

She showed me a message from Beatrice.

It contained a number of suggestions concerning what could be done to make the software less irritating to use. At the bottom she'd written that it was the first time she'd been able to show somebody, without spending most of an afternoon on it, how an idea in one of her papers had come from an idea that wasn't in any of her papers.

I laughed.

June closed the laptop.

"There are a couple of universities that want to use it," she said.

"Wonderful."

"And there's a group of people who are trying to put together a public collection of mathematical arguments."

"Okay."

"They've asked whether I'd be interested in working with them."

"Would you?"

"Very much."

She told me about the arrangements. She would be able to continue developing the software openly. She would also have opportunities to work on some questions that hadn't been particularly relevant to our work at Open Question.

It sounded like a wonderful job.

I told her so.

"Yeah," she said.

She looked around the room.

"Can I tell you something?"

"Sure."

"When I came to work for you, I thought you might be a little disappointed by the stuff I wanted to do."

"Why?"

"Well, I'd read some of the things you wanted to do."

I laughed.

She smiled.

"Anyway, thanks for letting me do it."

I didn't know what to say.

I picked up her coffee by mistake.

"That's mine," she said.

"Oh."

I put it down.

Then I thanked her.

* * *

Beatrice was in Argentina.

She had been invited to visit the researchers who'd first written to us about the public collection. She sent me a photograph of a group of people sitting at a table. There were some papers on the table and several plates of food, and there was a dog underneath it.

I called her.

"How's it going?"

"Terrific."

"Have you proved anything?"

"Not today."

"Okay."

"I've eaten an enormous amount of meat."

"Good."

She told me about the people she was working with. One of them was a young mathematician named Inés who had begun working on a version of the reconstruction problem independently of us. Her approach to it was very different from ours, and there were things that Beatrice hadn't known how to do that Inés knew how to do.

"She keeps giving me things to read," Beatrice said.

"That's good."

"Yeah."

I asked her whether she'd seen the new version of the proof.

"Of course."

"Do you think we ought to try to write something about the history of it?"

"Yes."

"I think there's a fairly interesting question about how much of it we ought to attribute to Peregrine."

"Well, let's attribute to it the things it did."

"Yeah."

"And let's not start attributing to it the things we did afterward."

"Agreed."

She told me that one of the people she'd been working with was particularly interested in understanding the original problem. He'd been going through the old correspondence. He'd found several suggestions that none of us had subsequently made use of.

"He wants to try a few of them."

"Good."

"Adrian?"

"Yeah?"

"Do you remember that night when I found the counterexample with nineteen counters?"

"Yeah."

"I was so pleased when I found it."

"I wasn't."

"I know."

She laughed.

"Well, we're getting a lot of counterexamples down here."

"Wonderful."

"I'll send you some."

"Please do."

When we hung up, I looked at the photograph again.

There were six people at the table.

I knew two of them.

I hadn't met the dog.

* * *

Nadia and I went to the workshop the following afternoon.

Curtis had put the table in a room at the back of the building. There were several things on it that I would never have permitted anybody to put on it at the institute, including the engine of a small lawn mower.

He'd also repaired one of its legs.

"How's that?" he asked.

"Very good."

"Thing was about to fall off."

"I know."

He showed me what he'd done.

There was a young woman sitting at one end of the table. She was making something out of a piece of wood. Nadia went over to talk to her.

I stood looking at the engine.

"Do you want to know what the problem is?" Curtis asked.

"Sure."

He explained it to me.

When he'd finished, I understood the problem well enough to be of no assistance to him whatsoever.

He asked if I'd like to try taking a part off.

"Will I ruin it?"

"Probably not."

He handed me a tool.

I took the part off.

Then he showed me how to tell whether it was doing what it was supposed to do.

We spent the next half hour at the table.

It was the first time I'd ever done anything of much practical use on it.

* * *

I was home that evening when I received a message from Inés.

She wanted to know whether the public collection would accept contributions from people who weren't currently affiliated with a research institution.

I told her it would.

She said that she had a friend who'd been working on a problem for several years. He'd finished his degree, obtained a job doing something else, and had continued working on the problem in his spare time. There were some things in the collection that she thought might be of interest to him.

I told her to send him the link.

She did.

About a month later he submitted a short argument.

It didn't prove the thing he wanted it to prove.

Beatrice told him so.

He sent her another argument.

She told him that one was quite interesting.

They were still working on it when I began writing this book.

I've occasionally thought about the man I was when I first received the telephone call from Helen. I'd been hoping somebody would give me a place in which I could do some work.

I'm very glad somebody did.

But I think one of the nicest things we managed to make at Open Question was a place that didn't particularly mind where a person was sitting.
